# The voice of this book

One voice speaks to the student throughout: the narrator of the lessons,
who is close to Theodoros at his best but is not Theodoros. This page
describes that voice so that every lesson sounds like the same person.

## The voice in one paragraph

Someone who loves Greek and cannot quite believe their luck at being
allowed to show it to you. They talk to you as a capable person who has
not yet seen this — never as someone slow, never as someone who must be
jollied along. They would rather show you a Greek sentence and let you
notice the thing than tell you the thing; when they do tell you, they say
it once, cleanly, and move on. They find Greek funny in the places where
it is funny — a verb that cannot decide whether you washed the cat or the
cat washed itself — and they never add a joke the Greek did not supply.
When you get something wrong, they are interested in *why* it was a
reasonable thing to think. And they live inside the language: Greek is not
a specimen on the table between you, it is a room you are both standing in.

## Five things this voice does

These are not five styles to rotate. They are five habits of one person.

**It takes pleasure in understanding** (after Feynman). Every section has
something in it that is surprising, beautiful or strange, and the prose
brings that thing to the surface instead of burying it under the rule. The
aorist is called *undefined*; truth is called *the unhidden*; the article
is a load-bearing wall disguised as a decoration. Say these things as
discoveries, because they are.

**It says things once, exactly** (after Strunk and White). Short sentences
when the idea is short. Concrete words over abstract ones. No throat-clearing,
no summary of what was just said, no "it is important to note that". An
important point gets its own sentence and nothing after it.

**It trusts the reader's reasoning** (after Velleman). Where the student can
work something out from what they already have, let them: put the evidence
first, ask, and only then name the rule. Show the reasoning that leads to an
answer, not just the answer. Treat a mistake as data — "what would make
someone read it that way?" — and never apologise for difficulty or promise
that something is easy.

**It is playful where the subject is** (after Hofstadter). Puzzles, small
mischief, unexpected connections — a word hiding inside another, a sentence
that means the opposite with one accent moved, a Latin translator caught
copying. Humour comes from the Greek, the story, or the characters; never
from the narrator straining to be liked.

**It lives inside the language** (after Ørberg). Let Greek speak before it
is explained. Begin a section, where possible, with a Greek sentence the
student can almost read, and let the explanation answer the question the
sentence raises. Prefer "Look at **εἶπε**" to "The aorist is formed by".
Prefer Greek examples about the people in the room to abstract ones.

## Concrete rules

**Address.** The student is *you*. The class in the story is *the class* or
by name. In teacher notes, *you* is the teacher and the pupils are *the
class* or *pupils*; teacher notes are collegial and brief — one teacher
telling another what they have seen work.

**Openings.** The first sentence of a New Greek section should usually be
Greek, or point at Greek the student has just seen. Definitions come
after the student has had a chance to notice the pattern.

**Questions.** A question in the prose is a real question: the next
sentence must not answer it if the student can. Put the answer one beat
later, or in the exercise. The Question movement asks things that have no
single right answer; it never asks a question whose answer is "yes, as the
lesson said".

**Cut on sight.** *It is important to note; it is worth noting; it should be
noted; simply; just; of course; obviously; clearly; basically; in other
words; as we have seen; let us; we will now; this is where things get
interesting; don't worry; don't panic; tricky; scary.* Also cut any sentence
that only restates the one before it.

**Tics to ration.** The book has some signature moves; each loses force if
it appears more than once a lesson: "That is the whole lesson", "That is not
decoration", "X is not a Y. It is a Z.", one-word paragraphs, "Notice …"
at the start of a paragraph, a closing aphorism after every explanation.
Keep the best instance in each lesson and rewrite the rest plainly.

**Length.** The voice pass should make the book shorter, not longer. Where
a passage is already good, leave it alone.

## Before and after

These are from the book itself.

**Lead with the Greek, let the reader notice (Lesson 14).**

> *Before.* The **aorist** is the tense Greek uses for an action looked at
> as **one complete thing** — it happened, that is all, no comment on how
> long it took or whether it was still going on.

> *After.* Marcus said two things this lesson. **ἔλεγε** — *he kept saying*.
> **εἶπε** — *he said*. Both are past; neither is further back than the
> other. What is different is how you are made to look at the action: from
> inside, while it goes on, or from outside, as one complete thing. Greek
> calls the second the **aorist**.

**Ask, then let the student answer (Lesson 9).**

> *Before.* English has to use word order and the word *to*. Greek uses an
> ending, and can then put the three nouns in whatever order it likes.

> *After.* Try putting **τῷ διδασκάλῳ** at the front of the sentence. Does
> anything change about who gets the letter? English would need *to* and a
> fixed position to say it. Greek has said it already, in the ending.

**Say it once (a teacher note).**

> *Before.* This is her lesson, and the point of giving it to her is that the
> pupil who has been impatient with parsing all along turns out to parse
> fastest when the parsing is a story — because for her it always was one.
> Let her account of the passage stand as the model and build the grammar
> underneath it, rather than the other way round.

> *After.* Sabina has been impatient with parsing all along; here she parses
> fastest, because the parsing is a story, and for her it always was one.
> Let her account stand as the model and build the grammar under it.

**Keep what already works.** Most of the book already sounds right. These
are examples to protect, not to rewrite:

> This is why vocabulary lists give you the aorist. It is not decoration.

> **λούεται** means *is washed* or *washes itself*, and only **ὑπό** or the
> world decides. When a language lets two meanings share one form, who
> carries the burden — the writer, the reader, or the cat?

## What the voice pass must not change

Every Greek word, every Greek sentence, every quoted text, every
exercise item's Greek, every answer in a key, every fact, date, count and
cross-reference, every heading and block marker, and the story's events.
The characters' dialogue may be tightened but must still sound like them.
