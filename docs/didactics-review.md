# What the research says: didactics of beginners' Greek at 13–15

A review made in October 2026 for the expansion of this course, from the
published literature on teaching ancient Greek and Latin at school level
and from second-language-acquisition and cognitive-science research where
it transfers. Each claim is marked **[Strong]**, **[Moderate]**, **[Weak]**
or **[Tradition]** for the strength of the evidence behind it.

**Headline caveat.** The empirical literature specific to ancient Greek at
school level is close to non-existent. What exists is (a) textbook
tradition, (b) frequency and corpus work (Major; the Dickinson College
Commentaries core lists), (c) small teacher-researcher action studies in
the *Journal of Classics Teaching*, almost all on Latin, with 8–30 pupils,
one class and no control group, and (d) robust general findings from SLA
vocabulary research and cognitive psychology that transfer with
reasonable confidence.

## 1. Order of grammar

What the major courses actually do, from their tables of contents:

Points of consensus **[Tradition, near-universal]**: the article in the
first lesson; contract verbs early (they are too frequent to avoid);
participles before the subjunctive in every course; -μι verbs and the
subjunctive at the end of the first year or in the second. Genuine
disagreement: aorist before imperfect (Athenaze, Taylor) or imperfect
before aorist (Reading Greek, Mastronarde). Empirical work on sequencing
in classical languages: essentially none. Carlon (*Teaching Classical
Languages*, 2013) calls for it and finds none; her transferable SLA
principles are: teach reliable high-frequency rules first, treat
exceptions as vocabulary, and make paradigms "the summary at the finish
line, not the signal at the start" **[Moderate]**.

Frequency data **[Strong for the counts]**: Major finds about 1,100 lemmas
cover ~80% of a typical Greek text; the DCC Greek core (~500 lemmas) covers
~65% of word forms. The article, εἰμί, contract verbs, participles and the
second-aorist and deponent patterns are so frequent that frequency argues
for *early* participles and *early* middle, which the reading-method books
do and the grammar-first books do not.

## 2. Vocabulary presentation

Glossing works, and the form matters **[Strong]**. Yanagisawa, Webb and
Uchihara's 2020 meta-analysis (42 studies, 3,802 participants) found
glossed reading yielded 45% of target words learned on an immediate test
against 27% unglossed, and 33% against 20% delayed. First-language
glosses beat second-language glosses at every level. In-text glosses and
end-glossaries were the least effective formats; multiple-choice glosses
the most effective; marginal glosses in between. Consequence for this
book: a running vocabulary beside the passage, in English, beats both a
list at the end of the book and glosses printed under the text.

List before or after **[Moderate]**: Nation finds deliberate learning from
word lists highly efficient and that courses combining incidental and
deliberate learning outpace incidental-only. Casselman (JCT 2024, n = 8)
found pre-teaching words with a low-stakes test gave the fastest unseen
translations **[Weak: tiny n]**.

Exposures needed **[Moderate–Strong]**: Webb (2007) — ten encounters give
large receptive gains but only ~29% productive recall; Pellicer-Sánchez
and Schmitt (2010) — recognition after 10–15 encounters. A word
introduced in Lesson 12 must recur in at least six to eight later lessons
or it will not stick.

How many words **[Weak–Moderate]**: no Greek data. Milton's benchmark for
successful GCSE French learners is ~2,000 words over five years at two
hours a week, about five a lesson; the Cambridge Latin Course makes
pupils responsible for about 25 words a Stage. For this book: 12–15
required new words a lesson, the rest glossed and not examinable.

## 3. Reading volume and comprehensible input

Theory strong, classical-language evidence weak. The *Journal of Classics
Teaching* "CI FAQs" rest on Krashen and teacher testimony; Moran (JCT
2022) notes that no controlled outcome data exist. Cooper (JCT 2023, 14
Year-10 boys, silent novella reading): 86% found it valuable; gains were
self-reported **[Weak]**. Dutmer (TCL 2021) reports 1930s data where a
reading-only curriculum beat grammar-translation on comprehension, plus
an uncontrolled pilot **[Weak]**.

Transferable **[Strong in general SLA]**: incidental learning needs
roughly 95–98% known-word coverage; below that, reading becomes decoding.
With a 500-word core covering ~65% of real Attic, a fully authentic
passage early on is a decoding exercise — fine for a short Ancient Voice,
not for the longer Reading, which should be composed or adapted until
coverage is high, with the unadapted text as the last tier. Passage
length: no empirical figure; the Cambridge Latin Course runs ~40–60 words
in Stage 1 rising to 150–250 by Book 2. Jacobs (JCT 2024, a Year 9 class):
pupils read word by word, skip unknowns and lean on context; more forms
in connected text help more than more tables **[Weak, but on this age
group]**.

## 4. Exercise design

- **Retrieval practice [Strong].** Karpicke and Roediger (2008): ~80%
  retention after a week with repeated testing against ~35% when testing
  stopped after the first recall; re-studying known items added nothing.
  Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing and distributed practice
  "high utility"; rereading and highlighting "low".
- **Spacing [Strong].** Cepeda et al. (2008): optimal gap about 20–40% of
  a one-week retention interval, 5–10% of a one-year interval. For a
  school year: review a form two to four weeks after introduction and
  again a term later.
- **Interleaving [Moderate].** Pan et al. (2018), Spanish preterite and
  imperfect: interleaving gave no benefit within one session but
  substantially better retention a week later when spread across
  sessions. Mix aorist and imperfect across lessons, not blocked within
  one.
- **Explicit grammar [Moderate].** Norris and Ortega (2000): explicit
  instruction beats implicit, most for structures with no first-language
  analogue — for these pupils the middle voice, aspect, participles.
- **Prose composition [Weak].** No evidence it helps beginners; the
  Cambridge Latin Course excludes it on principle. Keep output short and
  meaningful.
- **Keys [Moderate inference].** Feedback after retrieval is what makes
  testing work, so a delayed key (not printed under the exercise) is
  consistent with the testing-effect literature.

## 5. Narrative and motivation

Evidence is almost entirely anecdotal. The Cambridge School Classics
Project's consultation kept "narrative strength of ongoing storylines and
characters" as a principle — a preference survey, not a learning study
**[Weak/tradition]**. Cooper's extensive-reading pupils preferred novellas
with real plots and disliked repetitive ones. No study isolates a
narrative effect on retention for ages 12–15 in any classical language.

## 6. Other things

- **Accents.** No empirical work. Major argues for teaching the recessive
  rule as one system from the start; Polis prints accents from the first
  lesson. Print them always, teach the rule once, test them lightly.
- **Reading aloud.** Advocacy only, but the phonological-loop literature
  points the same way: one read-aloud task per lesson.
- **Built-in spaced review.** No Greek course has it; the evidence for it
  is the strongest in this review.
- **Assessment.** The Cambridge Latin Course recommends at least 60% of
  grades from comprehension and translation, with diagnostic tests every
  four Stages.

## What this course does with it

1. A ten-item **Retrieval** exercise in every lesson from Lesson 2,
   drawing on the previous three or four lessons, keyed in the teacher's
   edition only. **[Strong]**
2. Forms return in the Retrieval exercises two to four weeks after
   introduction; the aorist and imperfect, active and middle, are
   interleaved rather than blocked. **[Strong for spacing; Moderate for
   interleaving]**
3. Readings carry a **running vocabulary beside the Greek**, in English;
   the examinable words stay in the lesson's vocab box. **[Strong]**
4. Required vocabulary is 12–15 words a lesson, chosen from the
   frequency core; everything else is glossed and non-examinable.
   **[Moderate for load; Strong for the frequency basis]**
5. Every core word recurs in the readings of at least six later lessons.
   **[Moderate–Strong]**
6. The grammar order is kept (article first; participles before the
   subjunctive; middle, future, perfect, contract verbs, subjunctive and
   -μι verbs in Part VI). **[Tradition, consistent with the frequency
   data]**
7. Readings are composed through Lesson 14 and tiered (adapted, then
   original) from Lesson 15, growing from about 50 to about 300 words.
   **[Moderate for the coverage threshold; tiering unmeasured]**
8. Composition stays short. **[Weak either way]**
9. Accents are printed throughout and the recessive rule is taught once.
   **[Tradition]**
