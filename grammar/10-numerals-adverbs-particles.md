# Numerals, Adverbs, and Particles

Three subjects that have nothing in common except that they are all
small words, and that a reader meets them constantly.

## Cardinal numbers

**Only the first four decline.** From five onwards the cardinals are
indeclinable, which makes the whole system cheaper to learn than it looks.

| | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | **εἷς, μία, ἕν** | 6 | **ἕξ** |
| 2 | **δύο** | 7 | **ἑπτά** |
| 3 | **τρεῖς, τρία** | 8 | **ὀκτώ** |
| 4 | **τέτταρες, τέτταρα** | 9 | **ἐννέα** |
| 5 | **πέντε** | 10 | **δέκα** |

**εἷς** is third declension in the masculine and neuter, first in the
feminine, and the breathings repay attention:

| | masc. | fem. | neut. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| nom. | **εἷς** | **μία** | **ἕν** |
| gen. | **ἑνός** | **μιᾶς** | **ἑνός** |
| dat. | **ἑνί** | **μιᾷ** | **ἑνί** |
| acc. | **ἕνα** | **μίαν** | **ἕν** |

**ἕν** *one* against **ἐν** *in* is a breathing and an accent; the two are
distinguished in writing and were distinguished in speech.

**δύο** has one oblique form, **δυοῖν**, for genitive and dative, and is
often left undeclined.

**τρεῖς** and **τέτταρες** have two forms each: one for masculine and
feminine, one for neuter.

> **τρεῖς, τριῶν, τρισί, τρεῖς** · neuter **τρία**
> **τέτταρες, τεττάρων, τέτταρσι, τέτταρας** · neuter **τέτταρα**

**τέτταρες** is the Attic form; Ionic and Koine have **τέσσαρες** (§16.3).

Further on: 11 **ἕνδεκα** · 12 **δώδεκα** · 20 **εἴκοσι** ·
100 **ἑκατόν** · 1000 **χίλιοι** · 10,000 **μύριοι**. **ἑκατόν** does not
decline; **χίλιοι** and **μύριοι** do, as ordinary plural adjectives.

**οὐδείς, οὐδεμία, οὐδέν** *no one, nothing* is **οὐδέ** + **εἷς** and
declines exactly like **εἷς**. So does **μηδείς**, its counterpart in
every construction that takes **μή** (§10.5).

## Ordinals, and the alphabetic numerals

Ordinals are ordinary first-and-second-declension adjectives and agree with
their noun.

| | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1st | **πρῶτος** | 6th | **ἕκτος** |
| 2nd | **δεύτερος** | 7th | **ἕβδομος** |
| 3rd | **τρίτος** | 8th | **ὄγδοος** |
| 4th | **τέταρτος** | 9th | **ἔνατος** |
| 5th | **πέμπτος** | 10th | **δέκατος** |

**Greek wrote numbers with its letters.** The twenty-four letters, plus
the three that had dropped out of the alphabet (§1.1), give twenty-seven
signs: nine for the units, nine for the tens, nine for the hundreds. A
keraia (**ʹ**) marks the figure.

| | | | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **αʹ** 1 | **βʹ** 2 | **γʹ** 3 | **δʹ** 4 | **εʹ** 5 | **ϝʹ** 6 |
| **ζʹ** 7 | **ηʹ** 8 | **θʹ** 9 | **ιʹ** 10 | **κʹ** 20 | **λʹ** 30 |
| **μʹ** 40 | **νʹ** 50 | **ξʹ** 60 | **οʹ** 70 | **πʹ** 80 | **ϟʹ** 90 |
| **ρʹ** 100 | **σʹ** 200 | **τʹ** 300 | **υʹ** 400 | **φʹ** 500 | **χʹ** 600 |
| **ψʹ** 700 | **ωʹ** 800 | **ϡʹ** 900 | | | |

So **ιβʹ** is 12 and **ρκεʹ** is 125. Thousands are marked by a stroke
below and to the left: **͵α** is 1000.

This is why the obsolete letters survived. **ϝ** *digamma* (often written
**ςʹ** *stigma* in later manuscripts), **ϟ** *koppa* and **ϡ** *sampi* are
not pronounced in any text a student will read, and the alphabet kept them
anyway, because the numbering would not work without them.

## Adverbs from adjectives

**Take the genitive plural of the adjective and change -ων to -ως.**

| adjective | genitive plural | adverb | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **σοφός** | **σοφῶν** | **σοφῶς** | wisely |
| **δίκαιος** | **δικαίων** | **δικαίως** | justly |
| **ἀληθής** | **ἀληθῶν** | **ἀληθῶς** | truly |
| **ταχύς** | **ταχέων** | **ταχέως** | quickly |
| **πᾶς** | **πάντων** | **πάντως** | altogether |

The accent comes with the genitive plural, which is the point of stating
the rule that way: **σοφῶν** has a circumflex, so **σοφῶς** does.

**The comparative adverb is the neuter singular of the comparative
adjective; the superlative adverb is the neuter plural of the
superlative.**

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **σοφῶς** | **σοφώτερον** | **σοφώτατα** |
| **ταχέως** | **θᾶττον** | **τάχιστα** |
| **εὖ** *well* | **ἄμεινον** | **ἄριστα** |

**εὖ** is irregular and extremely common; it is the adverb to
**ἀγαθός**, and it begins a great many compounds (**εὐδαίμων**,
**εὐγενής**, **εὐαγγέλιον**).

## Adverbs of place, time and manner

Greek builds correlative series: a question word, a demonstrative, a
relative, and an indefinite, all on one stem. Learning the frame is
cheaper than learning the words.

| | **interrogative** | **indefinite** | **relative** | **demonstrative** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| where | **ποῦ** | **που** | **οὗ**, **ὅπου** | **ἐνταῦθα**, **ἐκεῖ** |
| whither | **ποῖ** | **ποι** | **οἷ**, **ὅποι** | **δεῦρο**, **ἐκεῖσε** |
| whence | **πόθεν** | **ποθέν** | **ὅθεν** | **ἐντεῦθεν**, **ἐκεῖθεν** |
| when | **πότε** | **ποτέ** | **ὅτε**, **ὁπότε** | **τότε**, **νῦν** |
| how | **πῶς** | **πως** | **ὡς**, **ὅπως** | **οὕτω(ς)**, **ὧδε** |

The interrogatives are accented; the indefinites are enclitic (§2.7) and
effectively unaccented. **ποῦ ἐστιν;** is *where is he?* and
**ἔστι που** is *he is somewhere*.

Three suffixes do most of the work and transfer to other words:
**-θεν** *from*, **-σε** or **-οι** *to*, **-θι** or **-ου** *at*. So
**οἴκοθεν** *from home*, **οἴκαδε** *homewards*, **οἴκοι** *at home*.

## The negatives οὐ and μή

Greek has two words for *not*, and choosing between them is a grammatical
decision, not a stylistic one.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **οὐ** | denies a **fact**. Used with the indicative in statements. |
| **μή** | denies a **thought**: a wish, a command, a condition, a purpose, a generalisation. Used with the imperative, subjunctive, optative, and with the infinitive and participle when these are conditional or generic. |

> **οὐ γράφει** — *he is not writing* (a fact)
> **μὴ γράφε** — *do not write* (a command)
> **ἐὰν μὴ γράφῃ** — *if he does not write* (a supposition)
> **ἵνα μὴ γράφῃ** — *in order that he may not write* (a purpose)

**οὐ** is written **οὐκ** before a smooth breathing and **οὐχ** before a
rough one. It is proclitic, except at the end of a clause, where it is
written **οὔ**.

**Two negatives do not cancel in Greek.** A compound negative following a
simple one strengthens it: **οὐδεὶς οὐδὲν λέγει** is *nobody says
anything at all*, not *everybody says something*. The order is the test —
compound after simple reinforces; simple after compound cancels.

**οὐ μή** with the aorist subjunctive is an emphatic denial about the
future: **οὐ μὴ γένηται** — *it certainly will not happen*.

And in a question, the negative gives away the expected answer:
**οὐ** expects *yes*, **μή** expects *no* (§14.8).

## μέν … δέ

**μέν** is a promise and **δέ** is its payment. **μέν** says *on the one
hand, and there is another hand coming*; **δέ** brings it.

> **ὁ μὲν πατὴρ σοφός, ὁ δὲ υἱὸς ἄφρων** — *the father is wise, but the
> son is foolish*

Both are **postpositive**: they cannot stand first in their clause, and
take second place behind whatever word the writer chose to put first. That
first word is therefore the word being contrasted, and finding it is how
you read the sentence.

**δέ** also stands on its own as a plain connective, where English would
use *and* or a semicolon. Most sentences of Greek prose after the first
begin with one of **δέ**, **γάρ** or **οὖν**, and the chain is the
architecture of the paragraph:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **δέ** | and, but — the next step |
| **γάρ** | for — the ground of what was just said |
| **οὖν** | therefore — the consequence |
| **ἀλλά** | but — a real adversative, and *not* postpositive |
| **καί** | and; also, even, when it stands before the word it emphasises |
| **τε … καί** | both … and; **τε** is enclitic and postpositive |

A paragraph of Plato can be read as a diagram on this basis alone, before
any of the vocabulary is looked up.

## Modal and emphatic particles

The hardest words in Greek to translate, and often the right translation is
a change of word order or none at all.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἄν** | marks a form as *contingent*: potential optative, contrary-to-fact indicative, generalising subjunctive. It has no translation of its own (§14.4). |
| **γε** | at least, at any rate — restricts or emphasises the word before it. Enclitic. |
| **δή** | indeed, precisely — points, often ironically. |
| **που** | I suppose, somewhere — hedges. Enclitic. |
| **τοι** | you know, let me tell you — addresses the hearer. Enclitic. |
| **μήν**, **μέντοι** | truly; however |
| **ἄρα** | then, it turns out — marks a realisation. Not **ἆρα**, which asks a question. |
| **καίτοι** | and yet |

::: note
**On translating particles.** Denniston's *The Greek Particles* runs to
six hundred pages for this material, which should suggest how much a
one-word gloss leaves out.

The practical advice for a first-year reader is this: **do not translate a
particle twice**. If **γε** has already made you choose an emphatic word
order, it has been translated. Adding *at any rate* on top produces the
fussy English that makes translated Greek sound unlike anything a person
would say.
:::
