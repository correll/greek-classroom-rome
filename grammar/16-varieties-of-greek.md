# Attic, Homeric, and Koine

This course teaches **Attic**, the prose of Athens in the fifth and fourth
centuries BC. It also reads Homer and the Greek of the Septuagint and the
New Testament, which are not Attic. This section says what the differences
are, so that a reader meeting one does not take it for an error.

## The written text

**Everything on the page except the letters is editorial.** A fifth-century
Athenian inscription or a third-century papyrus has:

- **capitals only** — the minuscule hand was developed in the ninth century AD
- **no spaces between words** — *scriptio continua*
- **no accents or breathings** — devised about 200 BC (§2) and not applied
  systematically for a thousand years after that
- **no punctuation**, or almost none
- **no iota subscript** — written adscript where written at all

So **ΤΟΔΕΜΗΔΕΝΑΓΑΝ** is what the reader faced, and **τὸ δὲ μηδὲν ἄγαν**
is what an editor made of it. Nearly always the division is certain. When
it is not, the variants appear in the apparatus at the foot of a critical
edition, and the choice is an argument, not a fact.

Three consequences for a student:

- An accent or a breathing is **evidence about how a later scholar read
  the text**, not about how the author wrote it. It is usually excellent
  evidence; it is not the author's.
- Word division can carry meaning. **οὐδὲν** *nothing* and **οὐδ᾽ ἕν**
  *not even one* are the same letters.
- The **Ionic alphabet** was adopted officially at Athens in 403 BC.
  Texts composed before that were written in an older alphabet with no
  separate **η** or **ω**, and were transliterated afterwards.

## Homeric Greek

Homer's language was never anyone's speech. It is an **artificial literary
dialect**, chiefly Ionic with Aeolic and older forms preserved because the
metre needed them: a poet composing orally keeps a phrase that scans, and
the phrase keeps its archaic shape inside.

The differences a reader meets at once:

| | Homeric | Attic |
|:--|:--|:--|
| the article | **ὁ, ἡ, τό** is a **demonstrative**, *that one, he* | a definite article |
| genitive sg. (2nd decl.) | **-οιο**, **-οο** | **-ου** |
| genitive sg. (1st decl. m.) | **-ᾱο**, **-εω** | **-ου** |
| dative pl. | **-οισι**, **-ῃσι**, **-εσσι** | **-οις**, **-αις**, **-σι** |
| infinitive | **-μεναι**, **-μεν** (**ἔμμεναι**) | **-ειν**, **-ναι** |
| the augment | **optional** — **βῆ** for **ἔβη** | obligatory |
| **ἄν** | **κε(ν)** | **ἄν** |
| **ἡ** for *the* | **ἠ-** forms, **τοῖο**, **τάων** | |
| **-σσ-** | **ὅσσος**, **μέσσος** | **ὅσος**, **μέσος** |
| double consonant | **θάλασσα**, **τέσσαρες** (Ionic) | **θάλαττα**, **τέτταρες** |

**The digamma.** Homer's text has lost the letter **ϝ** [w] (§1.1), but
the metre has not: a line will scan only if a word that once began with
**ϝ** is still treated as beginning with a consonant: **ϝοῖνος** *wine*,
**ϝέργον** *work*, **ϝάναξ** *lord*, **ϝιδεῖν** *to see*, none of which is
printed with its digamma in any edition. A hiatus that should not be there
is usually a digamma.

**Formulas.** The epic is built from repeated phrases — **πολύμητις
Ὀδυσσεύς** *Odysseus of many wiles*, **πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς**
*swift-footed Achilles*, **οἶνοψ πόντος** *the wine-dark sea* — each
filling a fixed part of the line. They are the technique of oral
composition, not decoration, and they explain why an epithet may be
unsuited to the moment: **πόδας ὠκύς** describes a hero who is sitting
down, because the metre needed those syllables.

## Koine

The *common* Greek of the Hellenistic and Roman world, from about 300 BC:
Attic as a second language, spoken from Egypt to Syria by people whose
first language was not Greek, and simplified accordingly. The Septuagint,
the New Testament, Polybius, Epictetus and the papyri are all Koine.

What has changed from Attic:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **the dual** | gone entirely (§6.9) |
| **the optative** | rare; about 70 instances in the New Testament, mostly fixed phrases such as **μὴ γένοιτο** (§14.9) |
| **indirect statement** | **ὅτι** with a finite verb displaces the accusative-and-infinitive (§13.4) |
| **-ττ-** | becomes **-σσ-**: **θάλασσα**, **τέσσαρες** |
| **neuter plural subjects** | increasingly take a plural verb (§5.2) |
| **prepositions** | multiply, taking over work the bare cases did (§15.6); **εἰς** encroaches on **ἐν** |
| **comparatives** | do the work of superlatives |
| **vocabulary** | diminutives lose their force: **τὸ παιδίον** is simply *the child* |
| **-μι verbs** | drift towards **-ω** forms: **ἱστάνω** beside **ἵστημι** |

None of this is decay, though ancient critics said it was and the
Atticising movement of the second century AD tried to reverse it. It is
what happens to any language that becomes an international medium, and the
same list of simplifications could be written for Latin, or for English.

::: note
**Translation Greek.** The Septuagint is a further case again: Greek
written by translators following Hebrew word for word. Its oddities —
**καὶ ἐγένετο** opening a sentence, a redundant pronoun after a relative,
**ἐν** for the Hebrew instrumental *be-* — are not Greek idiom at all but
Hebrew showing through.

A reader should know that **ὁ κύριος ποιμαίνει με** is grammatically
ordinary and rhetorically foreign, and that Plato would not have written
a line of it.
:::

## Metre, enough to hear a line

Greek verse counts **quantity** — long and short syllables — not stress.
This is the hardest thing for an English ear, because English verse counts
stress and nothing else.

A syllable is **long** if it contains a long vowel or a diphthong, **or**
if its vowel is followed by two consonants (which may belong to the next
word). Otherwise it is short. **ζ ξ ψ** count as two.

**Dactylic hexameter**, the metre of Homer, is six feet. A foot is a
**dactyl** (— ∪ ∪) or a **spondee** (— —); the fifth foot is nearly always
a dactyl and the sixth always two syllables.

> **ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλά**

Read it with the long syllables held, not stressed, and the line's shape
appears. The practical rule for a beginner: read what is written, hold the
**η** and **ω** and the diphthongs, and let the rhythm emerge. Beating it
out in stresses produces a jingle that is nothing like the verse.
