# Infinitives and Participles

These are the verb's **non-finite** forms: they have voice and aspect but
no person, and so they cannot by themselves make a statement. The
infinitive is the verb turned into a **noun**; the participle is the verb
turned into an **adjective**. Between them they carry most of the weight of
a Greek sentence, and a reader who can handle them can read Greek.

## Infinitive forms

Six in common use, built from the principal parts (§11.5).

| | active | middle | passive |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| present | **λύειν** | **λύεσθαι** | **λύεσθαι** |
| future | **λύσειν** | **λύσεσθαι** | **λυθήσεσθαι** |
| aorist | **λῦσαι** | **λύσασθαι** | **λυθῆναι** |
| perfect | **λελυκέναι** | **λελύσθαι** | **λελύσθαι** |

Second aorist: **λιπεῖν**, **λιπέσθαι**. Contract: **τιμᾶν**,
**ποιεῖν**, **δηλοῦν**. Athematic: **διδόναι**, **τιθέναι**, **εἶναι**.

**The aorist infinitive has no augment**, and therefore no past meaning
(§11.4). **λῦσαι** is not *to have loosed*; it is *to loose*, seen as a
single act.

Infinitive accents are fixed, not recessive (§11.8): **λῦσαι**,
**λυθῆναι**, **λελυκέναι**, **λιπεῖν**.

## The complementary infinitive

After verbs of **wishing, being able, ordering, beginning, daring,
needing** the infinitive completes the sense, as it does in English.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **βούλομαι μανθάνειν** | I wish to learn |
| **δύναμαι γράφειν** | I am able to write |
| **κελεύει ἡμᾶς μένειν** | he orders us to remain |
| **δεῖ σε λέγειν** | it is necessary for you to speak |

With **δεῖ**, **χρή**, **ἔξεστι** and the other impersonals the person
concerned goes in the **accusative** (**δεῖ σε**) or, with some verbs, the
**dative** (**ἔξεστί μοι**).

The negative with a complementary infinitive is **μή** (§10.5).

## The articular infinitive

The **neuter article** turns an infinitive into a fully declinable noun,
which can then take any case and stand after any preposition. Greek uses
this where English uses a gerund in *-ing*.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **τὸ μανθάνειν** | learning, the act of learning |
| **τοῦ μανθάνειν** | of learning |
| **διὰ τὸ μανθάνειν** | because of learning |
| **πρὸ τοῦ μανθάνειν** | before learning |
| **ἐν τῷ μανθάνειν** | in the course of learning, while learning |
| **εἰς τὸ μανθάνειν** | for the purpose of learning |

The infinitive keeps its verbal nature inside the phrase: it can take an
object and be modified by an adverb. **διὰ τὸ τὴν πόλιν φυλάττειν
καλῶς** — *because of guarding the city well*, with **τὴν πόλιν**
accusative object of an infinitive that is itself the object of **διά**.

This construction is how an abstract idea gets a name in Greek, and it is
one reason Greek philosophical prose is so hard to translate briefly.

## Indirect statement with the infinitive

After verbs of **saying** and **thinking**, Greek reports what was said by
putting the reported verb into the infinitive and its subject into the
**accusative**.

> **λέγει τὸν ἄνδρα σοφὸν εἶναι** — literally *he says the man to be
> wise*; English, *he says that the man is wise*.

Two rules govern the construction and both matter:

**The tense of the infinitive keeps the tense of the original speech**, not
of the reporting verb. **ἔλεγε τὸν ἄνδρα ἐλθεῖν** is *he said that the man
had gone* — the aorist infinitive representing an aorist indicative in the
words actually spoken.

**If the subject of the infinitive is the same as the subject of the main
verb, it is omitted**, and any predicate word stands in the nominative:
**φησὶ σοφὸς εἶναι**, *he says that he himself is wise*, against
**φησὶ αὐτὸν σοφὸν εἶναι**, *he says that the other man is wise*. The case
of one adjective is the whole difference.

The alternative construction, with **ὅτι** and a finite verb, is treated
at §14.8. Verbs of *knowing* and *perceiving* use a third construction,
the supplementary participle (§13.8).

## Participle forms

A participle is declined like an adjective (§7) and agrees with the word
it belongs to in gender, number and case.

**Present active, λύων** — third declension in masculine and neuter, first
in feminine:

| | masc. | fem. | neut. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| nom. sg. | **λύων** | **λύουσα** | **λῦον** |
| gen. sg. | **λύοντος** | **λυούσης** | **λύοντος** |
| dat. sg. | **λύοντι** | **λυούσῃ** | **λύοντι** |
| acc. sg. | **λύοντα** | **λύουσαν** | **λῦον** |
| nom. pl. | **λύοντες** | **λύουσαι** | **λύοντα** |
| gen. pl. | **λυόντων** | **λυουσῶν** | **λυόντων** |
| dat. pl. | **λύουσι(ν)** | **λυούσαις** | **λύουσι(ν)** |
| acc. pl. | **λύοντας** | **λυούσας** | **λύοντα** |

The nominative singulars of the rest, which is what a reader needs:

| | active | middle | passive |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| present | **λύων, λύουσα, λῦον** | **λυόμενος, -η, -ον** | **λυόμενος** |
| future | **λύσων, λύσουσα, λῦσον** | **λυσόμενος** | **λυθησόμενος** |
| aorist | **λύσας, λύσασα, λῦσαν** | **λυσάμενος** | **λυθείς, λυθεῖσα, λυθέν** |
| perfect | **λελυκώς, -υῖα, -ός** | **λελυμένος** | **λελυμένος** |

Second aorist active: **λιπών, λιποῦσα, λιπόν**, declined like **λύων**.
**εἰμί** gives **ὤν, οὖσα, ὄν**.

**The middle and passive participle in -μενος is an ordinary
first-and-second-declension adjective** and is the easiest form in the
system. The active participles are third declension and are not.

Participles are accented **persistently, as nouns** (§11.8):
**λύων, λύοντος**; **λύσας, λύσαντος**; **λυθείς, λυθέντος**.

## Attributive and circumstantial use

**With the article**, a participle is attributive and names a person or
thing by what they do:

> **ὁ γράφων** — *the one writing*, *the writer*
> **οἱ πενθοῦντες** — *those who mourn*
> **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος** — *the one who did the mercy*

**Without the article**, a participle is circumstantial: it stands for a
whole subordinate clause, and the reader must decide which kind.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| time | **ταῦτα εἰπὼν ἀπῆλθεν** — *having said this, he left* |
| cause | **ταῦτα γιγνώσκων οὐκ ἐφοβεῖτο** — *because he knew this …* |
| condition | **ταῦτα ποιῶν ἁμαρτήσει** — *if he does this …* |
| concession | **καίπερ ταῦτα εἰδώς** — *although he knew this* |
| purpose (future ptc.) | **ἦλθε λύσων** — *he came in order to loose* |
| manner, means | **ταῦτα λέγων ἔπειθεν** — *he persuaded by saying this* |

Greek marks the sense when it wants to — **καίπερ** for concession,
**ὡς** for an alleged reason, **ἅτε** for a real one — and otherwise leaves
it to the reader. A translation must choose; the Greek did not have to.

## The genitive absolute

When the participle refers to **somebody not otherwise in the sentence**,
both the participle and its subject stand in the **genitive**, attached to
nothing.

> **ταῦτα λέγοντος τοῦ διδασκάλου, οἱ παῖδες ἐσίγων** — *while the
> teacher was saying this, the children kept silent*
> **τούτων ῥηθέντων, ἀπῆλθον** — *when these things had been said, they
> left*

**The test**: find the participle, find the word it agrees with. If that
word has no other job in the sentence — it is not the subject, not the
object, not governed by any preposition — the construction is absolute.

Aspect works as everywhere else:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| present participle | the action is going on at the same time |
| aorist participle | the action is complete before the main verb |
| perfect participle | the resulting state obtains |

This is the exact counterpart of the Latin **ablative absolute** — *his
rebus dictis* is **τούτων ῥηθέντων** — and a student who has met the Latin
has only to change the case.

## The supplementary participle

After verbs of **perceiving, knowing, showing** and after a few others,
the participle completes the verb's meaning, where English uses *that* or
a gerund.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ὁρῶ σε γράφοντα** | I see that you are writing |
| **οἶδα θνητὸς ὤν** | I know that I am mortal |
| **ἀκούω αὐτὸν ἐλθόντα** | I hear that he has come |
| **παύομαι λέγων** | I stop speaking |
| **τυγχάνει ὢν σοφός** | he happens to be wise |
| **λανθάνει ποιῶν** | he does it unnoticed |

Note **οἶδα θνητὸς ὤν** — the participle is **nominative**, agreeing with
the subject of **οἶδα**, because the knower and the mortal are the same
person. The rule of §13.4 applies here too.

**τυγχάνω**, **λανθάνω** and **φθάνω** reverse the English construction
entirely: the participle carries the main idea and the finite verb becomes
an adverb. **ἔφθη ἐλθών** is *he came first*, not *he anticipated coming*.

## Aspect in the participle is not time

The third and last statement of the rule, and the most important.

A participle's tense tells you **how its action relates to the main
verb**, not when it happened.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **λύων** | loosing — at the same time as the main verb |
| **λύσας** | having loosed — completed before the main verb |
| **λελυκώς** | having loosed, with the result standing |
| **λύσων** | about to loose — after the main verb; purpose |

So **λύσας** in a sentence whose main verb is future refers to the
**future**: **λύσας ἀπελεύσεται**, *having loosed, he will go away*. The
aorist participle is not past; it is prior.
