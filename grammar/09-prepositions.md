# Prepositions

A Greek preposition does not carry its meaning alone. It carries it
**together with the case of the noun that follows**, and a preposition that
takes three cases has three meanings — sometimes three unrelated ones.

The organising idea is spatial, and it survives even in the abstract uses:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **genitive** | motion *from*, origin, separation |
| **dative** | rest *at*, position, accompaniment, means |
| **accusative** | motion *towards*, extent, direction |

A reader who has that triad in mind can usually guess a new preposition's
third sense before looking it up.

## Prepositions with one case

These are the ones a beginner should know first, because they are
unambiguous.

**With the genitive**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἀπό** | from, away from |
| **ἐκ** (**ἐξ** before a vowel) | out of |
| **πρό** | before, in front of |
| **ἀντί** | instead of, in return for |

**With the dative**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἐν** | in, among |
| **σύν** | together with |

**With the accusative**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **εἰς** (**ἐς**) | into, to |
| **ὡς** | to (a person only) |
| **ἀνά** | up, up along |

**ἐν**, **εἰς** and **ἐξ** are proclitic (§2.7): they have no accent and
lean onto the word that follows.

Note the pairing. **εἰς** and **ἐκ** are opposites — *into* and *out of* —
and **ἐν** is the rest between them. Latin keeps the same three with *in*,
*ex* and *in* with the ablative.

## Prepositions with two cases

| | + genitive | + accusative |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **διά** | through | on account of, because of |
| **κατά** | down from, against | down along, according to, during |
| **ὑπέρ** | over, on behalf of | beyond, over (motion) |
| **μετά** | with, among | after |

**μετά** is the one to watch: **μετὰ τῶν φίλων** is *with friends*, and
**μετὰ ταῦτα** is *after this*. Nothing but the case tells you which.

**κατά** generates a great many compounds and idioms: **καθ᾽ ἡμέραν**
*daily*, **κατὰ Ματθαῖον** *according to Matthew*, **κατ᾽ ὀλίγον**
*little by little*.

## Prepositions with three cases

Four of these, and the genitive and dative senses have largely merged in
later Greek, so the three-way distinction is at its sharpest in Attic
prose.

| | + genitive | + dative | + accusative |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἐπί** | upon, in the time of | on, at, in addition to | onto, against, for the purpose of |
| **παρά** | from (a person) | beside, at the house of | to, alongside, contrary to |
| **περί** | concerning, about | around (rare in prose) | around, about (of place or number) |
| **πρός** | from, in the name of | at, near, in addition to | towards, against, with a view to |
| **ὑπό** | by (the agent of a passive), from under | under | under (motion), towards (of time) |

**ὑπό with the genitive is the agent construction**: it is how Greek says
*by whom* a passive action was done (§11.2, §15.3).

> **ὁ παῖς ἐπαιδεύθη ὑπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου** — *the child was taught by the
> teacher*

**παρά** with the three cases is the clearest illustration of the triad:
**παρὰ τοῦ βασιλέως** *from the king*, **παρὰ τῷ βασιλεῖ** *at the
king's court*, **παρὰ τὸν βασιλέα** *to the king*.

## Elision and aspiration

A preposition ending in a short vowel **elides** it before a word
beginning with a vowel, and the elision is marked with an apostrophe. If
the following word carries a **rough breathing**, a final voiceless stop
turns into its own aspirate. Both changes are treated generally at §1.6;
this is where they are met most often.

| | before a smooth breathing | before a rough breathing |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἀπό** | **ἀπ᾽ ἐμοῦ** | **ἀφ᾽ ἡμῶν** |
| **ἐπί** | **ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ** | **ἐφ᾽ ἵππῳ** |
| **κατά** | **κατ᾽ ἐμέ** | **καθ᾽ ἡμέραν** |
| **μετά** | **μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ** | **μεθ᾽ ἡμῶν** |
| **ὑπό** | **ὑπ᾽ αὐτοῦ** | **ὑφ᾽ ἡμῶν** |

**περί** and **πρό** do not elide. **ἐκ** becomes **ἐξ**, not **ἐκ᾽**.

The aspiration is worth recognising for its own sake: **καθ᾽** is
**κατά**, **μεθ᾽** is **μετά**, and a reader who does not know that will
look for a word beginning **καθ-** and not find one.

## Prepositions in compound verbs

Most Greek verbs with a prefix are a preposition plus a simple verb, and
the compound's sense runs from transparent to opaque.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἐκ** + **βάλλω** | **ἐκβάλλω** | I throw out — transparent |
| **ἀπό** + **θνῄσκω** | **ἀποθνῄσκω** | I die — the prefix merely completes |
| **συν** + **ἵημι** | **συνίημι** | I understand — *put together* |
| **ἀνά** + **γιγνώσκω** | **ἀναγιγνώσκω** | I read — *know again* |

Three mechanical facts follow, and all three matter for reading:

**The augment goes inside the prefix, not in front of it** (§11.7).
**ἐκβάλλω** has imperfect **ἐξέβαλλον**, not *ἤκβαλλον*. A past-tense
compound verb therefore hides its augment in the middle of the word, which
is where a beginner forgets to look.

**The prefix elides and assimilates** before the verb exactly as it does
before a noun: **ἀπ-άγω → ἀπάγω**, **συν-λέγω → συλλέγω**,
**ἐν-πίπτω → ἐμπίπτω**.

**A compound verb often repeats its preposition** before the object:
**ἐκβάλλει ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας**. This is not redundancy to be edited away; it
is the normal construction.

## Improper prepositions

A group of adverbs used as prepositions. They are called *improper*
because they never form compound verbs — the test that separates them from
the true prepositions above. Nearly all take the genitive.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἄνευ** | without |
| **ἕνεκα** (usually following its noun) | for the sake of |
| **χάριν** (following its noun) | for the sake of |
| **μέχρι**, **ἄχρι** | as far as, until |
| **πλήν** | except |
| **ἐγγύς** | near |
| **πόρρω**, **πρόσω** | far from |
| **ἔξω**, **ἔσω** | outside, inside |
| **ὁμοῦ**, **ἅμα** (+ dative) | together with |

**ἕνεκα** and **χάριν** normally stand *after* the noun they govern —
**τούτου ἕνεκα**, *for this reason* — which is the opposite of what the
word *preposition* promises, and is the usual reason a reader fails to
construe the phrase.
