# How to Use This Grammar {.unnumbered}

This is the companion volume to *Schola Graeca*. The
textbook teaches Greek in a sequence, through a story, one idea at a time.
This book does not teach anything. It states what is true about the
language, in order, so that you can look something up.

The two are cross-referenced in both directions. A box in the textbook
reading **[§4.2]{.gref}** points to section 4.2 here. A note in this book
reading *(Lesson 8)* tells you where the textbook introduces that topic and
what a student will already know when they meet it.

## What is and is not covered {.unnumbered}

This volume covers Classical Attic as a student of Volume I will meet it,
plus the differences in Homeric and Koine Greek that the textbook raises
in Parts III and IV. It is not a complete scholarly grammar: Smyth and
Kühner–Gerth exist, they are better than this, and they are freely
available. What this book offers instead is a reference sized to a
beginning reader, with the rare forms left out so that the common ones are
easy to find.

Where a rule has exceptions that a first-year reader will actually meet,
the exceptions are given. Where the exceptions are rare, they are named but
not listed, with a pointer to a fuller grammar.

## How sections are numbered {.unnumbered}

Chapters are **§1** to **§16**; subsections take a second number
(**§4.2**). The numbering is stable: new material is added as a further
subsection rather than by renumbering what is already there, so a reference
printed in the textbook will not rot.

## Conventions {.unnumbered}

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ᾱ** | a macron marks a long vowel where the spelling is ambiguous |
| **ᾰ** | a breve marks a short one, in the same case |
| \* | an asterisk marks a reconstructed form that is not attested |
| **†** | a dagger marks a form a beginner may safely ignore |
| (L8) | the textbook lesson that introduces this topic |

Paradigms are given in the order **nominative, genitive, dative,
accusative, vocative**, singular before plural. This is the order used in
most English-language Greek textbooks. It is not the order used in Latin
grammars, and students coming from Latin should notice the difference once
and then stop being bothered by it.

::: note
**Status of this volume.** All sixteen sections are written, and the
paradigm appendix (§17) carries every paradigm the course uses.

The numbering is stable and was fixed before the sections were written, so
every reference printed in the textbook points where it did. New material
will be added as a further subsection rather than by renumbering.

What is deliberately left out: the dual beyond recognition (§6.9), the
optative beyond recognition (§14.2), the fuller enclitic rules (§2.7),
verbal adjectives in **-τέος**, and the rarer third-declension stems. Each
is named where it would have gone, with a pointer to Smyth.
:::
