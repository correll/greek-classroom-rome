# The Second Declension

The second declension contains nouns whose stem ends in **-ο**. The great
majority are masculine or neuter; a small group of feminines exists and
must be learned individually.

## Masculine

| | **ὁ λόγος** *word* | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| | **Singular** | **Plural** |
| nominative | **λόγος** | **λόγοι** |
| genitive | **λόγου** | **λόγων** |
| dative | **λόγῳ** | **λόγοις** |
| accusative | **λόγον** | **λόγους** |
| vocative | **λόγε** | **λόγοι** |

Like **λόγος**: **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** *person* · **ὁ θεός** *god* ·
**ὁ φίλος** *friend* · **ὁ ἵππος** *horse* · **ὁ οἶκος** *house* ·
**ὁ νόμος** *law* · **ὁ χρόνος** *time*.

## Neuter

| | **τὸ δῶρον** *gift* | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| | **Singular** | **Plural** |
| nominative | **δῶρον** | **δῶρα** |
| genitive | **δώρου** | **δώρων** |
| dative | **δώρῳ** | **δώροις** |
| accusative | **δῶρον** | **δῶρα** |
| vocative | **δῶρον** | **δῶρα** |

Like **δῶρον**: **τὸ βιβλίον** *book* · **τὸ ἔργον** *work* ·
**τὸ τέκνον** *child* · **τὸ ζῷον** *living thing* · **τὸ ἱερόν**
*temple*.

::: note
**The neuter rule.** In every Greek neuter noun, of every declension, in
both numbers, the **nominative, accusative, and vocative are identical**,
and the plural of all three ends in **-α**.

This is not a coincidence of the second declension. It is true of **τὸ
ὄνομα** and **τὸ γένος** in the third declension as well, and of every
neuter adjective and participle. It is the single most portable fact in
Greek morphology.

The consequence for reading is that a neuter noun never tells you by its
form whether it is the subject or the object. Something else in the
sentence must.
:::

::: note
**Neuter plural subjects take a singular verb.**

> **τὰ ζῷα τρέχει** — *the animals run*, with **τρέχει** singular.

This rule holds in Attic prose with high regularity and is one of the most
distinctive features of the language. It is usually explained as a survival
from a time when the neuter plural was a collective singular — *the animal
kingdom runs* rather than *the animals run*.

It weakens in Koine, where a plural verb becomes common (§16.3).
:::

## Feminine second-declension nouns

A small number of **-ος** nouns are feminine. Nothing in their form shows
it; the article is the only evidence, and the dictionary is the only
source.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἡ ὁδός** | road, way |
| **ἡ νῆσος** | island |
| **ἡ νόσος** | disease |
| **ἡ βίβλος** | book, papyrus roll |
| **ἡ ἤπειρος** | mainland |
| **ἡ παρθένος** | maiden |

They decline exactly like **λόγος**; only the article differs.
**ἡ ὁδός, τῆς ὁδοῦ, τῇ ὁδῷ, τὴν ὁδόν**.

Several are names of countries, cities, islands, and trees — a semantic
grouping that is real but not reliable enough to replace learning them.

## The vocative

The masculine vocative singular is **-ε**: **ὦ λόγε**, **ὦ φίλε**,
**ὦ ἄνθρωπε**.

**ὁ θεός** has vocative **ὦ θεός**, identical to the nominative, which is
irregular and frequent.

All neuter vocatives are identical to the nominative, in both numbers.

Plural vocatives are identical to the nominative plural throughout the
declension.

## The Attic second declension

A small class has **-ως** for **-ος** throughout, with the omega appearing
in every case: **ὁ νεώς** *temple*, **ὁ λεώς** *people*,
**ἡ ἕως** *dawn*.

| | Singular | Plural |
|:--|:--|:--|
| nominative | **νεώς** | **νεῴ** |
| genitive | **νεώ** | **νεών** |
| dative | **νεῴ** | **νεῴς** |
| accusative | **νεών** | **νεώς** |

The class is small and the forms are given here for recognition only. It is
confined chiefly to Attic, which is where the name comes from; other
dialects have **ναός**, **λαός**.

## Contract nouns

A few second-declension nouns contract: **ὁ νοῦς** (from **νόος**) *mind*,
**τὸ ὀστοῦν** (from **ὀστέον**) *bone*, **ὁ πλοῦς** (from **πλόος**)
*voyage*.

| | **ὁ νοῦς** | **τὸ ὀστοῦν** |
|:--|:--|:--|
| nominative | **νοῦς** | **ὀστοῦν** |
| genitive | **νοῦ** | **ὀστοῦ** |
| dative | **νῷ** | **ὀστῷ** |
| accusative | **νοῦν** | **ὀστοῦν** |

The circumflex throughout is the mark of the contraction, and it is the
reason contract forms are recognisable at sight.

## Accent in the second declension

As in the first declension, the accent is **persistent**: it stays where
the nominative puts it, moving only when §2.3 compels it.

Two consequences recur constantly:

**An accent on the antepenult must move when the ultima lengthens.**

> **ἄνθρωπος** → **ἀνθρώπου**, **ἀνθρώπῳ**, **ἀνθρώπων**, **ἀνθρώποις**
> but **ἄνθρωπον**, **ἄνθρωποι** (short ultima; **-οι** counts short, §2.4)

**A circumflex on a long penult must become an acute when the ultima
lengthens** (§2.3, Rule 3).

> **δῶρον** → **δώρου**, **δώρῳ**, **δώρων**, **δώροις**
> but **δῶρον**, **δῶρα** (short ultima)

Words with the accent already on the ultima take a **circumflex in the
genitive and dative**, singular and plural:

> **θεός, θεοῦ, θεῷ, θεόν · θεοί, θεῶν, θεοῖς, θεούς**
