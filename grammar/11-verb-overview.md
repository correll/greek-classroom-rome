# The Verb: An Overview

A Greek verb form carries five pieces of information at once: **person,
number, voice, mood and aspect**. English needs a string of auxiliaries to
say what Greek says in one word, and the price Greek pays is that the one
word has to be taken apart.

This section names the five categories. §12 shows how the forms are built.

## Person and number

Three persons and, in principle, three numbers.

| | **Singular** | **Plural** |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1st | **λύω** *I loose* | **λύομεν** *we loose* |
| 2nd | **λύεις** | **λύετε** |
| 3rd | **λύει** | **λύουσι(ν)** |

The **dual** (§6.9) exists in the second and third persons — **λύετον**,
past **ἐλυέτην** — and is rare in prose.

Because the ending names the subject, **a separate subject pronoun is not
required**, and when one appears it is emphatic (§8.2).

One consequence that catches every beginner: **a neuter plural subject
takes a singular verb** (§5.2). **τὰ ζῷα τρέχει** is *the animals run*.

## Voice

Three voices, and the middle is the one with no English equivalent.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **active** | the subject acts | **λούω τὸν παῖδα** — *I wash the child* |
| **middle** | the subject acts with itself in view | **λούομαι** — *I wash* (myself, or for my own purposes) |
| **passive** | the subject is acted on | **λούομαι ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός** — *I am washed by my father* |

The middle and passive **share their forms** everywhere except the aorist
and the future, where each has a set of its own. In the present, imperfect
and perfect, one form does both jobs and only the sentence tells you which
is meant: an agent with **ὑπό** and the genitive (§9.3) settles it for the
passive.

The middle is not the reflexive pronoun (§8.7). **λούω ἐμαυτόν** has an
object and presses on it; **λούομαι** has none and reports the washing as
something the subject is involved in. Where English must choose between
*I wash myself*, *I get washed*, and *I have a wash*, Greek has one word
and does not choose.

A large class of verbs has **middle or passive forms and no active at
all**, with perfectly active meaning. These are the **deponents**:
**ἔρχομαι** *I come*, **γίγνομαι** *I become*, **βούλομαι** *I wish*,
**δέχομαι** *I receive*, **ἀποκρίνομαι** *I answer*. The name is a bad
one — nothing was laid aside — but it is universal.

## Mood

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **indicative** | states, asks, denies. The mood of fact. |
| **subjunctive** | the mood of what is willed, feared, planned, or supposed (§14.1) |
| **optative** | the mood of what is wished or merely possible; also the mood of indirect speech in a past framework (§14.2) |
| **imperative** | commands (§14.3) |

The **infinitive** and the **participle** are not moods. They are the verb
turned into a noun and into an adjective respectively, and they keep the
verb's voice and aspect while losing its person (§13).

**Only the indicative has time.** Every other mood carries aspect alone —
which is the subject of the next subsection, and the single most important
idea in the Greek verb.

## Aspect, not tense

An English verb's forms are about **when**. A Greek verb's forms are
mostly about **what shape the action has**.

| | Aspect | What it says |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **present stem** | imperfective | going on, repeated, habitual |
| **aorist stem** | aoristic | the action as a single whole, without regard to duration |
| **perfect stem** | perfective | the action is over and its **result stands** |

In the **indicative**, aspect and time travel together, and the names of
the tenses reflect it:

| | imperfective | aoristic | perfective |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| present time | present **λύει** | — | perfect **λέλυκε** |
| past time | imperfect **ἔλυε** | aorist **ἔλυσε** | pluperfect **ἐλελύκει** |
| future time | future **λύσει** | — | future perfect (rare) |

Outside the indicative, **the time drops away and only the aspect is
left**. This is why the same pair of forms means different things in
different moods:

> **λύειν** and **λῦσαι** — present and aorist infinitive. Not *to loose
> now* and *to loose then*, but *to be loosing* and *to loose*.
> **λῦε** and **λῦσον** — present and aorist imperative. Not *loose now*
> and *loose later*, but *be loosing* (make it your practice) and
> *loose* (do it, once).

::: note
**The commonest mistake in reading Greek** is to translate an aorist
subjunctive, infinitive, imperative or participle as a past tense. It is
not past. The augment is the mark of past time (§11.7), and outside the
indicative the aorist does not take one: **λῦσαι**, **λῦσον**,
**λύσῃ**, **λύσας** have no **ἐ-** anywhere.

If there is no augment, there is no past.
:::

## The principal parts

A Greek verb is quoted in six forms, because six stems are needed and
several of them are not predictable from the first.

| | | **λύω** | **λείπω** *leave* |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | present | **λύω** | **λείπω** |
| 2 | future | **λύσω** | **λείψω** |
| 3 | aorist active | **ἔλυσα** | **ἔλιπον** |
| 4 | perfect active | **λέλυκα** | **λέλοιπα** |
| 5 | perfect middle/passive | **λέλυμαι** | **λέλειμμαι** |
| 6 | aorist passive | **ἐλύθην** | **ἐλείφθην** |

**λύω** is regular and all six follow from the first. **λείπω** is not:
nothing in **λείπω** predicts the aorist **ἔλιπον**, and this is exactly
why the parts are listed.

From the six parts every form of the verb can be built:

| Principal part | gives |
|:--|:--|
| 1 | present and imperfect, all voices |
| 2 | future active and middle |
| 3 | aorist active and middle |
| 4 | perfect and pluperfect active |
| 5 | perfect and pluperfect middle and passive |
| 6 | aorist passive and future passive |

A lexicon entry for a verb **is** its principal parts. Learning to read
that line is the single thing that makes a lexicon usable, which is why
the textbook introduces it alongside the lexicon in Lesson 29.

## Thematic and athematic conjugations

Two ways of attaching an ending to a stem.

**Thematic** verbs — the great majority — insert a vowel, **ο** before
**μ** and **ν**, **ε** elsewhere, between stem and ending:
**λύ-ο-μεν**, **λύ-ε-τε**. They are called **-ω verbs** after their first
person singular.

**Athematic** verbs attach the ending straight onto the stem, with no
vowel between: **δίδο-μεν**, **δίδο-τε**. They are called **-μι verbs**
after their first person singular, and they are the older pattern
(§12.8). There are not many, but they include **εἰμί**, **δίδωμι**,
**τίθημι**, **ἵστημι** and **φημί**, and you cannot read a page of Greek
without them.

## The augment and reduplication

Two prefixes, each marking one thing.

**The augment marks past time**, and occurs **only in the indicative**.
Before a consonant it is **ἐ-**; before a vowel it lengthens that vowel
instead.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **λύω** | **ἔλυον**, **ἔλυσα** | syllabic augment |
| **ἀκούω** | **ἤκουον** | **ἀ-** lengthened to **η-** |
| **ἐλπίζω** | **ἤλπιζον** | **ἐ-** lengthened to **η-** |
| **οἰκέω** | **ᾤκουν** | **οι-** lengthened to **ᾠ-** |

In a **compound verb the augment goes between the prefix and the stem**,
not in front of the word: **ἐκβάλλω → ἐξέβαλλον**,
**προσφέρω → προσέφερον** (§9.6).

**Reduplication marks the perfect**, and it is present in every mood, not
only the indicative, because the perfect is an aspect and not a time. The
initial consonant is repeated with **ε**: **λύω → λέλυκα**,
**γράφω → γέγραφα**. An aspirate reduplicates with its voiceless partner
(**θύω → τέθυκα**), and a verb beginning with a vowel or two consonants
reduplicates by simple lengthening instead (**ἀκούω → ἀκήκοα**,
**γιγνώσκω → ἔγνωκα**).

The pluperfect takes **both**: reduplication for the aspect, augment for
the past time — **ἐ-λε-λύκειν**.

## Accent in the verb

**A verb's accent is recessive** (§2.5): it goes as far back from the end
as the three rules of §2.3 will allow, so it never has to be learned.

> **λύω, λύεις, λύομεν, λύουσι** · **ἔλυον, ἐλύομεν** ·
> **ἀκούω, ἀκούομεν** — and **ἀκούομεν** is accented on the antepenult
> while **ἀκούω** is not, because the ultima differs in length.

Four exceptions are worth naming, and only these:

- **Contract verbs** show a circumflex where the contraction falls:
  **ποιῶ**, **ποιεῖς**, **τιμῶμεν** (§12.2).
- **Participles are accented as nouns**, persistently (§2.5), not
  recessively: **λύων, λύοντος**; **λύσας, λύσαντος**.
- **Infinitives have fixed accents** that are not recessive:
  **λῦσαι**, **λελυκέναι**, **λυθῆναι**.
- A few **imperatives** are irregular: **εἰπέ**, **ἐλθέ**, **λαβέ**,
  **εὑρέ**, **ἰδέ**.
