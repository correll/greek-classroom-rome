# Moods and Clauses

The indicative states what is. The other three moods state what is wanted,
feared, supposed, or commanded — and Greek keeps them apart far more
carefully than English does.

Remember throughout: **outside the indicative there is no time, only
aspect** (§11.4). A present subjunctive is not present; an aorist optative
is not past.

## The subjunctive

Formed from the ordinary stems with the thematic vowel **lengthened**:
**ο → ω**, **ε → η**. That is the whole morphology, and it means a
subjunctive is recognised by a long vowel where a short one was expected.

| | present | aorist act. | aorist pass. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sg. | **λύω** | **λύσω** | **λυθῶ** |
| 2 sg. | **λύῃς** | **λύσῃς** | **λυθῇς** |
| 3 sg. | **λύῃ** | **λύσῃ** | **λυθῇ** |
| 1 pl. | **λύωμεν** | **λύσωμεν** | **λυθῶμεν** |
| 2 pl. | **λύητε** | **λύσητε** | **λυθῆτε** |
| 3 pl. | **λύωσι(ν)** | **λύσωσι(ν)** | **λυθῶσι(ν)** |

**εἰμί** gives **ὦ, ᾖς, ᾖ, ὦμεν, ἦτε, ὦσι(ν)**.

**The aorist subjunctive has no augment** (§11.4), so **λύσω** is both the
future indicative and the aorist subjunctive. Context separates them, and
in practice a subordinating conjunction or **ἄν** is usually standing next
to the subjunctive to settle it.

Its independent uses:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **hortatory** (1st person plural) | **ἴωμεν** — *let us go* |
| **deliberative** (a real question) | **τί ποιῶμεν;** — *what are we to do?* |
| **prohibitive** (**μή** + aorist) | **μὴ τοῦτο ποιήσῃς** — *don't do that* |

Its subordinate uses are purpose (§14.6), the future and general
conditions (§14.5), and indefinite temporal and relative clauses, where it
always stands with **ἄν**.

## The optative

The mark is **-οι-** or **-αι-** before the ending.

| | present act. | aorist act. | present mid. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sg. | **λύοιμι** | **λύσαιμι** | **λυοίμην** |
| 2 sg. | **λύοις** | **λύσαις** | **λύοιο** |
| 3 sg. | **λύοι** | **λύσαι** | **λύοιτο** |
| 1 pl. | **λύοιμεν** | **λύσαιμεν** | **λυοίμεθα** |
| 2 pl. | **λύοιτε** | **λύσαιτε** | **λύοισθε** |
| 3 pl. | **λύοιεν** | **λύσαιεν** | **λύοιντο** |

**εἰμί** gives **εἴην, εἴης, εἴη, εἶμεν, εἶτε, εἶεν**.

Note that a final **-οι** or **-αι** counts **long** in the optative for
accentuation, against the general rule of §2.4: hence **λύσαι**
(optative, accent on the penult) against **λῦσαι** (infinitive).

Three uses, and a first-year reader needs to recognise all three and
produce none.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **wish** (with **εἴθε** or **εἰ γάρ**) | **εἴθε γένοιτο** — *may it happen* |
| **potential** (always with **ἄν**) | **λέγοι ἄν** — *he might say*, *he would say* |
| **of indirect speech**, in a past framework | **εἶπεν ὅτι ἔλθοι** — *he said that he had come* (§14.9) |

::: note
**Why this course treats the optative for recognition only.** It is alive
in Plato and Xenophon and a student reading Attic will meet it on most
pages. But it is already receding in the fourth century, is rare in the
New Testament, and is gone from later Greek (§16.3).

A reader needs to see **-οι-** or **-αι-**, recall *might* or *would*, and
move on. Producing optatives belongs to a second year.
:::

## The imperative

Second and third person, present and aorist. There is no first-person
imperative; the hortatory subjunctive does that job.

| | present act. | aorist act. | present mid. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 2 sg. | **λῦε** | **λῦσον** | **λύου** |
| 3 sg. | **λυέτω** | **λυσάτω** | **λυέσθω** |
| 2 pl. | **λύετε** | **λύσατε** | **λύεσθε** |
| 3 pl. | **λυόντων** | **λυσάντων** | **λυέσθων** |

The third-person imperative has no English equivalent and must be
paraphrased: **λυέτω** is *let him loose*, meaning not permission but
command.

**Aspect decides which stem.** A present imperative asks for an activity —
ongoing, repeated, a rule of life. An aorist imperative asks for a deed,
once and complete.

> **αἰτεῖτε καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν** — *keep asking*, present
> **δός μοι** — *give it to me*, now, aorist

**Prohibition.** Greek forbids with **μή**, never with **οὐ** (§10.5), and
the two aspects use two different forms:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **μή** + **present imperative** | *stop doing it*, or *do not make a habit of it* |
| **μή** + **aorist subjunctive** | *do not do it* (on this occasion) |

There is **no aorist imperative in a prohibition**: the aorist
subjunctive supplies it. **μὴ ποιήσῃς**, not *μὴ ποίησον*.

A few common second-person singular aorist imperatives are irregularly
accented on the ending: **εἰπέ**, **ἐλθέ**, **εὑρέ**, **ἰδέ**, **λαβέ**.

## The particle ἄν

**ἄν** has no meaning of its own and no translation. It marks the form it
stands with as **contingent** — dependent on a condition, whether that
condition is stated or not. Its presence is one of the most informative
signals in the language.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἄν** + **optative** | potential | **λέγοι ἄν** — *he would say* |
| **ἄν** + **past indicative** | contrary to fact | **εἶπεν ἄν** — *he would have said* |
| **ἄν** + **subjunctive**, in a subordinate clause | indefinite or general | **ὃς ἂν λέγῃ** — *whoever says* |

In a subordinate clause **ἄν** often fuses with the conjunction:
**εἰ + ἄν → ἐάν** (or **ἤν**, **ἄν**), **ὅτε + ἄν → ὅταν**,
**ἐπεὶ + ἄν → ἐπάν**, **ἐπειδὰν**. A conjunction ending in **-αν** is
almost always one of these, and it tells you that a subjunctive is coming.

**ἄν** never stands with a future indicative, and never with an
infinitive or participle except in indirect speech representing one of the
constructions above.

## Conditional sentences

The *if*-clause is the **protasis**, the main clause the **apodosis**.
Four classes, and the forms of the two clauses together identify which.

| | protasis | apodosis | sense |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **simple** | **εἰ** + indicative | any | *if this is so* — no comment on whether it is |
| **contrary to fact** | **εἰ** + past indicative | past indicative + **ἄν** | *if it were / had been* — and it is not |
| **future more vivid** | **ἐάν** + subjunctive | future indicative | *if this happens* — and it may |
| **future less vivid** | **εἰ** + optative | optative + **ἄν** | *if this were to happen* — remoter |
| **general** | **ἐάν** + subj. (present), **εἰ** + opt. (past) | present / imperfect indicative | *whenever this happens* |

> **εἰ ταῦτα λέγει, ἁμαρτάνει** — *if he says this, he is wrong* (simple)
> **εἰ ταῦτα ἔλεγεν, ἡμάρτανεν ἄν** — *if he were saying this, he would
> be wrong* (contrary to fact, present)
> **εἰ ταῦτα εἶπεν, ἥμαρτεν ἄν** — *if he had said this, he would have
> been wrong* (contrary to fact, past)
> **ἐὰν ταῦτα λέγῃ, ἁμαρτήσεται** — *if he says this, he will be wrong*
> **εἰ ταῦτα λέγοι, ἁμαρτάνοι ἄν** — *if he should say this, he would be
> wrong*

**The negative of a protasis is μή; of an apodosis, οὐ** (§10.5). This
single fact identifies the two halves even when the word order is unusual.

The contrary-to-fact construction distinguishes present from past by the
**tense** of the indicative: imperfect for present time, aorist for past.
This is the one place where a past indicative does not refer to the past,
and it is worth meeting twice.

## Purpose clauses

**ἵνα**, **ὅπως** or **ὡς** with the **subjunctive**. The negative is
**μή**.

> **ἔρχεται ἵνα μάθῃ** — *he is coming in order to learn*
> **ἔρχεται ἵνα μὴ ἁμάρτῃ** — *in order that he may not go wrong*

After a **past** main verb the optative may replace the subjunctive, by
the sequence rule of §14.9 — **ἦλθεν ἵνα μάθοι** — but the subjunctive is
also kept, for vividness, and is commoner in Koine.

Greek has other ways of expressing purpose, all of them common:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| future participle | **ἦλθε μαθησόμενος** (§13.6) |
| **εἰς τό** + articular infinitive | **εἰς τὸ μαθεῖν** (§13.3) |
| relative + future indicative | **πέμπει ὃς τοῦτο ποιήσει** |

## Result clauses

**ὥστε**, and the mood distinguishes two things English does not
distinguish at all.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ὥστε** + **infinitive** | the result that *naturally follows* — tendency, capacity |
| **ὥστε** + **indicative** | the result that *actually happened* — fact |

> **οὕτω σοφός ἐστιν ὥστε πάντα γιγνώσκειν** — *he is so wise as to know
> everything*
> **οὕτω σοφὸς ἦν ὥστε πάντες ἐθαύμαζον** — *he was so wise that everyone
> marvelled* — and they did

The infinitive construction is far the commoner and is what a reader
should expect. Its negative is **μή**; the indicative's is **οὐ**, which is
the quickest way to tell which one you have.

**The result is usually announced in advance** by **οὕτω(ς)** *so*,
**τοσοῦτος** *so great*, **τοιοῦτος** *such*. When one of these appears,
a **ὥστε** is coming.

**ὥστε** also begins a main sentence, meaning *and so, therefore*.

## Temporal clauses, πρίν, and indirect questions

**Temporal clauses** with **ὅτε**, **ἐπεί**, **ἐπειδή**, **ἕως** take the
indicative for a definite occasion, and **ἄν** with the subjunctive for an
indefinite or repeated one.

> **ὅτε ἦλθεν, ἐχάρημεν** — *when he came, we rejoiced*
> **ὅταν ἔλθῃ, χαιρήσομεν** — *whenever he comes, we shall rejoice*

**πρίν** *before* takes the **infinitive** after an affirmative main
clause, and **ἄν** with the subjunctive (or the plain indicative) after a
negative one:

> **ἀπῆλθε πρὶν ἐμὲ ἐλθεῖν** — *he left before I came*
> **οὐκ ἄπειμι πρὶν ἂν ἀκούσω** — *I shall not leave before I hear*

**Indirect questions** keep the question word and put the verb into the
indicative, or into the optative after a past main verb (§14.9).

> **οὐκ οἶδα τίς ἐστιν** — *I do not know who he is*
> **οὐκ ᾔδει τίς εἴη** — *he did not know who he was*

**ὅστις** (§8.8) is the indirect-question pronoun as readily as **τίς**,
and the indirect forms **ὅπου, ὅποι, ὁπότε, ὅπως** correspond to the
direct ones in §10.4.

A question introduced by **εἰ** is *whether*: **ἐρωτᾷ εἰ ταῦτα ἀληθῆ
ἐστιν**.

## Sequence of moods

Greek, like Latin, divides main verbs into **primary** (present, future,
perfect) and **secondary** (imperfect, aorist, pluperfect). After a
**secondary** main verb, a subjunctive in a subordinate clause **may**
become an optative, and an indicative in indirect speech **may** do the
same.

| main verb | subordinate clause |
|:--|:--|
| primary | subjunctive, or indicative |
| secondary | optative, **or** the original mood retained |

The change is optional, and retaining the original mood is called the
**vivid** construction, because it quotes rather than reports.

> **λέγει ὅτι ταῦτα ποιεῖ** — *he says that he is doing this*
> **εἶπεν ὅτι ταῦτα ποιοίη** — *he said that he was doing this* (optative)
> **εἶπεν ὅτι ταῦτα ποιεῖ** — same sense, mood retained, more vivid

**The optative of indirect speech is the first thing to go** as Greek
develops. In the New Testament the retained indicative is nearly
universal, and by the Byzantine period the optative has vanished
altogether (§16.3). A reader moving between Attic and Koine should expect
the construction to thin out, not to change meaning.
