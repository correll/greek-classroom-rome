# Accentuation

The Greek accent was one of **pitch**, not stress: the accented syllable
was pronounced at a higher musical tone. Dionysius of Halicarnassus
(*De compositione verborum* 11) puts the interval at about a fifth.

Accents were devised by Alexandrian scholars about 200 BC, long after the
texts they annotate, to help readers for whom Greek was no longer native.
By the Koine period the distinction had already become one of stress.

## The three accents

| | | Occurs on |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **´** acute | rising pitch | any of the last three syllables |
| **῀** circumflex | rise and fall within one syllable | only a long vowel or diphthong, and only on the last two syllables |
| **`** grave | the acute suppressed | only on the final syllable, and only when another word follows |

## Names of the syllables

Counting from the end of the word:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ultima** | the last syllable |
| **penult** | the second from the end |
| **antepenult** | the third from the end |

## The three governing rules

**Rule 1 — the three-syllable limit.** The accent cannot stand further back
than the antepenult. **ἄνθρωπος** is accented as far back as Greek permits.

**Rule 2 — the antepenult requires a short ultima.** An acute may stand on
the antepenult only if the last syllable is short. When the ultima
lengthens, the accent must move forward:

> **ἄνθρωπος** (ultima short) → genitive **ἀνθρώπου** (ultima long)

**Rule 3 — the long penult rule.** If the penult is accented *and* is long
*and* the ultima is short, the accent **must** be a circumflex. In every
other case it is an acute.

> **δῶρον** — penult long, ultima short → circumflex
> **δώρου** — penult long, ultima long → acute
> **λόγος** — penult short → acute

These three rules account for almost every accent shift in the noun and
adjective paradigms. They are not optional refinements; a form accented
against them is misspelled.

::: note
**Why a student should care.** Two reasons, and only two.

The accent distinguishes real words — **τίς** *who?* against **τις**
*someone*, **ποῦ** *where?* against **που** *somewhere*, **ἦ** *truly*
against **ἡ** *the*.

And the accent is evidence about the *form*. If you see **ἀνθρώπου** you
know without further inspection that the ultima is long, because otherwise
the accent would have stayed on the antepenult. Readers use this
constantly, usually without noticing.
:::

## Final -αι and -οι

For purposes of accentuation, a final **-αι** or **-οι** counts as
**short**, even though both are diphthongs. Hence **ἄνθρωποι**, not
*ἀνθρώποι*.

The exceptions are the optative mood (**παιδεύσαι**, third singular aorist
optative, where the **-αι** counts long) and the locative adverb
**οἴκοι** *at home*.

## Persistent and recessive accent

**Nouns and adjectives have a persistent accent.** It stays on the syllable
where the nominative singular puts it, moving only when Rules 1–3 compel
it. This is why the accent must be learned with the word: nothing about the
stem predicts whether a given noun is **λόγος** or **θεός**.

**Verbs have a recessive accent.** It retreats as far from the end as the
rules allow, so it can always be predicted. **παιδεύω**, **παιδεύομεν**,
**ἐπαίδευον** — in each case as far back as possible.

The two systems meet in the participle, which is accented as a noun
(persistently) rather than as a verb.

## The grave

An acute on the final syllable becomes a grave when another word follows
without intervening punctuation.

> **ἀγαθός** in isolation → **ἀγαθὸς ἄνθρωπος** in a phrase

The grave is therefore not a separate accent but a notation for an acute
that has been suppressed — probably indicating that the pitch rise did not
occur. An acute stands at the end of a clause or before a punctuation mark.

The interrogative **τίς** is a deliberate exception: it keeps its acute
always, so that it can never be confused with indefinite **τις**.

## Enclitics and proclitics

**Proclitics** have no accent of their own and lean forward onto the
following word. The common ones are the article forms **ὁ ἡ οἱ αἱ**, the
prepositions **εἰς ἐν ἐξ**, the conjunctions **εἰ ὡς**, and the negative
**οὐ**.

**Enclitics** have no accent of their own and lean back onto the preceding
word, which may acquire a second accent as a result: **ἄνθρωπός τις**
*a certain man*. The common ones are **τις τι** (indefinite), the oblique
cases of **ἐγώ σύ**, the present indicative of **εἰμί** and **φημί**
(except the second singular **εἶ** and **φῄς**), and the particles **γε
τε περ**.

The full rules for accenting an enclitic group are intricate and are not
reproduced here; see Smyth §§183–187. A reader needs to recognise an
enclitic, not to accent one.

## Accent in this course

Volume I introduces accentuation in three stages: the marks themselves in
Lesson 2, the three governing rules alongside the article in Lesson 7, and
the recessive verb accent with the present tense in Lesson 11. Students are
expected to accent correctly in written work from Lesson 7 onward, and to
read accents aloud — as stress, if pitch is beyond them — from the start.
