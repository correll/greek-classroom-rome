# The Article

Greek has a definite article and no indefinite article. **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** is
*the man*; **ἄνθρωπος** is *a man* or simply *man*.

The article is the most frequent word in Greek and the most informative.
It agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case, and so announces all
three before the noun arrives.

## Forms

| | masc. | fem. | neut. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Singular** | | | |
| nominative | **ὁ** | **ἡ** | **τό** |
| genitive | **τοῦ** | **τῆς** | **τοῦ** |
| dative | **τῷ** | **τῇ** | **τῷ** |
| accusative | **τόν** | **τήν** | **τό** |
| **Plural** | | | |
| nominative | **οἱ** | **αἱ** | **τά** |
| genitive | **τῶν** | **τῶν** | **τῶν** |
| dative | **τοῖς** | **ταῖς** | **τοῖς** |
| accusative | **τούς** | **τάς** | **τά** |

::: note
**Four forms have no τ.** **ὁ ἡ οἱ αἱ** — the nominatives, masculine and
feminine, singular and plural. These four are also proclitic (§2.8) and so
carry no accent. Everything else in the paradigm begins with **τ-** and
carries an accent.

Learning this one observation removes most of the difficulty from the
table.
:::

The dual (**τώ**, **τοῖν**) survives in Attic but is rare enough that a
first-year reader may safely defer it; see §6.9.

## The article as a signpost

Because the article agrees with its noun, it resolves ambiguities the noun
alone cannot.

**It supplies gender the ending hides.** **ἡ ὁδός** *road* is feminine
despite the **-ος** ending that marks almost every other second-declension
noun as masculine. No rule predicts this. The article is the only evidence,
which is why vocabulary in this course is always learned article-first.

**It distinguishes cases that have collapsed.** Neuter nominative and
accusative are identical in every declension and both numbers. The article
does not help there either — **τὸ δῶρον** is also both — but elsewhere it
does: **τῆς ψυχῆς** is unambiguously genitive where the bare **ψυχῆς**
would be too.

**It marks the subject when no ending can.** Where two nominatives stand
together, the one with the article is the subject and the one without is
the predicate:

> **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά** — *the fine things are hard*, not *hard things are
> fine*. **τὰ καλά** has the article; **χαλεπά** does not.

This convention is regular and is the ordinary way Greek forms a predicate
(§15.2). It is also how a reader tells a definition from a description.

## Attributive and predicate position

The *position* of a modifier relative to the article changes the meaning.

**Attributive position** — the modifier stands between the article and the
noun, or after a repeated article. The modifier describes the noun.

> **ὁ σοφὸς ἄνθρωπος** — *the wise man*
> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὁ σοφός** — *the wise man* (same sense, more emphatic)

**Predicate position** — the modifier stands outside the article–noun
group. The modifier is *asserted* of the noun, and the verb *to be* is
understood.

> **σοφὸς ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — *the man is wise*
> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος σοφός** — *the man is wise*

No verb appears in either of the last two. The position does the work that
English needs *is* to do. (Lesson 10.)

## The article with no noun

The article plus a modifier can stand alone, the noun being understood.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **οἱ σοφοί** | the wise (people) |
| **τὰ καλά** | fine things; what is fine |
| **οἱ ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** | those in the house |
| **τὰ τῆς πόλεως** | the affairs of the city |
| **ὁ τοῦ Φιλίππου** | the (son) of Philip |

This is extremely common and is one of the places where Greek is markedly
more compact than English. **τὰ τῆς πόλεως** is four syllables for what
takes English five words.

### The article with an infinitive

The neuter article turns an infinitive into a noun, which can then be
declined:

> **τὸ μανθάνειν** — *learning*, *the act of learning*
> **διὰ τὸ μανθάνειν** — *because of learning*

See §13.3.

## Uses that differ from English

**With abstract nouns and generalisations**, Greek uses the article where
English does not:

> **ἡ δικαιοσύνη** — *justice* (not *the justice*)
> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — *man*, humanity in general

**With proper names**, Greek often uses the article where English never
does: **ὁ Σωκράτης**. The presence or absence can carry a slight nuance of
familiarity, but it is frequently just idiom and should not be
over-interpreted.

**With possessives**, Greek uses the article where English uses *my*,
*his*, *their*: **ὁ πατήρ** can mean *my father* when the context supplies
the owner.

**It is omitted** in many fixed phrases and prepositional expressions —
**ἐν ἀρχῇ** *in the beginning*, **κατὰ νόμον** *according to law* — and in
poetry, which uses the article far more sparingly than prose. Homer barely
has an article at all in the later sense; what looks like one is usually a
demonstrative pronoun (§16.2).
