# Writing and Sound

## The alphabet

Twenty-four letters, in the order used by every Greek lexicon.

| | Name | Transliteration | Classical Attic value |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Α α | ἄλφα | a | [a] [aː] |
| Β β | βῆτα | b | [b] |
| Γ γ | γάμμα | g | [g]; [ŋ] before **κ γ χ ξ** |
| Δ δ | δέλτα | d | [d] |
| Ε ε | ἒ ψιλόν | e | [e] (always short) |
| Ζ ζ | ζῆτα | z | [zd] |
| Η η | ἦτα | ē | [ɛː] (always long) |
| Θ θ | θῆτα | th | [tʰ] |
| Ι ι | ἰῶτα | i | [i] [iː] |
| Κ κ | κάππα | k | [k] |
| Λ λ | λάμβδα | l | [l] |
| Μ μ | μῦ | m | [m] |
| Ν ν | νῦ | n | [n] |
| Ξ ξ | ξῖ | x | [ks] |
| Ο ο | ὂ μικρόν | o | [o] (always short) |
| Π π | πῖ | p | [p] |
| Ρ ρ | ῥῶ | r, rh | [r]; [r̥] initially |
| Σ σ ς | σίγμα | s | [s]; [z] before a voiced consonant |
| Τ τ | ταῦ | t | [t] |
| Υ υ | ὖ ψιλόν | u, y | [y] [yː] |
| Φ φ | φῖ | ph | [pʰ] |
| Χ χ | χῖ | ch, kh | [kʰ] |
| Ψ ψ | ψῖ | ps | [ps] |
| Ω ω | ὦ μέγα | ō | [ɔː] (always long) |

**Sigma.** **ς** is written at the end of a word, **σ** elsewhere. The
distinction is purely graphic and is a medieval convention; it carries no
information.

**The aspirates.** **θ φ χ** were aspirated stops in the classical period —
**τ π κ** followed by a puff of breath — not the fricatives [θ f x] of
later Greek and of the Erasmian convention. They became fricatives during
the Koine period, which is why **φ** is transliterated *ph* in older
borrowings and *f* in later ones.

**Letters no longer written.** Three letters had dropped out of the Ionic
alphabet adopted at Athens in 403 BC but survive as numerals and in other
dialects: **ϝ** *digamma* [w], **ϟ** *koppa* [k] before back vowels, and
**ϡ** *sampi*. The digamma matters for reading Homer (§16.2), where its
lost consonant still affects the metre.

## Vowels and vowel length

Seven vowel letters represent twelve vowels, because length is phonemic and
is not always written.

| Always short | Always long | Either |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ε**, **ο** | **η**, **ω** | **α**, **ι**, **υ** |

Length is not a detail. It determines where the accent may fall (§2),
whether a syllable scans long or short in verse (§16.4), and in several
paradigms it is the only difference between two forms — first-declension
nominative **ἡ χώρᾱ** and accusative **τὴν χώρᾱν** are distinguished from
the genitive **τῆς χώρᾱς** by the final consonant alone, but **θάλαττᾰ**
nominative and **θάλατταν** accusative differ in the quantity of the
alpha as well.

This grammar marks length with a macron (**ᾱ**) or breve (**ᾰ**) wherever
it is not deducible and wherever it affects accentuation. Printed Greek
texts do not mark it at all.

## Diphthongs

A diphthong is two vowel letters forming one syllable. There are seven
proper diphthongs.

| | Value | | Value |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **αι** | [ai] | **ευ** | [eu] |
| **αυ** | [au] | **οι** | [oi] |
| **ει** | [eː] | **ου** | [uː] |
| | | **υι** | [yi] |

**ει** and **ου** are *spurious diphthongs* in most of their occurrences:
by the fifth century they represented long close [eː] and [uː]
respectively, arising from contraction or compensatory lengthening rather
than from a genuine glide. The spelling is historical.

**ηυ** [ɛːu] and **ωυ** (Ionic) also occur but are uncommon in Attic prose.

### Improper diphthongs (iota subscript)

When long **ᾱ η ω** were followed by **ι**, the iota ceased to be
pronounced in Attic and is written beneath the vowel: **ᾳ ῃ ῳ**. Under a
capital it is written beside the letter instead and is called *iota
adscript*: **ᾼ ΗΙ ῼ**.

The subscript is never pronounced. It is frequently the only mark
distinguishing the dative singular from the nominative (**τῇ ψυχῇ** against
**ἡ ψυχή**), and omitting it in writing is an error, not a shortcut.

## Breathings

Every word beginning with a vowel, a diphthong, or **ρ** carries a
breathing mark.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **᾿** | smooth (*spiritus lenis*) | no sound: **ἀγαθός** = *agathos* |
| **῾** | rough (*spiritus asper*) | [h] before the vowel: **ἁγνός** = *hagnos* |

Rules of placement:

1. On a diphthong the mark stands over the **second** vowel: **αἱ**,
   **οὐ**, **εὐθύς**.
2. On a capital it stands **before** the letter: **Ἀθῆναι**, **Ἑλλάς**. On
   a capital diphthong it stands over the second letter: **Αἴγυπτος**.
3. Initial **υ** is **always** rough: **ὑπέρ**, **ὕδωρ**, **ὑγιής**.
4. Initial **ρ** is always rough, representing a voiceless trill:
   **ῥήτωρ**, **ῥῶ**. A doubled rho inside a word is written **ῤῥ** in some
   editions and **ρρ** in others.

The rough breathing continues an inherited \*s-, which survives as *s-* in
Latin: **ὑπέρ** ~ *super*, **ἕξ** ~ *sex*, **ἡμι-** ~ *semi-*,
**ἅλς** ~ *sal*. The correspondence is regular enough to be worth testing
and irregular enough not to be trusted.

## Consonant classes

Many rules in the verb and the third declension are stated in terms of
these three classes.

| | Voiceless | Voiced | Aspirate |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Labials** (π-class) | π | β | φ |
| **Dentals** (τ-class) | τ | δ | θ |
| **Velars** (κ-class) | κ | γ | χ |

The double consonants are each a stop plus sigma:
**ψ** = labial + σ · **ξ** = velar + σ · **ζ** = [zd].

This matters because a stop meeting a **σ** produces a predictable result,
and that is what generates the future and aorist stems (§12) and the
dative plural of the third declension (§6):

- labial + σ → **ψ** : **γράφ-σω → γράψω**
- velar + σ → **ξ** : **ἄγ-σω → ἄξω**
- dental + σ → **σ** : **πείθ-σω → πείσω**

The liquids (**λ ρ**) and nasals (**μ ν**) behave differently and are
treated at §12.4.

## Euphonic change

Greek adjusts sounds at junctions. Three adjustments are common enough to
recognise on sight.

**Elision.** A short final vowel drops before a word beginning with a
vowel, marked by an apostrophe: **ἀλλά ἐγώ → ἀλλ᾽ ἐγώ**;
**ἐπί αὐτῷ → ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ**. If the following word has a rough breathing, a
final voiceless stop becomes its aspirate: **ἐπί ἡμῖν → ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν**,
**κατά ὅ → καθ᾽ ὅ**.

**Movable consonants.** Certain endings add a final **ν** before a vowel or
at the end of a sentence: **λέγουσι** / **λέγουσιν**, **ἐστί** /
**ἐστίν**. **οὕτω** / **οὕτως** behaves the same way. The addition carries
no meaning; it prevents hiatus.

**Crasis.** Two words merge, marked by a *coronis* (which looks like a
smooth breathing): **τὰ ἄλλα → τἆλλα**, **καὶ ἐγώ → κἀγώ**. Common in
poetry and in fixed phrases.

## Punctuation

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **.** | full stop |
| **,** | comma |
| **·** | raised dot: colon or semicolon |
| **;** | question mark |

Ancient manuscripts had none of these, nor spaces between words, nor
lower-case letters. Word division, accents, breathings, and punctuation are
all editorial, and the earliest of them postdate the texts they are applied
to by centuries (§16.1).
