# Pronouns

Greek has more pronouns than English and uses them less, because the verb
ending already carries the subject (§11.1). A pronoun that is present is
usually present for a reason.

## Personal pronouns

First and second person have their own forms. **There is no third-person
personal pronoun**: Greek supplies one from the oblique cases of
**αὐτός** (§8.3).

| | **ἐγώ** *I* | **σύ** *you* | | **ἡμεῖς** *we* | **ὑμεῖς** *you* (pl.) |
|:--|:--|:--|:-:|:--|:--|
| nom. | **ἐγώ** | **σύ** | | **ἡμεῖς** | **ὑμεῖς** |
| gen. | **ἐμοῦ**, **μου** | **σοῦ**, **σου** | | **ἡμῶν** | **ὑμῶν** |
| dat. | **ἐμοί**, **μοι** | **σοί**, **σοι** | | **ἡμῖν** | **ὑμῖν** |
| acc. | **ἐμέ**, **με** | **σέ**, **σε** | | **ἡμᾶς** | **ὑμᾶς** |

Third person, supplied by **αὐτός**: **αὐτοῦ** *of him*,
**αὐτῇ** *to her*, **αὐτούς** *them*. The nominative **αὐτός** is never a
plain *he*; it always means *himself* or *the same* (§8.3).

::: note
**ἡμεῖς and ὑμεῖς differ by one letter** — a breathing and a vowel that
by the Koine period sounded alike. Scribes confused them constantly, and
so will you. Whenever a sentence turns on *we* against *you*, check the
manuscript tradition before building an argument on it.
:::

## Emphatic and unemphatic forms, and when a pronoun is used at all

The first- and second-person singular have two sets in the oblique cases.
The longer forms (**ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί, ἐμέ**) are **emphatic** and carry their own
accent; the shorter (**μου, μοι, με**) are **enclitic** (§2.7) and carry
none.

- After a preposition, the emphatic form is used: **πρὸς ἐμέ**, not
  *πρός με*.
- Where the pronoun is contrasted, the emphatic form is used:
  **ἐμοὶ μὲν … σοὶ δέ …**
- Otherwise the enclitic is normal: **λέγει μοι** *he tells me*.

**The nominative pronoun is omitted unless it is doing work.**
**λέγω** is *I say*. **ἐγὼ λέγω** is *I say*, as against someone else — and
that contrast is the only reason to write it. A reader who treats
**ἐγώ** as decoration will miss the argument; a reader who treats it as
emphatic will almost always be right.

## αὐτός in its three uses

One word, three jobs. The position relative to the article decides which,
and the test is the one from §3.3.

| | Position | Sense |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **αὐτὸς ὁ ἀνήρ** | predicate | *the man himself* |
| **ὁ αὐτὸς ἀνήρ** | attributive | *the same man* |
| **αὐτόν**, **αὐτῆς**, **αὐτοῖς** | oblique, no article | *him, of her, to them* |

So the intensive sense (*self*) stands outside the article, the identity
sense (*same*) inside it, and the plain pronoun sense occurs only in the
oblique cases, where there is no competition.

**αὐτός** declines like **ἀγαθός** except that the neuter nominative and
accusative singular is **αὐτό**, with no **-ν**.

The crasis **ὁ αὐτός → αὑτός**, **τὸ αὐτό → ταὐτό(ν)** is common; the
rough breathing is the only sign of it, and it must not be read as the
reflexive **αὑτοῦ** of §8.7.

## Demonstratives

Three, and they map onto a space: here, there, and the one just mentioned.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ὅδε, ἥδε, τόδε** | *this here* | points forward, to what is about to be said |
| **οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο** | *this, that* | points back, to what has just been said |
| **ἐκεῖνος, ἐκείνη, ἐκεῖνο** | *that over there* | remote, and often contemptuous or admiring |

**ὅδε** is the article plus enclitic **-δε** and declines accordingly.
**ἐκεῖνος** declines like **ἀγαθός** with neuter **ἐκεῖνο**.
**οὗτος** is irregular enough to set out:

| | masc. | fem. | neut. | | masc. | fem. | neut. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:-:|:--|:--|:--|
| nom. | **οὗτος** | **αὕτη** | **τοῦτο** | | **οὗτοι** | **αὗται** | **ταῦτα** |
| gen. | **τούτου** | **ταύτης** | **τούτου** | | **τούτων** | **τούτων** | **τούτων** |
| dat. | **τούτῳ** | **ταύτῃ** | **τούτῳ** | | **τούτοις** | **ταύταις** | **τούτοις** |
| acc. | **τοῦτον** | **ταύτην** | **τοῦτο** | | **τούτους** | **ταύτας** | **ταῦτα** |

Two features repay attention. The stem vowel follows the article's
(**οὗτος** with **ὁ**, **αὕτη** with **ἡ**, **τοῦτο** with **τό**), so the
forms are less arbitrary than they look. And **αὕτη** *this woman* differs
from **αὐτή** *she herself* by a breathing alone.

**A demonstrative takes the predicate position**: **οὗτος ὁ ἀνήρ** or
**ὁ ἀνὴρ οὗτος**, never *ὁ οὗτος ἀνήρ*. The noun still keeps its article.

## The relative pronoun

| | masc. | fem. | neut. | | masc. | fem. | neut. |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:-:|:--|:--|:--|
| nom. | **ὅς** | **ἥ** | **ὅ** | | **οἵ** | **αἵ** | **ἅ** |
| gen. | **οὗ** | **ἧς** | **οὗ** | | **ὧν** | **ὧν** | **ὧν** |
| dat. | **ᾧ** | **ᾗ** | **ᾧ** | | **οἷς** | **αἷς** | **οἷς** |
| acc. | **ὅν** | **ἥν** | **ὅ** | | **οὕς** | **ἅς** | **ἅ** |

This is the article without its **τ-**, carrying a rough breathing and an
accent instead. Once seen, it cannot be unseen, and it halves the learning.

**The rule of agreement.** A relative takes its **gender and number from
its antecedent** and its **case from its own clause**.

> **ὁ ἀνὴρ ὃν ὁρῶ** — *the man whom I see*: masculine singular from
> **ἀνήρ**, accusative because it is the object of **ὁρῶ**.
> **ἡ οἰκίᾱ ἐν ᾗ οἰκοῦμεν** — *the house in which we live*: feminine
> singular from **οἰκίᾱ**, dative because **ἐν** governs it.

::: note
**Attraction.** Greek often breaks that rule in one direction: a relative
that ought to be accusative is pulled into the **genitive or dative of its
antecedent**.

> **ἄξιος τῆς τιμῆς ἧς ἔχει** for … **ἣν ἔχει** — *worthy of the honour
> which he has*

This is not an error and it is very common. A relative in the wrong case
is nearly always attraction; look at the antecedent before suspecting the
text.
:::

## Interrogative τίς and indefinite τις

The same letters, and the accent is the whole difference (§2.6).

| | **τίς** *who? which?* | **τις** *someone, a certain* |
|:--|:--|:--|
| | always accented, acute, never grave | enclitic, usually unaccented |
| nom. sg. | **τίς**, **τί** | **τις**, **τι** |
| gen. sg. | **τίνος** (**τοῦ**) | **τινός** (**του**) |
| dat. sg. | **τίνι** (**τῷ**) | **τινί** (**τῳ**) |
| acc. sg. | **τίνα**, **τί** | **τινά**, **τι** |
| nom. pl. | **τίνες**, **τίνα** | **τινές**, **τινά** |
| gen. pl. | **τίνων** | **τινῶν** |
| dat. pl. | **τίσι(ν)** | **τισί(ν)** |
| acc. pl. | **τίνας**, **τίνα** | **τινάς**, **τινά** |

Both decline as third-declension nouns of the **-ν-** stem type, with one
set of forms for masculine and feminine and one for neuter.

- **τίς λέγει;** — *who is speaking?*
- **λέγει τις** — *somebody is speaking*
- **ἄνθρωπός τις** — *a certain man*; the enclitic throws a second accent
  back onto the noun

**τί** alone is also *why?*: **τί κλαίεις;** *why are you weeping?*

## Reflexive pronouns

A reflexive sends the action back to the subject. It therefore **has no
nominative**: the nominative is the subject, and a subject does not need
to be referred back to.

| | first person | second person | third person |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| gen. | **ἐμαυτοῦ, -ῆς** | **σεαυτοῦ, -ῆς** | **ἑαυτοῦ, -ῆς** |
| dat. | **ἐμαυτῷ, -ῇ** | **σεαυτῷ, -ῇ** | **ἑαυτῷ, -ῇ** |
| acc. | **ἐμαυτόν, -ήν** | **σεαυτόν, -ήν** | **ἑαυτόν, -ήν, -ό** |

Plural: **ἡμῶν αὐτῶν**, **ὑμῶν αὐτῶν**, and **ἑαυτῶν** (or
**σφῶν αὐτῶν**) — in the first two persons the two words stay apart.

**σεαυτοῦ** contracts to **σαυτοῦ** and **ἑαυτοῦ** to **αὑτοῦ**. That last
contraction is why the rough breathing matters:

> **αὐτοῦ** — *of him* (plain pronoun, §8.3)
> **αὑτοῦ** — *of himself* (reflexive)

**γνῶθι σαυτόν**, the inscription at Delphi, is the second-person
accusative, and it is the form a student of this course meets in Lesson 1
before meeting the paradigm in Lesson 17.

::: note
**The reflexive is not the middle voice.** **λούω ἐμαυτόν** and
**λούομαι** both come out in English as *I wash myself*, and they are not
the same sentence: the first has an object and insists on it, the second
has none and reports the action as something the subject undergoes.
See §11.2.
:::

## Reciprocal, possessive, and the indefinite relative

**ἀλλήλων** *of one another* is reciprocal: it has no nominative and no
singular.

> **ἀλλήλων, ἀλλήλοις, ἀλλήλους** · fem. **ἀλλήλαις, ἀλλήλᾱς** ·
> neut. **ἄλληλα**

**Possessives.** Greek prefers the genitive of the personal pronoun to a
possessive adjective: **ὁ πατὴρ μου** rather than **ὁ ἐμὸς πατήρ**. The
adjectives exist — **ἐμός, σός, ἡμέτερος, ὑμέτερος** — and are emphatic
when used. For the third person there is no possessive adjective at all in
Attic prose: **ὁ πατὴρ αὐτοῦ**, *his father*.

An enclitic **μου** stands outside the article; an emphatic **ἐμός** stands
inside it. The difference is the one in §3.3 again.

**ὅστις, ἥτις, ὅ τι** is the **indefinite relative**: *whoever, whichever,
anyone who*. Both halves decline — it is **ὅς** plus **τις** — giving
**οὗτινος** or **ὅτου**, **ᾧτινι** or **ὅτῳ**. The neuter is written
**ὅ τι**, with a space, to keep it apart from the conjunction **ὅτι**
*that, because*.

**ὅστις** is also the pronoun of the indirect question (§14.8):
**οὐκ οἶδα ὅστις ἐστίν** — *I do not know who he is*.
