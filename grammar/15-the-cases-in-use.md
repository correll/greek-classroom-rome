# The Cases in Use

§§3–7 gave the **forms** of the cases. This section gives their **jobs**.

Greek has five cases where Proto-Indo-European had eight. Two of the lost
three were absorbed by the genitive and the dative, which is why those two
cases each do work that looks unrelated to itself (§15.6).

## The nominative

**The subject of a finite verb**, and whatever is predicated of it.

> **ὁ ἀνὴρ γράφει** — *the man is writing*

**The predicate nominative.** A linking verb — **εἰμί**, **γίγνομαι**,
**φαίνομαι**, and the passives of verbs of calling and appointing — does
not take an object. It equates, and what follows it stays **nominative**:

> **ὁ ἀνήρ ἐστι σοφός** — *the man is wise*
> **ὁ ἀνὴρ ἐγένετο βασιλεύς** — *the man became king*

**Greek can omit the verb altogether.** A nominative noun and a nominative
adjective, with the adjective in the predicate position (§3.3), is a
complete sentence:

> **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί** — *blessed are the poor*
> **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά** — *fine things are hard*

The article is what marks the subject and its absence what marks the
predicate. This is also how Greek tells a definition from a description,
and it carries real weight in philosophical and theological texts.

## The genitive

The genitive is two older cases in one: a true genitive (*of*) and an
ablative (*from*). Nearly every use falls into one group or the other.

**Of the genitive proper:**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| possession | **ἡ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς οἰκίᾱ** — *the man's house* |
| the whole, of a part (*partitive*) | **οἱ σοφοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων** — *the wise among men* |
| material or content | **στέφανος χρυσοῦ** — *a crown of gold* |
| the thing measured | **σταδίων δέκα** — *of ten stades* |
| value or price | **πολλοῦ ἄξιος** — *worth much* |
| time **within which** | **τῆς νυκτός** — *during the night* |
| the object of certain verbs | **ἀκούω τοῦ ἀνδρός** — *I hear the man* |

**Of the ablatival genitive:**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| separation, source | **ἀπέχεται τῶν κακῶν** — *he keeps away from evils* |
| comparison (§7.9) | **σοφώτερος τοῦ πατρός** — *wiser than his father* |
| the agent, with **ὑπό** (§9.3) | **ὑπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου** — *by the teacher* |

Two further uses have sections of their own: the **genitive absolute**
(§13.7) and the **objective genitive**, where the noun governing the
genitive is itself verbal — **ὁ τῶν πολεμίων φόβος** may be *fear of the
enemy* or *the enemy's fear*, and only the context decides.

**Verbs that take a genitive** fall into recognisable families: hearing
and perceiving (**ἀκούω**, **αἰσθάνομαι**), remembering and forgetting
(**μιμνῄσκομαι**, **ἐπιλανθάνομαι**), ruling (**ἄρχω**, **κρατέω**),
desiring (**ἐπιθυμέω**), touching and taking hold of (**ἅπτομαι**,
**λαμβάνομαι**), and beginning (**ἄρχομαι**). The thread is *partial
contact*: to hear **of** a thing, to take hold **of** it.

## The dative

The dative too is more than one case: a true dative (*to, for*), an
instrumental (*with, by*), and a locative (*at, in*).

**Of the dative proper:**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| indirect object | **δίδωσι τῷ παιδὶ τὸ βιβλίον** — *he gives the child the book* |
| advantage or disadvantage | **τοῦτο ἡμῖν χαλεπόν** — *this is hard for us* |
| possession, with **εἰμί** | **ἔστι μοι βιβλίον** — *I have a book* |
| the object of certain verbs | **πείθομαι τῷ πατρί** — *I obey my father* |

**Of the instrumental dative:**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| means | **γράφει καλάμῳ** — *he writes with a pen* |
| manner | **σιγῇ** — *in silence* |
| cause | **φόβῳ** — *out of fear* |
| degree of difference | **πολλῷ σοφώτερος** — *wiser by much* |
| accompaniment, with **σύν** | **σὺν τοῖς φίλοις** |

**Of the locative dative:**

| | |
|:--|:--|
| place where, with **ἐν** | **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** |
| time **when** | **τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ** — *on the third day* |
| respect | **οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι** — *the poor in spirit* |

The **dative of means has no preposition**; **σύν** with the dative is
accompaniment. *He killed him with a sword* is **ἀπέκτεινεν αὐτὸν ξίφει**,
not *σὺν ξίφει* — a plain dative, and a frequent error in composition.

**Verbs that take a dative** are those of obeying, trusting, helping,
pleasing, following, being friendly or hostile: **πείθομαι**,
**πιστεύω**, **βοηθέω**, **ἀρέσκω**, **ἕπομαι**, **φθονέω**. Many
correspond to English verbs that take a direct object, so the construction
must be learned with the verb.

## The accusative

| | |
|:--|:--|
| direct object | **ὁρῶ τὸν ἄνδρα** |
| extent of space | **ἀπέχει δέκα σταδίους** — *it is ten stades away* |
| extent of time | **ἔμεινε τρεῖς ἡμέρας** — *he stayed three days* |
| respect | **ἀλγεῖ τὴν κεφαλήν** — *he has a pain in the head* |
| the subject of an infinitive (§13.4) | **λέγει τὸν ἄνδρα σοφὸν εἶναι** |
| cognate | **μάχην ἐμαχέσατο** — *he fought a fight* |
| adverbial | **τί;** *why?* · **πολύ** *much* · **τὸ λοιπόν** *for the rest* |

**Double accusative.** Verbs of teaching, asking, concealing, depriving and
reminding take two: a person and a thing.

> **διδάσκει τοὺς παῖδας τὴν μουσικήν** — *he teaches the children music*

In the passive the **person** becomes the subject and the **thing** stays
accusative: **οἱ παῖδες διδάσκονται τὴν μουσικήν**.

Note the contrast with §15.2 and §15.3: *time how long* is accusative,
*time when* is dative, *time within which* is genitive. The three are
regular and worth learning as a set.

## The vocative

Direct address, with or without **ὦ**.

> **ὦ Σώκρατες** · **ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι** · **ὦ παῖ**

**In Greek, ὦ is not an exclamation.** It is the ordinary, unmarked way to
address somebody, and its *absence* is the marked form — abrupt, urgent,
or angry. Translating **ὦ Σώκρατες** as *O Socrates* makes Plato sound
like scripture; *Socrates* is nearer.

For many nouns the vocative is identical to the nominative, and context
alone marks it.

## Why the genitive and dative do so much

Proto-Indo-European had eight cases. Greek kept five, and the lost three
were absorbed:

| lost case | absorbed by | surviving in Greek as |
|:--|:--|:--|
| ablative (*from*) | genitive | separation, source, comparison, agent |
| instrumental (*with, by*) | dative | means, manner, cause, accompaniment |
| locative (*at, in*) | dative | place where, time when |

This is why the Greek genitive looks like two cases to a Latinist — Latin
kept the ablative separate — and why the dative seems to do three
unrelated things. They are not unrelated; they are three cases wearing one
set of endings.

Knowing this converts a list to be memorised into a history to be
understood, and it predicts which uses will take a preposition: the older,
sharper distinctions get propped up by **ἐν**, **σύν**, **ὑπό**, **ἀπό**
as the bare case weakens.

## Agreement

Four rules, and the fourth is peculiar to Greek.

**A verb agrees with its subject** in person and number.

**An adjective, article or participle agrees with its noun** in gender,
number and case (§7), **not** necessarily in declension.

**A relative pronoun takes gender and number from its antecedent and case
from its own clause** (§8.5) — subject to attraction.

**A neuter plural subject takes a singular verb** (§5.2):
**τὰ ζῷα τρέχει**. The rule is nearly exceptionless in Attic prose and
weakens in Koine (§16.3).

Two further points of construction:

**Predicate adjectives agree with the subject** even across a linking
verb, which is what makes **φησὶ σοφὸς εἶναι** different from
**φησὶ αὐτὸν σοφὸν εἶναι** (§13.4).

**A compound subject may take a singular verb** agreeing with the nearest
member, especially when the verb comes first. This is common and not an
error.
