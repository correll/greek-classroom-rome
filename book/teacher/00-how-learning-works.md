# How This Book Uses What Is Known About Learning {.unnumbered}

::: note
**This chapter appears only in the teacher's edition.** It says what the
research on learning a language — and on learning anything — has to say
that bears on this book, how strong that research is, and what the book
does about it. Read it before the first lesson; most of the book's
design choices are explained here and nowhere else.
:::

## A caution first

Almost nobody has run a controlled experiment on teaching ancient Greek
to schoolchildren. What exists is a long tradition of textbooks, some
careful counting of which words Greek authors actually use, a scatter of
small classroom studies (nearly all on Latin, nearly all with one class
and no comparison group), and a large, solid body of work from
second-language research and cognitive psychology on how vocabulary and
grammar are learned and kept. This chapter leans on the last of these
wherever it can, says so when it is leaning on tradition instead, and
marks each claim with how far it can be trusted:

| Mark | Meaning |
|:--|:--|
| **Strong** | Many studies, many learners, consistent results; the finding transfers to Greek with little doubt. |
| **Moderate** | Good evidence from related settings, or a consistent pattern from smaller studies. |
| **Weak** | A few small studies, or reasonable inference from the strong findings. |
| **Tradition** | What experienced course-writers agree on; untested, but not to be lightly overturned. |

The sources are listed at the end of the chapter.

## Remembering: retrieval and spacing

**Retrieval practice** *(Strong)*. Trying to recall something is a far
better way to learn it than looking at it again. In the best-known study,
students who kept being tested on foreign words remembered about 80% of
them a week later; students who stopped being tested once they had got a
word right once remembered about 35%, however much they re-read. Re-reading,
highlighting and summarising come out near the bottom of every large
review of study methods; practice testing and spaced practice come out at
the top.

*What the book does.* Every lesson from Lesson 2 opens its Investigation
with a **Retrieval** exercise: ten short items drawn from the three or four
lessons before, never from the lesson in hand. Its key is in this edition
only. Do it from memory — books closed, no vocabulary list — and mark it at
once; the retrieval is the learning and the feedback is what makes it stick.
A pupil who looks the answer up has done a copying exercise.

**Spacing** *(Strong)*. The same amount of practice is remembered far better
when it is spread out. For a fact to be kept for a year, the best gap before
reviewing it is a few weeks; reviewing it the next day helps little.

*What the book does.* Because the Retrieval exercises reach three or four
lessons back, every form and word comes back after a gap and then again
later. Lessons 12, 18, 24 and 30 are review lessons, and their vocabulary
boxes consist of words reintroduced from across their Part. If you move
faster than a lesson a week, consider adding your own short retrieval
quiz from further back; if slower, the built-in gaps are already
generous.

**Interleaving** *(Moderate)*. Practising two easily confused things side by
side — the imperfect and the aorist, the active and the middle — makes the
difference between them stick better than practising each in its own block,
provided the mixed practice is spread over more than one session.

*What the book does.* From Lesson 15 the Retrieval exercises deliberately
mix imperfect with aorist, Attic with Koine, active with middle. Do not
"tidy" them into blocks.

## Words: how many, how often, and how to gloss them

**How many** *(Weak–Moderate)*. There is no evidence specific to Greek. In
school modern languages, successful learners acquire a few words per lesson
over years; the Cambridge Latin Course makes pupils responsible for about
twenty-five per stage.

*What the book does.* Each lesson introduces six to fourteen new words
(about 330 in all), chosen wherever possible from the words Greek authors
use most. Every vocabulary box also lists some earlier words again, marked
with the lesson that first taught them — *(Lesson 5)* — because the
lesson's reading needs them. Those are for review, not for learning anew.
The cumulative list at the back gives every word once, under the lesson
that introduced it.

**How often** *(Moderate–Strong)*. A word met once in reading is rarely
kept. Studies of learners reading in a foreign language find that about
ten encounters are needed to recognise a word reliably, and more to
produce it.

*What the book does.* The Readings are written to bring each lesson's words
back in later lessons. You can help: when a pupil asks for a word you know
was taught, ask which lesson it was in before you answer.

**How to gloss** *(Strong)*. A 2020 analysis of forty-two studies found that
readers learn nearly twice as many words from a glossed text as from an
unglossed one; that glosses in the learner's own language work better than
glosses in the language being learned; and that the *least* effective place
for a gloss is in the line of the text itself or in a list at the back of
the book. Glosses beside the text did better. Glosses that make the reader
choose between two meanings did best of all.

*What the book does.* Every Reading prints the Greek in a wide column with
its English glosses in a narrow column beside it, one line per word. The
vocabulary box tells the pupil which of those words to learn; the rest are
glossed so the reading can go on. A useful routine is to read once with the
gloss column, then a second time with a card over it.

## Reading: how much, how hard, and when the real thing

**Known words** *(Strong in general language research)*. A text can be read,
rather than decoded word by word, only when the reader already knows about
nineteen words in twenty. Below that, every sentence is a puzzle. The
five hundred commonest Greek words cover only about two-thirds of a page of
real Attic.

*What the book does.* The Readings in Lessons 3–14 are composed — a running
story, *Theodoros's tablets*, about the class itself, in the words the class
has. From Lesson 15 every Reading is a real text in **two tiers**: first an
adapted version in the class's vocabulary and grammar, labelled
*adapted*, then the original, glossed. The adapted tier is the reading;
the original is the reward, and the pupils should meet it last, after they
know what it says. The short Ancient Voice, by contrast, puts real Greek in
front of them from Lesson 1, because two or three words can be fully
glossed and still be the real thing.

**Volume** *(Weak in classical languages; Moderate in general)*. Advocates of
extensive reading in Latin report better motivation and fluency, but the
studies are small and mostly measure what pupils say about themselves. One
of the more useful small findings is about this age: pupils read word by
word, skip what they do not know and guess from context, and they benefit
more from meeting forms in connected text than from more tables.

*What the book does.* The Readings grow from about fifty words in Lesson 3
to two or three hundred in Part VI. If a class is struggling, have them read
aloud in pairs before translating; if it is flying, set the original tier
first as an unseen and the adapted tier afterwards as a check.

## Grammar and exercises

**Explicit grammar** *(Moderate)*. Telling learners the rule, and showing it,
works better than hoping they will infer it — most of all for features their
own language does not have. For these pupils that means the article, aspect,
the middle voice and participles, which is where this book spends its words.

**Order of topics** *(Tradition)*. Every major Greek course introduces the
article first and participles before the subjunctive, and leaves the -μι
verbs and the subjunctive to the end of the first year or the second. This
book does the same. Courses genuinely disagree about whether the imperfect
or the aorist comes first; this one teaches the imperfect first and the
aorist directly after, and contrasts them at once. Reading-method courses
introduce the middle voice and contract verbs earlier than this book does;
the frequency counts are on their side, and if your class meets many
middles in Part IV you may wish to preview Lesson 31.

**Ten items** *(Moderate inference)*. A handful of examples shows a pattern;
it takes repeated retrieval to make one automatic. Every exercise now has
ten items. They need not all be done in class: the first five make a
lesson's work, the second five a homework or a later retrieval.

**Composition** *(Weak either way)*. There is no good evidence that writing
Greek helps beginners read it, and none that it harms them; some courses
leave it out on principle. This book keeps it short — single sentences,
and a short letter in Lesson 28 — because putting a sentence together
is the clearest test of whether a pupil knows what each ending is for.

**Keys** *(Moderate inference)*. Feedback after an attempt is what makes
testing work. The answer keys are in this edition only, so that the attempt
comes first.

## The story

**Narrative** *(Weak)*. Every successful school course of the last fifty years
in Latin or Greek has a continuing story, and pupils and teachers say they
like it. Nobody has shown that it improves what pupils remember. The one
consistent finding is negative: pupils dislike repetitive text with no plot.
The story in this book is there because its scenes are chosen to dramatise
the grammar of each lesson — when the pupils argue about whose tablet it is,
they are arguing about the genitive — and because the characters'
mistakes let a pupil watch a reasonable error being made and corrected
without making it themselves first. Treat it as a hypothesis about
motivation, and drop it on a day it does not serve.

## Practicalities

**Pace.** Nothing in the book is timed. A lesson has six movements in a
fixed order, and most classes will need more than one meeting for one
lesson: the first three movements make a natural first meeting, the last
three a second. A class that meets often and does homework may take a
lesson a week; a class meeting once a week may need two. The review
lessons (12, 18, 24, 30) can be cut short or stretched to fit the calendar.

**Accents** *(Tradition)*. There is no research on whether to teach the
accents. This book prints them everywhere, teaches the rule for verbs once,
and asks for accents in pupils' own Greek only on verbs. Accept
unaccented nouns in composition; correct wrong ones.

**Reading aloud** *(Weak)*. No direct evidence, but every course that has
tried it recommends it, and what is known about working memory points
the same way. Have every Reading read aloud before it is translated.

**Assessment** *(Tradition)*. Weight comprehension and explanation more than
forms; a suggested scheme is in the Scope and Sequence. A short test of
vocabulary before a Reading, low-stakes and marked at once, is itself a
retrieval exercise.
