# The Characters: Notes for the Teacher {.unnumbered}

::: note
**This chapter appears only in the teacher's edition.** The student edition
contains no cast list, no portraits, and no biographies, by design.

A child told in advance that Marcus is *the clever skeptic* will watch for
the label instead of the behaviour, and will have been handed the answer to a
question the book wants them to work out. The characters are revealed through
what they do. Your job is to know who they are so that you can play them
straight; the class's job is to notice.

Nothing here should be read aloud.
:::

## How to use these notes

Each pupil exists to embody one way of being wrong, and one way of being
right. They are not personality types for students to identify with. They are
**arguments about method**, walking around in tunics.

Three practical consequences.

**Play the error, not the character.** When Marcus produces a translation
that is grammatically possible and absurd, the class should be asked to
evaluate the translation, never the boy. The point lands when a student says
"that's allowed by the endings but it can't be right in context" — not when
they say "that's just Marcus."

**Let the quiet ones be right.** Livia and Julia are correct more often than
anyone, and the book repeatedly has them say so too late or too quietly. If
your class contains a student like this — it does — that pattern is being
modelled for their benefit, and it should be allowed to pay off visibly.

**Do not resolve the arguments.** Several lessons end with the class split and
Theodoros declining to settle it. That is not an oversight.

## Theodoros of Antioch

*Greek grammarian, about 45.*

**What he is for.** He asks one question — **"What in the sentence tells you
that?"** — and the entire course is downstream of it. He is the voice of the
method: that a reading must be defensible from evidence on the page, and that
a correct answer reached by guessing is a lucky accident rather than
knowledge.

**His manner.** Quietly funny rather than theatrical. He rarely raises his
voice; an especially long silence is more alarming than shouting would be. He
likes questions, including difficult ones, provided they are honest. He is
not fooled by the children, but he remembers being a mischievous pupil
himself.

**His exploitable weakness.** He can be provoked into defending the finer
points of Greek literature at considerable length. The pupils have discovered
this and use it to postpone exercises. If your class discovers the same thing
about you, the book has told them it is a legitimate tactic, and you may take
that up with the author.

**His background**, for consistency if a student asks: he grew up in Antioch,
where Greek was ordinary and Christian communities lived alongside a rich
classical literary culture. His father copied and sold manuscripts, and
Theodoros learned as a boy to tell a good copy from a bad one — and that a
single misplaced word changes a sentence's meaning. His mother taught him
scripture by memory. In his family, classical literature and Christian faith
were not rival possessions; both demanded careful reading, though not with
equal authority. He treats scripture with reverence and will not let the
pupils turn sacred passages into cheap jokes. He respects Homer and Plato
without pretending every pagan author is right about everything.

**His ambition** is modest and worth stating to a class at the end, not the
beginning: to leave behind students who can read a difficult text without
being afraid of it.

## The eight pupils

### Marcus Valerius — the clever skeptic

*Boy, 13, son of the household.*

**For:** cleverness as a failure mode. He finds the shortest route to a
plausible answer and is often rewarded for it, which is exactly the habit the
course has to break. He is the student who would accept a fluent wrong answer
because it sounds authoritative.

**Shows it by:** inventing translations that are technically permitted by the
grammar and absurd in context, hoping to force an admission that Greek is
ambiguous.

**Arc:** he learns that cleverness without attention is a sophisticated form
of laziness. His rivalry with Theodoros becomes genuine intellectual respect.
He reads last in Lesson 30, having prepared properly for the first time.

### Julia Valeria — the precise one

*Girl, 13, Marcus's older sister.*

**For:** accuracy, and its cost. She notices endings and patterns before
anyone else and will not commit until certain.

**Shows it by:** leaving immaculate corrections to Marcus's deliberate
nonsense where he will find them.

**Arc:** she learns to take an intellectual risk before she is sure. Her
difficulty is not competence but the fear of being publicly wrong — which is
worth naming to a class, since it is the commonest reason able students stop
volunteering around this age.

### Felix — the professional troublemaker

*Boy, 12, son of a merchant family.*

**For:** the ear. He is more curious about real speech than formal rules, and
is usually first to notice that a textbook rule does not explain every
sentence.

**Shows it by:** giving everyone Greek nicknames, including the teacher, and
labelling the household cat with a noun on the grounds that it counts as
vocabulary practice.

**Arc:** his ear turns out to be a real talent. He becomes the pupil most
interested in comparing everyday Koine with literary Attic, and Part IV
vindicates him.

### Sabina — the storyteller

*Girl, 12, daughter of a family friend.*

**For:** the claim that grammar and meaning are separable — and its refutation.
She remembers plots and dialogue exceptionally well and is impatient with
parsing.

**Shows it by:** turning exercises into drama, assigning roles to nouns and
verbs and making the class act out sentences.

**Arc:** she comes to see that case, tense, and word order are precisely what
determine who does what to whom — that parsing *is* plot. She is fastest in
the room in Lesson 22. She also has a strong sense of justice and will defend
a ridiculed classmate when it is inconvenient.

### Quintus — the aspiring philosopher

*Boy, 14, son of a minor official.*

**For:** the belief that big ideas float free of small details.

**Shows it by:** deploying a philosophical argument for why he should not have
to do an ordinary exercise.

**Arc:** he learns that a profound idea cannot be separated from the words it
is expressed in. Lesson 17 is his, and it is also where he discovers that
reading philosophy in Greek is mostly a matter of tracking very small words.

### Livia — the quiet observer

*Girl, 14, daughter of a physician.*

**For:** evidence over rhetoric. Her father taught her to observe before
concluding. She is less interested in winning an argument than in finding out
what is true.

**Shows it by:** rarely starting trouble and frequently ending it with one
understated observation — usually that Marcus's elaborate explanation
contradicts his own translation.

**Arc:** she learns to speak earlier. Her reasoning becomes the class's model.
Give her line in Lesson 2 its full weight; it is the first time a pupil
catches the teacher.

### Theodosius — the earnest Christian

*Boy, 12, nephew of a local deacon.*

**For:** the worry that studying pagan authors is a betrayal — a real question,
treated as real.

**Shows it by:** competitiveness about memorisation, and the occasional
biblical proverb deployed to settle an argument it has nothing to do with. He
is sincere rather than sanctimonious.

**Arc:** he learns to read classical literature with discernment rather than
fear, and to distinguish literary appreciation from religious agreement.
Lessons 19 and 21 are his.

### Aelia — the adventurous one

*Girl, 13, family with eastern Mediterranean connections.*

**For:** familiarity mistaken for mastery. She has heard Greek spoken and
knows a few expressions, and sometimes exaggerates the advantage.

**Shows it by:** claiming to understand a phrase from having overheard it,
then improvising when asked to explain its grammar. Her introduction in
Lesson 5 goes wrong in a way Felix repeats all term.

**Arc:** she discovers that recognising is not knowing. Her curiosity becomes
the bridge between the Latin-speaking pupils and the Greek world.

## The class as a system

Marcus and Julia compete over accuracy. Felix and Sabina conspire to make
lessons more entertaining. Quintus turns everything into a debate. Livia
catches the errors everyone else misses. Theodosius guards the seriousness of
scripture and discovers humour in ordinary learning. Aelia connects the room
to the world outside it.

The four girls are not present to balance the four boys. They carry three of
the course's sharpest intellectual positions — Julia's precision, Livia's
evidentialism, Sabina's narrative sense — and their presence in a private
household school of this period is plausible, though it was not universal
Roman practice. If a student asks whether girls really were taught like this,
the honest answer is: in households like this one, sometimes; in the empire at
large, far less often than here.

No pupil is confined to a strength. Each improves at something they began
badly, and that is the arc worth pointing out to a class around Lesson 18 —
by which time they will have watched it happen.
