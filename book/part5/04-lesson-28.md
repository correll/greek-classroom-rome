# A Greek Letter from Rome

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Each pupil writes a letter in Greek. Aelia writes to a cousin in Antioch; Felix writes to the cat. Theodoros marks both with equal seriousness.

> **To be written.** The scene above is the plan; the prose is not yet
> drafted. See the course notes in `README.md` for the drafting order.
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
**Grammar for this lesson.** Composition: the ancient letter formula (**χαίρειν**), greetings, requests, closing; writing correct Greek within a deliberately limited range.

> **To be written.** Present one main grammatical idea with a paradigm
> table, two or three worked examples, and a **From Latin** box wherever
> the Latin parallel either helps or misleads.
:::

::: vocab
**χαίρειν** greetings · **ἐρρῶσθαι** farewell, be well · **ὁ ἀδελφός** brother · **ἡ ἀδελφή** sister · **γράφω σοι** I write to you · **εὔχομαι** I pray, wish

> **To be checked.** Accentuation, vowel length, and the article for every
> noun must be verified against a lexicon before this lesson is taught.
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min"}
**Text for this lesson.** A genuine private letter on papyrus from Roman Egypt — an ordinary person writing to family, with ordinary mistakes, which the pupils find enormously comforting.

> **To be selected and verified.** The passage must be quoted exactly from
> a public-domain edition and cited. Vocabulary beyond the course list must
> be glossed in full. Do not present a composed sentence as a quotation.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
The papyrus letter was written by someone who never expected it to be read nineteen hundred years later. Does that change how you should read it?
:::
