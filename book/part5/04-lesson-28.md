# A Greek Letter from Rome

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"A letter," said Theodoros, "to anyone you like. In Greek. Using only what
you have. I will mark it as I would mark a letter from a grown man, and I
will mark it on whether it would have been understood."

Aelia wrote to a cousin in Antioch, and filled two tablets with the correct formulas and a great many
questions about the harbour.

Quintus wrote to Socrates, on the grounds that the exercise had not
specified the recipient be alive.

Felix wrote to the cat.

**Φῆλιξ τῷ αἰλούρῳ χαίρειν.** *Felix to the cat, greetings.* **πρὸ μὲν
πάντων εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν.** *Before all else I pray that you are well.*
**σὺ εἶ ὁ κύριος τοῦ οἴκου.** *You are the master of the house.* **ἐγὼ
δὲ οὔ.** *I, however, am not.* **ἔρρωσο.** *Farewell.*

Theodoros read it twice and marked it in silence, and then read it aloud
to the class, and then said that it was correct in every particular, that
the formulas were in the right order, that **εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν** was the
accusative and infinitive of Lesson 16 used exactly as a Greek of any
century would have used it, and that the cat would have understood every
word if it had chosen to.

"It is a *joke*," said Marcus.

"It is a letter," said Theodoros. "The joke is in the recipient. The Greek
is in the letter, and the Greek is good. I did not ask for solemnity. I
asked for a letter a Greek could read, and Felix has written one, to a
cat, and the cat's part in it is not my concern." He handed the tablet
back. "Aelia's letter is also correct, and longer, and I have marked them
the same. Anyone who thinks that is unfair may write to me about it. In
Greek."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The shape of a Greek letter

Greek letters, for a thousand years, began and ended the same way, and the
way is built out of grammar you have.

**The opening.** The sender, in the nominative. The recipient, in the
dative. Then the single word **χαίρειν**.

**Ἀπίων Ἐπιμάχῳ χαίρειν.** — *Apion to Epimachus, greetings.*

**χαίρειν** is an infinitive — *to rejoice* — and the verb it hangs from is
not written. The full thought is *Apion [says] to Epimachus to rejoice*:
**λέγει** understood, infinitive following, exactly the construction of
Lesson 16 with its main verb worn away by use. Every Greek letter in
existence begins with an indirect command whose verb nobody has bothered to
write for seven centuries.

Extra warmth goes between recipient and **χαίρειν**: **τῷ πατρὶ καὶ
κυρίῳ**, *to his father and master*; **πλεῖστα χαίρειν**, *very many
greetings*.

**The health wish.** Immediately after, nearly always:

**πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν.** — *Before all else I pray that you
are well.*

**εὔχομαι** — *I pray, I wish* — then **σε**, accusative, then
**ὑγιαίνειν**, infinitive. Lesson 16 again: *I pray you to be well.* The
**μέν** promises a **δέ** that the letter will supply when it gets to its
business — which makes the whole opening one balanced sentence: *first,
health; and then …*

**The body.** Whatever needs saying, in the Greek you have. Short sentences
on **καί** and **δέ** are not a weakness here; they are how people wrote.

**The greetings.** **ἀσπάζομαι** — *I greet* — with the accusative, as many
times as there are people to greet. **ἀσπάζομαι τὸν ἀδελφόν μου καὶ τὴν
ἀδελφήν.** *I greet my brother and my sister.* The verb is **-ομαι**,
Lesson 16's family.

**The close.** One word: **ἔρρωσο** — *be strong*, *farewell*. (To more than
one person, **ἔρρωσθε**.) It is an imperative — a perfect one, a form you
need not learn — and the verb it comes from, **ῥώννυμι**, you need not
learn either: the one form is the one
everybody used.

## Writing within your range

The rule for this lesson is the opposite of the rule for most of them: do
not reach. Use only the forms you are sure of. A letter that says less in
correct Greek is a better letter than one that says more in Greek the
reader has to repair.

This is how real letters were written. Apion, whose letter you will read,
was a sailor, not a scholar; he misspells things, his sentences run on
**καί**, and he is perfectly understood. Aim for Apion.

::: latinbridge
**Latin letters have the same bones and you know them.** *Cicero Attico
salutem* — sender nominative, recipient dative, and *salutem* (*dicit*
understood) where Greek has **χαίρειν** (**λέγει** understood). *Si vales,
bene est* for the health wish. *Vale* for **ἔρρωσο**.

Cicero's letters are the most Greek thing he wrote. He had learned the
form in Greek, as every educated Roman did, and kept it. When you write
**χαίρειν**, you are using the original of the first Latin letter you ever
read.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **χαίρειν** — greetings *(infinitive of **χαίρω**, I rejoice; the opening
  formula)*
- **ἔρρωσο** — farewell *(to several, **ἔρρωσθε**; the closing formula)*
- **ὑγιαίνω** — I am well, I am in health
- **εὔχομαι** — I pray, I wish *(+ accusative + infinitive)*
- **ἀσπάζομαι** — I greet
- **ὁ ἀδελφός** — brother
- **ἡ ἀδελφή** — sister
- **ὁ πατήρ** — father *(genitive **πατρός**; Lesson 16's Homer had it)*
- **ἡ μήτηρ** — mother *(genitive **μητρός**)*

::: note
**ὑγιαίνω** gives English *hygiene*. **ἀσπάζομαι** is the verb behind every
greeting at the end of every letter in the New Testament — *greet
so-and-so* — and it means, originally, to draw someone to yourself; it is
the word for an embrace.

Two of the entries are **-ομαι** verbs, and **ἔρρωσο** is a middle form
too. By now you can use them in the forms given without needing the rest
of the family. **γράφω** takes the dative of the person written to:
**γράφω σοι**, *I write to you*.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros set the exercises before the letters were written, so that the
formulas would be in hand.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **Αἰλία Μαρίᾳ τῇ ἀδελφῇ χαίρειν.**
2. **πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν· ἐγὼ δὲ ὑγιαίνω.**
3. **ἀσπάζομαι τὸν πατέρα καὶ τὴν μητέρα.**
4. **γράφω σοι ὅτι ὁ διδάσκαλος ἀγαθός ἐστιν. ἔρρωσο.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Aelia to Maria her sister, greetings.* — sender nominative, recipient
   dative, **χαίρειν**.
2. *Before all else I pray that you are well; and I am well.* — the **μέν**
   finds its **δέ**, and the health wish is answered with a health report,
   which is how most letters proceed.
3. *I greet my father and my mother.* — **πατέρα**, **μητέρα**: third
   declension accusatives, recognisable from the stem.
4. *I write to you that the teacher is good. Farewell.* — **ὅτι** +
   indicative, the Koine way (Lesson 23); **ἔρρωσο** to one person.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
**Φῆλιξ τῷ αἰλούρῳ χαίρειν.**

1. Which word is the sender and which the recipient? What tells you?
2. **χαίρειν** is an infinitive. What verb is it hanging from, and where is
   that verb?
3. Rewrite the opening as a complete sentence with the missing verb
   supplied.
:::

::: answers
1. **Φῆλιξ** is nominative, so sender; **τῷ αἰλούρῳ** is dative, so
   recipient. The endings, not the order, as in every lesson since 4.
2. **λέγει** — or any verb of saying or bidding — understood and not
   written. It has been dropped by convention: everybody knew it was there.
3. **Φῆλιξ λέγει τῷ αἰλούρῳ χαίρειν.** — *Felix tells the cat to rejoice.*
   A pupil who sees that this is an indirect command in the accusative and
   infinitive shape of Lesson 16, with the dative recipient standing in for
   an accusative subject, has understood the formula from the inside.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Felix to the cat, greetings.
2. Before all else I pray that you are well.
3. I greet my brother and my sister.
4. Farewell. *(to one person)*
:::

::: answers
1. **Φῆλιξ τῷ αἰλούρῳ χαίρειν.**
2. **πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν.** — the formula, whole. Note the
   accent thrown back onto **εὔχομαί** by the enclitic **σε** (Lesson 25).
3. **ἀσπάζομαι τὸν ἀδελφόν μου καὶ τὴν ἀδελφήν μου.** — accept without
   **μου**.
4. **ἔρρωσο.**

Then the letter itself. Mark it on whether a Greek would have understood
it; nothing else.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="A papyrus letter from Roman Egypt (BGU II 423), opening"}
A real letter, from a real sailor, to his father. Apion has just joined the
Roman fleet at Misenum and writes home to a village in Egypt. He is not a
scholar. He spells by ear. He is understood.

**Ἀπίων Ἐπιμάχῳ τῷ πατρὶ καὶ κυρίῳ πλεῖστα χαίρειν. πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαί
σε ὑγιαίνειν καὶ διὰ παντὸς ἐρρωμένον εὐτυχεῖν μετὰ τῆς ἀδελφῆς μου καὶ
τῆς θυγατρὸς αὐτῆς καὶ τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ μου. εὐχαριστῶ τῷ κυρίῳ Σεράπιδι ὅτι
μου κινδυνεύσαντος εἰς θάλασσαν ἔσωσε εὐθέως.**

- **Ἀπίων Ἐπιμάχῳ** — Apion to Epimachus *(nominative, dative)*
- **τῷ πατρὶ καὶ κυρίῳ** — his father and master *(**κύριος**, Lesson 19,
  used of one's father)*
- **πλεῖστα χαίρειν** — very many greetings
- **πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαί σε ὑγιαίνειν** — before all else I pray you are
  well *(the formula)*
- **καὶ διὰ παντὸς ἐρρωμένον εὐτυχεῖν** — and always, in strength, to
  prosper
- **μετὰ τῆς ἀδελφῆς μου** — with my sister
- **καὶ τῆς θυγατρὸς αὐτῆς** — and her daughter *(**θυγάτηρ**, Lesson 8)*
- **καὶ τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ μου** — and my brother
- **εὐχαριστῶ τῷ κυρίῳ Σεράπιδι** — I give thanks to the lord Serapis
  *(**κύριος** again, of a god)*
- **ὅτι** — because
- **μου κινδυνεύσαντος εἰς θάλασσαν** — when I was in danger at sea *(a
  participle with its noun in the genitive, standing apart from the
  sentence: recognise the shape)*
- **ἔσωσε εὐθέως** — he saved me at once *(**εὐθέως** is **εὐθύς**, Lesson
  14, in its Koine dress)*

*Apion to Epimachus his father and master, very many greetings. Before all
else I pray that you are well and always in strength prosper, with my
sister and her daughter and my brother. I give thanks to the lord Serapis
because when I was in danger at sea he saved me at once.*

**Count what you know.** The formula, whole. **κύριος** twice — once of a
father, once of a god, and the same word for both. **ἀδελφή**, **θυγάτηρ**,
**ἀδελφός**: a family, in the cases you learned in Lesson 8. **ὅτι** meaning
*because*. **θάλασσα** in the Koine spelling. **εὐθέως**. A sailor from
Egypt, writing in the second century AD, used almost nothing you have not had.

**And notice what the letter does with the formulas.** It fills them. The
health wish is not an empty phrase; he has just nearly drowned, and *I pray
you are well* is followed at once by *I give thanks that I am*. The shape
every Greek letter had turns out to hold a real event, when a real person
writes in it.

The original is full of spellings no grammar would allow — he writes
**ἐρωμένον** for **ἐρρωμένον**, **Μησήνους** for Misenum, **χέραν** for
**χεῖρα** — and
the letter has been read and understood by every scholar who has handled it
since it came out of the sand. Aim for Apion.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The letter is Berlin papyrus BGU II 423, widely
reprinted; the text here regularises Apion's spellings and is given from
memory of the standard editions. Check it against a printed text before
putting it on the board, and consider showing the class the unregularised
version afterwards: the misspellings are the point.

**On the anachronism.** The papyrus was found in Egypt in modern times; no
teacher in Rome in AD 400 could have had it. The course gives Theodoros a
copy anyway, because no better specimen of an ordinary person's Greek
exists, and admits the licence here rather than hide it. If a pupil asks
how he came by it, the honest answer is that the book did.

**On Felix's letter.** It is marked equal to Aelia's on purpose, and the
reason is given in the scene: the task was correct Greek, and his is
correct. If a class feels the mark is unfair, that is a good argument to
have, and the honest answer is that a longer correct letter and a shorter
correct letter have both done what was asked. Do not raise Aelia's mark;
if anything, point out that Felix's **ἐγὼ δὲ οὔ** is a **μέν**-less **δέ**
used with real wit.

**On Quintus's letter to Socrates.** Allow it. The instruction did not say
the recipient had to be alive, and he will enjoy having found the gap.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. The papyrus letter was written by someone who never expected it to be
   read nineteen hundred years later. Does that change how you should read
   it?
2. Apion spells by ear and is understood. Felix writes correctly to a cat.
   Which of them is the better writer of Greek? Is that the right question?
3. Every Greek letter begins with an infinitive whose verb nobody writes.
   What else in your own language is like that — a form everybody uses and
   nobody could parse?
:::

Aelia asked whether her cousin in Antioch would actually be able to read
the letter.

"Every word," said Theodoros. "Your cousin speaks the Greek of Antioch,
which is the Greek of Apion, which is the Greek you have just written. She
will think you have been living there."

"I have never been there."

"No. But you have been reading its letters for a year, and that is the
next best thing, and in some ways the better one."
