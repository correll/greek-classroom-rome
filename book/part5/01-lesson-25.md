# The Grammar of Questions

::: story
"I will answer any question," said Theodoros, "that is correctly formed in
Greek. Any question at all. You have until the water-clock runs out to write
one."

This was greeted with the suspicion it deserved.

Felix wrote **ποῦ ἐστιν ὁ αἴλουρος;** — *where is the cat?* — and was told,
correctly, that the cat was on the roof.

Sabina asked **τίς ἐστιν ὁ σοφώτατος τῶν μαθητῶν;** — *who is the wisest of
the pupils?* — and was told that the question was grammatical and that the
answer was Livia, and nobody argued.

Livia, when it came to her, read out **λέγει τις τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**

Theodoros waited.

"Is that your question?"

"Yes."

"It is not a question. It is a statement. *Someone is telling the truth.*
True, as it happens, but not something I can answer." He went to the board
and wrote the word twice. "**τίς** with an accent is *who?*, and it stands
at the front, where a question word belongs. **τις** without one is
*someone*; it has no accent of its own, it leans on the word before it, and
so it cannot stand first at all. You have written the second — and it has
slid into second place to prove it. One stroke of the pen, Livia, and you
have told me a fact instead of asking me one."

Livia, who did not make mistakes, looked at her tablet for a long time and
then said, quietly, "I would like to try again," and was allowed to.

Marcus went last, as he had arranged to.

**τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια;**

The room recognised it. It had been on the board in Lesson 4.

"That," said Theodoros, "is a perfectly formed question. It is the best
formed question in this room. And I will answer it exactly as it was
answered the first time it was asked."

He said nothing. He let the silence run until it was clearly the answer.

"Pilate asked it," he said eventually, "and walked out before anyone could
reply. You have asked it, Marcus, and the water-clock has run out. There is
such a thing as a grammatical question that is its own punishment, and you
have just written one. Sit down."
:::

::: newgreek
## The question word

**τίς** — *who?*, *which?* — and its neuter **τί** — *what?* — decline like
the third-declension nouns you have been recognising all year.

| | Masc./Fem. | Neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Nominative sg.** | **τίς** | **τί** |
| **Accusative sg.** | **τίνα** | **τί** |
| **Genitive sg.** | **τίνος** | **τίνος** |
| **Dative sg.** | **τίνι** | **τίνι** |
| **Nominative pl.** | **τίνες** | **τίνα** |
| **Accusative pl.** | **τίνας** | **τίνα** |

**τίς ἐστιν;** — *who is it?* **τίνα βλέπεις;** — *whom do you see?*
**τίνος ἐστὶ τὸ βιβλίον;** — *whose is the book?* **τί λέγεις;** — *what
are you saying?*

## The accent is the whole difference

**τίς** with the acute is the question word. It keeps its acute always — it
never turns to a grave before another word, which no other word in the
language can say.

**τις** with no accent is a different word: *someone*, *a certain*, *some*.
It is an **enclitic**, leaning on the word before it. You met it in Lesson
22 — **ἄνθρωπός τις**, *a certain man*; **Σαμαρίτης δέ τις** — and now you
know why it had no accent of its own.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **τίς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν;** | *Who is telling the truth?* |
| **λέγει τις τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** | *Someone is telling the truth.* |

One accent. A question or a statement. The only other thing that changes
is the word's place: without its accent it cannot stand first, so it slips
in behind the verb. (And a detail for later: before another enclitic, such
as **ἐστιν**, even *someone* takes an accent — **τίς ἐστιν** can be either
word, and only the context tells you which.)

## The other question words

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ποῦ** | where? | **ποῦ ἐστιν ὁ οἶκος;** |
| **πῶς** | how? | **πῶς γιγνώσκεις;** |
| **πότε** | when? | **πότε ἦλθεν;** |
| **διὰ τί** | why? *(literally "on account of what?")* | **διὰ τί βούλεται μανθάνειν;** |

Each has a twin without the accent — **που** *somewhere*, **πως** *somehow*,
**ποτε** *at some time* — and each twin is enclitic. The pattern is the
same every time: the accent asks; its absence states.

## Questions with no question word

*Is the man wise?* has no *who* or *what* in it. Greek asks it in three
ways.

- **σοφός ἐστιν ὁ ἀνήρ;** — the words of the statement, with a question
  mark. The voice did the work.
- **ἆρα σοφός ἐστιν ὁ ἀνήρ;** — **ἆρα** at the front marks a question and
  expects nothing in particular.
- **ἆρ᾽ οὐ σοφός ἐστιν;** — with **οὐ**, the asker expects *yes*. **μὴ
  σοφός ἐστιν;** — with **μή**, the asker expects *no*.

The Greek question mark is **;** — what English uses for a semicolon. You
have been reading it since Lesson 2.

## Indirect questions

*I do not know who he is.* The question has gone inside another sentence.
Greek keeps the question word, accent and all:

**οὐκ οἶδα τίς ἐστιν.**

**οἶδα** is *I know* — a different verb from **γιγνώσκω**, meaning to know a
fact rather than to get to know a thing. Learn it; it is one of the
commonest words in the language.

::: latinbridge
**Latin has the same trap, and you have been falling into it for years.**
*Quis* is *who?* and *quis* is also *anyone*, after *si*, *nisi*, *ne*,
*num*. Latin tells them apart by position; Greek tells them apart by the
accent. If you have ever construed *si quis* as *if who?*, you already know
what Livia felt.

Latin's *num* and *nonne* are **μή** and **ἆρ᾽ οὐ**: *surely not* and
*surely*. The correspondence is exact.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **τίς, τί** — who? what? which? *(the accent is the question)*
- **τις, τι** — someone, something; a certain *(enclitic)*
- **ποῦ** — where?
- **πῶς** — how?
- **πότε** — when?
- **διὰ τί** — why?
- **ἆρα** — *(marks a question; untranslated)*
- **οἶδα** — I know *(a fact)*
- **ἐρωτάω** — I ask *(a question)*
- **ἀποκρίνομαι** — I answer
- **ἡ ἀρετή** — excellence, virtue; being good at what you are *(Lesson 2)*
- **ἡ πόλις** — city *(third declension; accusative **πόλιν**, genitive **πόλεως**)* *(Lesson 15)*
- **οὐδείς, οὐδέν** — no one, nothing *(the **οὐ** word; **οὐδείς, οὐδεμία, οὐδέν**, genitive **οὐδενός** — **οὐδὲ εἷς**, not even one)* *(Lesson 19)*
- **θαυμάζω** — I wonder at, I am amazed; I admire *(Lesson 6)*

::: note
**οἶδα** against **γιγνώσκω**. **γιγνώσκω** is to come to know, to
recognise, to get acquainted with — a process. **οἶδα** is to know, flat —
to have the fact. Socrates's famous claim is built on **οἶδα**: he knows
that he knows nothing. He would have had to use the other verb to say he
was getting to know it.

**ἀποκρίνομαι** is the verb for answering in the Gospels — **ἀποκριθείς**,
*answering*, opens a hundred sentences — and it belongs to the **-ομαι**
family of Lesson 16.
:::

   ending and not the stem.
2. *Blessed are the pure in heart.* — dative of respect: pure *in respect
   of* the heart (Lesson 21).
3. Koine. Attic **ἐθέλω** (Lessons 11 and 23).
4. Made a noun of it: *the one who did the mercy* (Lesson 22).
5. **λέγει ὅτι ὁ διδάσκαλος σοφός ἐστιν.** — the accusative goes back to

6. Dative (Lesson 21; neuter, third declension).
7. Homeric (Lessons 15 and 24).
8. Aorist; *it happened*, *it became*, *there was* (Lesson 14).
9. Emphasis. The ending already says *they*; the pronoun says *they and
   nobody else* (Lesson 21).
10. A command: *go, and you do likewise* (Lesson 22). Two present
    imperatives, though the class has not yet been told the form's name;
    that comes next lesson.

::: {.exercise title="Question or statement?"}
1. **τίς ἔγραψε τὴν ἐπιστολήν;**
2. **ἔγραψέ τις τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
3. **ποῦ ἐστιν ὁ ἀνὴρ ὃς εἶπε τὴν ἀλήθειαν;**
4. **οὐκ οἶδα διὰ τί ἦλθεν.**
5. **πῶς ἦλθεν ὁ παῖς εἰς τὴν πόλιν;**
6. **ἦλθέ πως ὁ παῖς εἰς τὴν πόλιν.**
7. **ἆρα οἶδεν ὁ Μᾶρκος τί λέγει;**
8. **βούλεταί τις μανθάνειν τὴν ἀρετήν.**
9. **τίνα θαυμάζουσιν οἱ παῖδες;**
10. **οὐκ οἶδα ποῦ ἐστιν ὁ αἴλουρος.**
:::

::: answers
1. Question. *Who wrote the letter?* — **τίς**, accented.
2. Statement. *Someone wrote the letter.* — **τις**, enclitic; note that it
   has thrown an accent back onto **ἔγραψέ**, which is what enclitics do to
   the word before them when that word has room.
3. Question. *Where is the man who told the truth?* — **ποῦ** at the front;
   **ὅς** is the relative from Lesson 17, not a question word.
4. Statement containing a question. *I do not know why he came.* — an
   indirect question: the question word is there, the question mark is
   not.
5. Question. *How did the child come into the city?* — **πῶς**, accented
   and first.
6. Statement. *The child came into the city somehow.* — **πως** without
   its accent is *somehow*, and it has done to **ἦλθέ** what **τις** did
   to **ἔγραψέ** in 2. A pupil who translates *how did the child come?*
   has read the letters and not the accent — read it aloud.
7. Question. *Does Marcus know what he is saying?* — **ἆρα** marks it, and
   it has an indirect question inside it: **τί λέγει**.
8. Statement. *Someone wants to learn virtue.* — enclitic **τις** in
   second place, with its accent thrown back onto **βούλεταί**.
9. Question. *Whom do the children admire?* — **τίνα**, accusative: the
   answer will be a person in the accusative.
10. Statement containing a question. *I do not know where the cat is.* —
    like 4. **ποῦ** keeps its accent inside the indirect question, which
    is how you know it is not **που**, *somewhere*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
**τίνος ἐστὶ τὸ βιβλίον;** / **τίνι ἔδωκε τὸ βιβλίον;**

1. Translate both.
2. The question word changes case between them. What does each case tell
   you about the answer that is expected?
3. **ἐστί** has an accent in the first sentence. Why? *(Think about what
   comes before it.)*

Now more pairs, and single sentences. For each, translate, and say what in
the Greek tells you.

4. **τί λέγει ὁ διδάσκαλος;** — what case is **τί**, and what tells you it
   is not the subject?
5. **τίνες εἰσὶν οἱ παῖδες;** — singular or plural, and what tells you?
6. **ἄνθρωπός τις ἦλθεν.** — why does **ἄνθρωπός** carry two accents?
7. **διὰ τί οὐ λέγεις;** — what case is **τί** here, and why? What does the
   phrase mean word for word?
8. **πότε ἦν Σωκράτης ἐν τῇ πόλει;** / **ἦν ποτε Σωκράτης ἐν τῇ πόλει.** —
   translate both; what does **ποτε** mean without its accent?
9. **ποῦ ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή;** / **ἔστι που ἡ ἀρετή.** — translate both. Why
   does **ἔστι** carry an accent in the second sentence, and what does
   **που** mean?
10. **οὐκ οἶδα τίς ἔγραψεν.** — does **τίς** keep its accent inside the
    indirect question? Why can it not mean *someone* here?
:::

::: answers
1. *Whose is the book?* / *To whom did he give the book?*
2. Genitive asks for an owner; dative asks for a recipient — **ἔδωκε** is
   the aorist of **δίδωμι**, Lesson 9, and takes the dative. The case of the
   question word is the case the answer will come in.
3. Because **τίνος** is accented on its first syllable and a two-syllable
   enclitic after such a word keeps its own accent. This is the first time
   the course has asked a pupil to *explain* an enclitic accent rather than
   simply reproduce one; take the answer in any words that show they have
   seen that the enclitic's accent depends on the word before it.
4. *What is the teacher saying?* — **τί** is accusative, the object of
   **λέγει**. The form is the same in both cases (neuter, Lesson 3), so the
   form cannot tell you; **ὁ διδάσκαλος** has the article and the
   nominative ending, and a sentence has one subject.
5. *Who are the children?* — plural: **τίνες** ends in **-ες** like the
   third-declension plurals, and **εἰσίν** and **οἱ παῖδες** agree with it.
6. *A certain man came.* — **τις** is enclitic and has thrown an accent
   onto the last syllable of **ἄνθρωπος**, which, being accented on its
   third syllable from the end, has room for a second. This is **ἄνθρωπός
   τις** from Lesson 22, explained at last.
7. *Why are you not speaking?* — **τί** is accusative, because **διά** with
   the accusative means *on account of*; word for word, *on account of
   what?* A pupil who says *nominative* has forgotten that the preposition
   governs the case.
8. *When was Socrates in the city?* / *Once upon a time Socrates was in the
   city.* — **ποτε** without the accent is *at some time*, *once*. A pupil
   who translates the second as a question has not heard that **ἦν** keeps
   its circumflex and nothing follows it with an accent of its own.
9. *Where is virtue?* / *Virtue is somewhere, I suppose.* — **ἔστι** at the
   head of a sentence takes an accent on its first syllable, because an
   enclitic that begins the sentence has nothing to lean on; **που** is
   *somewhere*, and gives the sentence the shrug of *I suppose*. Accept
   either sense of **που**.
10. Yes: **οὐκ οἶδα τίς ἔγραψεν**, *I do not know who wrote it*. It cannot
    be *someone* because the enclitic cannot stand at the head of its
    clause — it would have had to slip in behind the verb, as Livia's did.
:::

1. Who is the teacher?
2. Where is the book?
3. I do not know who he is.
4. Why does he want to learn?
5. Whom does the teacher see?
6. Someone is writing a letter.
7. When did the children come?
8. Whose is the house?
9. Surely virtue is a fine thing?
10. I do not know what virtue is.

::: answers
1. **τίς ἐστιν ὁ διδάσκαλος;** — with the accent. Without it the word
   would mean *someone*, and could not stand there at all (Livia's
   mistake).
2. **ποῦ ἐστι τὸ βιβλίον;**
3. **οὐκ οἶδα τίς ἐστιν.** — the question word keeps its accent inside the
   indirect question.
4. **διὰ τί βούλεται μανθάνειν;** — **βούλεται** + infinitive, Lesson 16.
5. **τίνα βλέπει ὁ διδάσκαλος;** — accusative **τίνα**. A pupil who writes
   **τίς** has asked *who sees the teacher?*, which is a different
   question with a different answer.
6. **γράφει τις ἐπιστολήν.** — no accent on **τις**, and it stands second.
   A pupil who writes **τίς γράφει ἐπιστολήν** has asked a question. Note
   that **γράφει**, accented on its second-last syllable, has no room to
   take an accent from the enclitic, so nothing visible happens to it;
   that is Livia's sentence exactly.
7. **πότε ἦλθον οἱ παῖδες;** — **ἦλθον** is both *I came* and *they came*;
   the subject settles it.
8. **τίνος ἐστὶν ἡ οἰκία;** — genitive, because the answer will be an
   owner.
9. **ἆρ᾽ οὐ καλή ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή;** — **ἆρ᾽ οὐ** expects *yes*. A pupil who
   writes **μὴ καλή ἐστιν** has asked *surely virtue is not fine?*, which
   expects *no*. Note **καλή ἐστιν**: a word accented on its last syllable
   keeps the acute before an enclitic instead of turning it to a grave.
10. **οὐκ οἶδα τί ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή.** — Socrates's own position, which you
    will read him stating in the Reading. Accept **τί ἐστιν ἀρετή**
    without the article, on the model of **τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια**.

::: {.voice source="Plato, *Meno* 70a; John 18:38"}
Two questions. One opens a dialogue; one ends an interrogation.

**Ἔχεις μοι εἰπεῖν, ὦ Σώκρατες, ἆρα διδακτὸν ἡ ἀρετή;**

- **ἔχεις** — you have; *can you* *(**ἔχω** + infinitive = to be able)*
- **μοι** — to me
- **εἰπεῖν** — to tell *(aorist infinitive, Lesson 16)*
- **ὦ Σώκρατες** — Socrates *(vocative, Lesson 4)*
- **ἆρα** — *(the question marker)*
- **διδακτόν** — teachable *(neuter)*
- **ἡ ἀρετή** — excellence, virtue

*Can you tell me, Socrates, whether virtue is a teachable thing?*

This is the first sentence of the *Meno*. A young man walks up to Socrates
and asks the question that the rest of the dialogue will fail to answer. And
look at the grammar: it is an **indirect question** — the real question,
*is virtue teachable?*, is tucked inside *can you tell me*. **ἆρα** marks
where the inner question begins.

**And look at διδακτόν.** It is neuter, and **ἀρετή** is feminine. They do
not agree, and that is not a mistake. A neuter adjective used as a predicate
puts the subject into a *category*: not *virtue is a teachable (feminine)
thing* but *virtue is-a-teachable-kind-of-thing*. Greek does this
constantly, and English can only do it clumsily.

Meno goes on: *or is it not teachable but acquired by practice? Or neither,
but comes to people by nature, or some other way?* Four questions in one
breath. Socrates's answer is that he does not even know what virtue *is*,
let alone whether it can be taught — and the dialogue spends the rest of
its length finding out whether that is honesty or a trick.

**τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια;**

You read this in Lesson 4, before you could parse a word of it. Now:
**τί**, neuter, *what?*, with its accent; **ἐστιν**; **ἀλήθεια** with no
article — *what is truth?*, not *what is the truth?* A question about the
thing itself, not about any particular fact.

Pilate asks it and leaves. The Gospel gives no answer. Marcus asked it this
afternoon and got none either, and was right to feel that the silence was a
kind of reply.

**Set them side by side.** Both are questions about an abstract noun —
**ἀρετή**, **ἀλήθεια**. Both have no answer in their text. One is asked by
a man who wants to know and will spend a day finding out that he does not.
One is asked by a man who does not wait. The grammar of the two questions is
nearly identical. What they *are* could not be more different, and the only
thing that tells you is what the asker did next.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The *Meno* opening follows the standard text
(see *Sources*) and should be checked against the edition in use; the
punctuation after **Σώκρατες** varies. John 18:38 is the text used in Lesson 4 and is stable.

**On Livia.** Her mistake is the lesson's centre and it is given to her on
purpose: the pupil who never errs makes the one error that is invisible on
the page and audible aloud. (Her sentence is real Greek — enclitic **τις**
after a paroxytone verb keeps the verb's accent unchanged — which is why
Theodoros can call it a true statement rather than a mistake in grammar.) Let her re-ask. The point is not that she
failed but that she heard the difference the moment it was named, which is
what the whole lesson is trying to make the others do.

**On Marcus.** His question is a good one and he knows it. The silence is
the honest answer and he should be allowed to see that it was honest rather
than evasive — the text he quoted gives none either.

ἐπὶ σοφίᾳ εἴληφεν Ἀλευαδῶν τε τοὺς πρώτους, ὧν ὁ σὸς ἐραστής ἐστιν
Ἀρίστιππος, καὶ τῶν ἄλλων Θετταλῶν. καὶ δὴ καὶ τοῦτο τὸ ἔθος ὑμᾶς
εἴθικεν, ἀφόβως τε καὶ μεγαλοπρεπῶς ἀποκρίνεσθαι ἐάν τίς τι ἔρηται, ὥσπερ
εἰκὸς τοὺς εἰδότας, ἅτε καὶ αὐτὸς παρέχων αὑτὸν ἐρωτᾶν τῶν Ἑλλήνων τῷ
βουλομένῳ ὅτι ἄν τις βούληται, καὶ οὐδενὶ ὅτῳ οὐκ ἀποκρινόμενος. ἐνθάδε
δέ, ὦ φίλε Μένων, τὸ ἐναντίον περιέστηκεν· ὥσπερ αὐχμός τις τῆς σοφίας
γέγονεν, καὶ κινδυνεύει ἐκ τῶνδε τῶν τόπων παρ᾽ ὑμᾶς οἴχεσθαι ἡ σοφία. εἰ
γοῦν τινα ἐθέλεις οὕτως ἐρέσθαι τῶν ἐνθάδε, οὐδεὶς ὅστις οὐ γελάσεται καὶ
ἐρεῖ· "Ὦ ξένε, κινδυνεύω σοι δοκεῖν μακάριός τις εἶναι — ἀρετὴν γοῦν εἴτε
διδακτὸν εἴθ᾽ ὅτῳ τρόπῳ παραγίγνεται εἰδέναι — ἐγὼ δὲ τοσοῦτον δέω εἴτε
διδακτὸν εἴτε μὴ διδακτὸν εἰδέναι, ὥστ᾽ οὐδὲ αὐτὸ ὅτι ποτ᾽ ἐστὶ τὸ παράπαν
ἀρετὴ τυγχάνω εἰδώς."

::: gloss
*The first sentence is glossed in The Ancient Voice.*

- **ἤ** — or
- **ἀσκητόν** — got by practice *(neuter, like **διδακτόν**)*
- **οὔτε … οὔτε** — neither … nor
- **μαθητόν** — learnable *(the root of **μανθάνω**)*
- **φύσει** — by nature *(dative)*
- **παραγίγνεται** — comes to, arrives in *(**γίγνομαι** with a prefix)*
- **ἄλλῳ τινὶ τρόπῳ** — in some other way
- **πρὸ τοῦ** — before this, formerly
- **ὦ Μένων** — Meno *(vocative)*
- **Θετταλοί** — Thessalians; Meno is one
- **εὐδόκιμοι ἦσαν** — were well thought of *(**ἦσαν**: they were)*
- **ἐν τοῖς Ἕλλησιν** — among the Greeks
- **ἐθαυμάζοντο** — were admired *(a passive; gloss it whole)*
- **ἐφ᾽ ἱππικῇ τε καὶ πλούτῳ** — for horsemanship and wealth
- **ὡς ἐμοὶ δοκεῖ** — as it seems to me
- **ἐπὶ σοφίᾳ** — for wisdom
- **οὐχ ἥκιστα** — not least
- **οἱ τοῦ σοῦ ἑταίρου Ἀριστίππου πολῖται Λαρισαῖοι** — the fellow-citizens
  of your friend Aristippus, the people of Larisa
- **τούτου … αἴτιος** — responsible for this
- **Γοργίας** — Gorgias, the famous teacher of speaking
- **ἀφικόμενος** — having arrived *(an aorist participle; gloss it whole)*
- **ἐραστὰς … εἴληφεν** — has won lovers, admirers *(**εἴληφεν**: has taken,
  a perfect of **λαμβάνω**)*
- **Ἀλευαδῶν τε τοὺς πρώτους** — the first men of the Aleuadae, the ruling
  family
- **ὧν ὁ σὸς ἐραστής** — of whom your admirer *(**σός**: your)*
- **καὶ δὴ καί** — and in particular
- **τοῦτο τὸ ἔθος ὑμᾶς εἴθικεν** — has given you this habit
- **ἀφόβως τε καὶ μεγαλοπρεπῶς** — fearlessly and grandly
- **ἐάν τίς τι ἔρηται** — if anyone asks anything *(**τίς** is accented only
  because **τι** follows it — see the exercise)*
- **ὥσπερ εἰκὸς τοὺς εἰδότας** — as is natural for those who know
- **ἅτε … παρέχων αὑτὸν ἐρωτᾶν** — inasmuch as he offers himself to be
  questioned
- **τῶν Ἑλλήνων τῷ βουλομένῳ** — to any Greek who wishes
- **ὅτι ἄν τις βούληται** — whatever anyone wishes *(**ὅτι** here is the
  relative *whatever*, not *that*)*
- **οὐδενὶ ὅτῳ οὐκ ἀποκρινόμενος** — refusing an answer to no one
  *(**οὐδενί**, dative of **οὐδείς, οὐδεμία, οὐδέν**, genitive **οὐδενός**:
  it is **οὐδὲ εἷς**, not even one, and declines like *one*, not like
  **τίς**)*
- **ἐνθάδε** — here
- **τὸ ἐναντίον περιέστηκεν** — the opposite has come about
- **αὐχμός τις … γέγονεν** — a kind of drought has set in
- **κινδυνεύει … οἴχεσθαι** — is in danger of having gone away
- **ἐκ τῶνδε τῶν τόπων παρ᾽ ὑμᾶς** — from these parts to you
- **εἰ γοῦν … ἐθέλεις** — if, at any rate, you are willing
- **οὕτως ἐρέσθαι** — to ask in this way *(aorist infinitive of **ἐρωτάω**)*
- **οὐδεὶς ὅστις οὐ** — there is no one who will not
- **γελάσεται καὶ ἐρεῖ** — will laugh and will say *(two futures)*
- **ὦ ξένε** — stranger *(vocative)*
- **κινδυνεύω σοι δοκεῖν** — I am in danger of seeming to you; I dare say I
  seem to you
- **μακάριός τις εἶναι** — to be a fortunate man
- **γοῦν** — at any rate
- **εἴτε … εἴτε** — whether … or
- **ὅτῳ τρόπῳ** — in whatever way
- **εἰδέναι** — to know *(the infinitive of **οἶδα**)*
- **τοσοῦτον δέω … ὥστε** — I am so far from … that
- **οὐδὲ αὐτό** — not even this itself
- **ὅτι ποτ᾽ ἐστί** — what on earth it is *(**ὅτι** again the relative;
  **ποτέ** adds *on earth*)*
- **τὸ παράπαν** — at all
- **τυγχάνω εἰδώς** — I happen to know *(**εἰδώς**: knowing, the participle
  of **οἶδα**)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both versions aloud before you answer anything.

1. Who asks the first question, and what is it about? Which word in it
   tells you that it is a question?
2. Meno offers four possibilities for how virtue comes to people. Find
   them in the original. What connector joins them?
3. In the adapted version, why are the Thessalians wise? Which **γάρ**
   gives the reason, and who is named?
4. **ἐάν τίς τι ἔρηται.** **τίς** has an accent. Is it *who?* Say what tells
   you.
5. Find **οὐδείς** in both versions. What does the original add to it?
6. In the adapted version, the Athenian is asked **ἆρα διδακτόν ἐστιν ἡ
   ἀρετή;** What is his answer, and what is his reason for it? Which word
   introduces the reason?
7. **οὐκ οἶδα τί ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή.** Is **τί** a question word here? What kind
   of question is this?
8. Find the sentence in the original that says the same thing as the
   adapted Athenian's **οὐδὲ γὰρ οἶδα τί ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή**. It is longer.
   Which words in it mean *what it is*?
9. The adapted version ends **καὶ ἐγώ, ὦ Μένων, οὐκ οἶδα.** The original
   does not say this in so many words. Where does Socrates say it?
10. Translate the last sentence of the adapted version. Then say what it
    does to Meno's question.
:::

::: answers
1. Meno; whether virtue is teachable. **ἆρα**, which marks the question
   and sits where the inner question begins — the Voice's point, not
   repeated here.
2. Teachable (**διδακτόν**); got by practice (**ἀσκητόν**); by nature
   (**φύσει**); or in some other way (**ἄλλῳ τινὶ τρόπῳ**). **ἤ**, *or*,
   joins them, three times.
3. Because Gorgias came to their city and taught them to answer: **Γοργίας
   γὰρ ἦλθεν … καὶ ἐδίδαξεν**. The **γάρ** is the second word of its
   clause, as it must be (Lesson 17).
4. No — it is *anyone*. The lesson's note said that even *someone* takes
   an accent before another enclitic, and **τι** is one. The context
   settles it too: *if who asks what?* is not a sentence. This is the one
   place in the passage where the accent does *not* ask, and a pupil who
   spots that has understood the lesson rather than the rule.
5. Adapted: **οὐδείς ἐστι σοφός**, *no one is wise*. Original: **οὐδεὶς
   ὅστις οὐ γελάσεται**, *there is no one who will not laugh* — a double
   negative that comes out positive: *everyone will laugh*.
6. *Teachable or not teachable, I do not know; for I do not even know what
   virtue is.* **γάρ** introduces the reason (**οὐδὲ γὰρ οἶδα**), and the reason
   is a real one: you cannot say whether a thing can be taught before you
   know what it is.
7. Yes, with its accent; and it is an indirect question, folded inside
   **οὐκ οἶδα** — the construction from the New Greek. Not *I do not know
   something is virtue*; the accent forbids that.
8. **ὥστ᾽ οὐδὲ αὐτὸ ὅτι ποτ᾽ ἐστὶ τὸ παράπαν ἀρετὴ τυγχάνω εἰδώς.** The
   words for *what it is* are **ὅτι ποτ᾽ ἐστί** — with **ὅτι** the relative
   *what(ever)*, which the gloss flags, and **ποτέ** turning it into *what
   on earth*.
9. He does not say it of himself at all in this passage; he puts it in the
   mouth of an imaginary Athenian, *anyone here*. The adapted version makes
   him own it. Plato's Socrates does own it a few lines later, at 71b,
   which is why the adaptation is fair — but a pupil who notices that the
   original is more careful than the adaptation has read both.
10. *And I, Meno, do not know.* It turns the question round: Meno asked
    whether virtue can be taught, and Socrates answers by asking what
    virtue is — a prior question that, it will turn out, nobody in the
    dialogue can answer either. The rest of the *Meno* is that question.
:::
