# The Translator's Dilemma

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Two tablets, one sentence, and Theodoros said that this time both
translations were defensible and only one was right.

**ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν.**

Julia had written *the kingdom of God is within you.*

Livia had written *the kingdom of God is among you.*

"Defend them."

Julia went first, and her defence was what everyone expected from Julia:
exact and complete. "**ἐντός** means *inside*. With a genitive, *inside of*.
**ὑμῶν** is *you*, plural, genitive. *Inside of you.* The word means what
it means. I have looked it up."

"Livia."

"The word means what it means," Livia agreed. "And he is talking to
Pharisees."

There was a pause while the room worked out why that mattered.

"The verse before," said Livia. "He has been asked a question — *when is
the kingdom coming?* — and the people asking are the Pharisees, and the
whole of the rest of the Gospel is a quarrel with them. He is not telling
the Pharisees that the kingdom of God is inside *them*. He is telling them
it is already here, in their midst, and they have not seen it. **ἐντὸς
ὑμῶν** can mean *in your midst*. With a plural *you*, it very often does.
And the sentence before it tells you which."

Julia looked at her tablet.

"Nothing in my sentence is wrong," she said.

"No," said Theodoros. "Nothing in your sentence is wrong. The grammar is
right. The lexicon is right. The translation is wrong, and the reason is
not in the sentence. It is in the one before it, and in who was in the
room when it was said." He sat down. "This is the lesson where I tell you
that everything you have learned this year is necessary and none of it is
sufficient. You have the grammar. Today you find out what the grammar
cannot do."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Where grammar stops

Lesson 18 gave you a method: endings, article, aspect, connectors, and then
the fifth question. That method will tell you which translations are
*possible*. It will eliminate the ones the Greek forbids.

It will not, by itself, choose between the ones the Greek allows. For that
you need three things that are not grammar.

**One. Range.** A Greek word covers a region (Lesson 19). When the region is
wider than any English word's, the translator must choose a corner, and the
grammar does not say which. **ἐντός** covers *inside* and *in the midst
of*. Both are the word.

**Two. Context.** What came before, who is speaking, to whom, in what
situation. The sentence before **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν** tells you the audience, and
the audience decides the corner.

**Three. Usage.** How the word is *actually used* elsewhere — by this
author, in this period, in this kind of text. A lexicon gives you the
range; usage tells you where in the range a writer like this one tends to
land.

A translation can be grammatically perfect and fail on any of the three.
Julia's failed on the second. Marcus's in Lesson 18 failed on none of them
— it failed on the grammar — and that is the difference between a bad
translation and a *wrong* one. A bad translation can be shown wrong from
the sentence. A wrong one needs the page.

## Ambiguity is not always a problem

Sometimes the Greek is open on purpose. **καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν**
(John 1:5) — *and the darkness did not* … what? **καταλαμβάνω** is *seize*,
*overtake*, *grasp*, and from *grasp* it comes to mean *understand*. *The
darkness did not overcome it* and *the darkness did not comprehend it* are
both the verb, and a great many readers have thought the writer wanted
both at once.

A translator who picks one has made a choice. A translator who finds an
English verb that keeps both — *the darkness has not mastered it* — has
done better, and such verbs are rare. The honest thing, often, is to pick
one and say in a note that the other was there.

## What a translator is actually choosing between

Not between right and wrong, mostly. Between *losses*. Every translation of
a sentence with range in it loses something; the translator's job is to
choose which loss the reader can best afford.

*Within you* loses the audience. *Among you* loses the inwardness that
**ἐντός** usually carries. *Overcome* loses the understanding; *comprehend*
loses the struggle. You cannot have everything. You can know what you gave
up, and say so.

::: note
**Marcus has been doing this on purpose all year.** Every one of his
technically-possible-and-absurd translations was a demonstration that the
grammar permits more than the sense does. He was being annoying. He was
also, every time, right about the grammar — and the lesson he was teaching
without meaning to is that *the grammar permits it* is never the end of
the argument.
:::

::: latinbridge
**The Latin kept the ambiguity and you never noticed.** *Regnum Dei intra
vos est.* *Intra vos* is *within you* and *among you* with exactly the same
openness as the Greek, and the Latin translators, who could have chosen,
did not. Every Latin reader has been making Julia's choice or Livia's
silently for two hundred years, and most of them never knew there was one.

That is the other thing a translation can do: pass the dilemma on intact.
It is sometimes the most faithful choice, and it is always the least
helpful.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἐντός** — inside; in the midst of *(with the genitive)*
- **ἰδού** — behold, look *(an interjection, from **εἶδον**)*
- **τὸ φῶς** — light *(genitive **φωτός**)*
- **ἡ σκοτία** — darkness *(Koine; Attic usually **ὁ σκότος**)*
- **ὁ κόσμος** — order; ornament; the world *(three corners of one field)*
- **ἡ δόξα** — opinion; reputation; glory *(the same word, moving)*
- **καταλαμβάνω** — I seize, overtake; grasp, understand *(aorist
  **κατέλαβον**)*

::: note
**Words whose range is wider in Greek than in English.** You now have most
of them. **λόγος** (Lesson 19): word, account, argument, reason. **ψυχή**
(Lesson 1): breath, life, soul, self. **ἀρχή** (Lesson 6): beginning,
origin, rule. **χάρις** (Lesson 19): charm, favour, thanks, grace.
**κόσμος**: order, adornment, world. **δόξα**: what seems so, reputation,
glory. **πνεῦμα** (Lesson 21): breath, wind, spirit.

Every one of these, when you meet it, is a dilemma. Every one of them is
a word English has borrowed — *logic*, *psyche*, *archaic*, *charisma*,
*cosmos*, *orthodox*, *pneumatic* — and kept one corner of.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros said that for every sentence he wanted two translations and a
reason for preferring one — and that *the dictionary says so* was not a
reason, since the dictionary said both.

::: {.exercise title="Two renderings, one reason"}
1. **ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω.** *(John 1:10; **ἔγνω** = knew, recognised)*
2. **ἡ δόξα τοῦ ἀνδρὸς μεγάλη ἦν.**
3. **καὶ τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν.**
   *(John 1:5; **φαίνει** = shines)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The world did not know him* / *the world did not recognise him.*
   **κόσμος** here is the world of people, not the ordered universe; the
   context (he was in the world, the world was made through him) settles
   it. **ἔγνω** is the aorist of **γιγνώσκω**: a recognition that did not
   happen. Prefer *recognise* if the class can say why — it keeps the
   *coming-to-know* in the verb.
2. *The man's reputation was great* / *the man's glory was great.* — a
   composed sentence, with no context, and so no way to decide. The right
   answer is *I cannot tell without the page*, and a pupil who says so has
   understood the lesson. Press one who chooses confidently.
3. *And the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not
   overcome it* / *… did not comprehend it.* Both are the verb. Accept
   either with the other acknowledged; give most credit to a pupil who
   finds an English word that holds both, or who says that the Greek may
   mean both and the English cannot.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
**ἐπερωτηθεὶς δὲ ὑπὸ τῶν Φαρισαίων πότε ἔρχεται ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ …**
*(Luke 17:20, the verse before the lesson's sentence. **ἐπερωτηθείς** =
having been asked; **ὑπό** + genitive = by.)*

1. Who asked the question?
2. What did they ask? *(There is a question word from Lesson 25 in it.)*
3. Now re-read **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν**. Who is **ὑμῶν**? What does that do to
   Julia's translation?
:::

::: answers
1. The Pharisees — **ὑπὸ τῶν Φαρισαίων**, the agent after a passive
   participle.
2. *When is the kingdom of God coming?* — **πότε** from Lesson 25, in an
   indirect question.
3. **ὑμῶν** is the Pharisees. Julia's *within you* makes the sentence say
   the kingdom of God is inside the people the Gospel treats as its
   opponents. That is not impossible, but it needs an argument, and
   Livia's reading needs none. This is what *context decides* looks like
   in practice: not a feeling about the sentence, but a fact about the
   previous one.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Behold, the kingdom of God is among you.
2. The light shines in the darkness.
3. The glory of God was great.
4. I do not know what he means. *(literally, "what he is saying")*
:::

::: answers
1. **ἰδοὺ ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν.** — and the class should
   notice that the Greek they have just written is *exactly as ambiguous*
   as the Greek they argued about. Writing it does not settle it.
2. **τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει.**
3. **ἡ δόξα τοῦ θεοῦ μεγάλη ἦν.** — and here **δόξα** is *glory*, because
   nobody says God's *reputation* was great; the genitive has decided the
   corner. Ask them to say so.
4. **οὐκ οἶδα τί λέγει.** — Lesson 25; and a fair description of a
   translator's condition most of the time.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Luke 17:20–21"}
The sentence, with the verse that decides it.

**ἐπερωτηθεὶς δὲ ὑπὸ τῶν Φαρισαίων πότε ἔρχεται ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ
ἀπεκρίθη αὐτοῖς καὶ εἶπεν· οὐκ ἔρχεται ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ μετὰ
παρατηρήσεως, οὐδὲ ἐροῦσιν· ἰδοὺ ὧδε ἤ· ἐκεῖ, ἰδοὺ γὰρ ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ
ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν.**

- **ἐπερωτηθείς** — having been asked *(aorist passive participle)*
- **ὑπὸ τῶν Φαρισαίων** — by the Pharisees
- **πότε ἔρχεται** — when is coming *(indirect question, Lesson 25)*
- **ἀπεκρίθη … καὶ εἶπεν** — he answered and said *(**ἀποκρίνομαι**,
  Lesson 25)*
- **οὐ … μετὰ παρατηρήσεως** — not with observation; not by watching for
  it
- **οὐδὲ ἐροῦσιν** — nor will they say
- **ἰδοὺ ὧδε ἤ· ἐκεῖ** — "look, here!" or "there!"
- **ἰδοὺ γάρ** — for look *(**γάρ**: here comes the reason)*
- **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν ἐστιν** — is inside you / is in your midst

*Having been asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God was coming, he
answered them and said: "The kingdom of God does not come by watching for
it, nor will they say 'look, here!' or 'there!' — for look, the kingdom of
God is* **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν**."

**Now the argument, laid out fairly.**

*For "within you":* **ἐντός** means *inside*; that is its first sense and
its commonest. The kingdom that does not come by watching, that cannot be
pointed at, is a kingdom of the inner life. Many careful readers across
many centuries have taken it so.

*For "among you":* the hearers are the Pharisees; the Gospel does not
elsewhere locate the kingdom inside them. **ἐντός** with a plural can mean
*in the midst of*. The point of *not here, not there* is that it is already
present, unrecognised, in the person speaking. Most modern translators take
it so.

**What the grammar settles:** nothing. Both readings are the word. **What
the page settles:** a good deal, and most of it is on Livia's side. **What
remains open:** the writer may have meant the reader to feel both — a
kingdom that is at once in your midst and not outside you — and a
translator who chooses one has, either way, closed something the Greek left
open.

That is the dilemma. It does not go away with more Greek. It goes away with
a decision, and the decision is yours, and you owe the reader an account of
it.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The passage follows the standard Greek New
Testament (see *Sources*); check verse 21 in particular, where the
punctuation after **ἐροῦσιν** and the form **ἤ** vary between editions.

**On adjudicating.** The lesson gives Livia the stronger argument *on this
evidence* and says so, and also says that the question is not closed. Keep
both. The failure modes are a class that concludes *within you* is simply
an error (it is not; it has a long and serious history) and a class that
concludes the question cannot be answered (it can be argued, and Livia
argues it). What the lesson wants is a class that can say which reading
the context favours and why, and can say what is lost by choosing it.

**On Julia.** Her translation is the lexicon's default and she defends it
exactly as a careful pupil should. She is not wrong about anything in her
sentence. Do not let the class treat her as having blundered; she has
done what Lesson 18 taught and discovered that Lesson 18 was not the end.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Marcus has spent the year producing translations that were technically
   possible and absurd. He was being annoying on purpose. When a real
   translator does it by accident, how would anyone know?
2. The Latin kept the ambiguity. Was that faithfulness or
   abdication? Is there a difference?
3. Every translation loses something. Is a translation that *knows* what
   it lost better than one that does not — even if the two are word for
   word the same?
:::

Julia asked, at the end, whether she should have looked at the verse
before.

"Yes," said Theodoros.

"I was given the sentence. I translated the sentence."

"You were given the sentence as a test of whether you would ask for the
page. Livia asked for the page." He paused. "It is the only thing she did
that you did not, and it is the thing that will matter most for the rest of
your life, in Greek and out of it. Nothing you are ever handed is the whole
of it. Ask for the page."
