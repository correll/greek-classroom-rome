# The Great Reading

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Three questions," said Theodoros. "Each of you. About your own passage,
which you have chosen, and which you will read to the courtyard next week.
I will answer three and no more, so decide what you most need to know
before you ask the first one."

The passages had been chosen from a list. Julia had taken the opening of
the *Odyssey*, on the grounds that she had been saying for fourteen lessons
that Homer had no article and intended to prove it in public. Theodosius had
the Beatitudes. Sabina had the Samaritan. Quintus, inevitably, had Socrates.
Livia had the kingdom **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν** and the verse before it. Aelia had the
Psalm. Felix had Apion's letter, which he said was the only text in the
book written by somebody he would have liked.

Marcus had not told anyone what he had chosen.

The three questions turned out to be the hardest part. Felix used his first
to ask whether the cat could attend and was told that it counted. Quintus
spent twenty minutes composing a question so precise it answered itself, and
then did not need to ask it. Livia asked one question, wrote the answer
down, and said she would keep the other two in case.

Marcus asked all three in the first five minutes, all about the same two
words, and all of them good. Theodoros answered them without comment and
then, when Marcus had gone back to his tablet, watched him for a while.

"He is preparing," said Julia, who had noticed too.

"He is."

"He has never prepared anything."

"No," said Theodoros. "I wonder what he has chosen."
:::

## Reading something you have not seen

Everything in this course so far has been scaffolded: glossed, prepared,
set beside something you knew. The passage you read next week will not be.
You need a *method* — a way of reading an unfamiliar sentence that does not
depend on hope.

Here it is. Do it in this order and do not skip steps.

**One. Read it aloud.** All of it, before you look anything up. You will
hear things — the shape of the clauses, the **καί**s and **δέ**s, a word
you know — that your eye skates over. Lesson 25 taught you the accent is
audible. So is everything else.

**Two. Mark the verbs.** Every finite verb — the ones with person endings.
Underline them. They are the spine. A sentence has as many clauses as it
has finite verbs, and now you know how many pieces you are dealing with.

**Three. Find each verb's subject.** Nominative noun with the article, or
the ending on the verb, or a pronoun. Lesson 3 and Lesson 5. Write *who* next
to each verb.

**Four. Mark the participles and infinitives.** They hang from the verbs.
Draw a line from each to the verb it belongs to. Lesson 22's choreography.

**Five. Read the connectors.** **γάρ**, **οὖν**, **δέ**, **ἀλλά**, **ὅτι**,
**ἵνα**. They tell you how the pieces are joined — reason, conclusion,
contrast, report, purpose.

**Six. Now, and only now, look up what is left.** You will find there is
less of it than you feared. Most of the sentence has already declared
itself.

**Seven. Translate. Then ask the fifth question.** *What would have to be
true in the Greek for this to be wrong?* If you cannot answer, go back to
step two.

## Using a lexicon

A lexicon lists words in their *dictionary form*: nouns in the nominative
singular with the article and the genitive ending, verbs in the first person
singular present. It does not list **ἦλθεν**. It lists **ἔρχομαι**.

So before you can look a word up, you have to undress it.

- **Strip the ending.** **λόγου** → **λόγ-** → **λόγος**. **ἀνθρώποις** →
  **ἄνθρωπ-** → **ἄνθρωπος**.
- **Strip the augment.** **ἔγραψε** → **γραψ-** → the aorist stem of
  **γράφω**. **ἦλθεν** → **ἐλθ-** → and here you must simply *know* that
  **ἐλθ-** belongs to **ἔρχομαι**, because nothing on the page will tell
  you. The irregular aorists of Lesson 14 are the price of reading.
- **Strip the participle ending.** **ἰδών** → **ἰδ-** → the aorist stem of
  **ὁράω**. Same problem, same price.

::: note
**The forms that will not undress.** A short list of stems that belong to
verbs they do not resemble. Learn these; they cannot be looked up.

| Form you meet | Belongs to | Means |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἦλθον, ἐλθών, ἐλθεῖν** | **ἔρχομαι** | come, go |
| **εἶπον, εἰπών, εἰπεῖν** | **λέγω** | say |
| **εἶδον, ἰδών, ἰδεῖν** | **ὁράω** | see |
| **ἔγνων, γνούς, γνῶναι** | **γιγνώσκω** | know |
| **ἐγένετο, γενόμενος** | **γίγνομαι** | become, happen |
| **ἔλαβον, λαβών, λαβεῖν** | **λαμβάνω** | take |
| **ἦν** | **εἰμί** | be |
:::

## What you may skip

Not everything in a sentence needs to be construed to be understood. A
place-name is a place-name. A string of genitives after **ὑπό** is *by
somebody*. A verb form you cannot parse but whose stem you recognise —
**παρακληθήσονται** from **παρακαλέω** — can be read as *something about
comforting, future, passive* and the sentence will usually still stand.

Reading is not parsing every word. It is parsing enough words to know what
the others must be doing.

::: latinbridge
**This is the method you were taught for an unseen in Latin**, if you were
taught one, and it is the same method because it is the method. Verbs
first, subjects second, connectives third, dictionary last. The only
difference is that the Greek dictionary form of a verb is first person
singular — **λέγω**, *I say* — where the Latin is also first person
singular — *dīcō* — and so there is no difference.

If you were never taught an unseen method in Latin, you now have one, and
it will work there too.
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros gave them one sentence none of them had seen and made them do the
seven steps on it, aloud, in turn.

::: {.exercise title="The method, applied"}
**ἐγώ εἰμι τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου· ὁ ἀκολουθῶν ἐμοὶ οὐ μὴ περιπατήσῃ ἐν τῇ
σκοτίᾳ, ἀλλ᾽ ἕξει τὸ φῶς τῆς ζωῆς.** *(John 8:12)*

1. Step two: find the finite verbs. How many clauses?
2. Step three: the subject of each.
3. Step four: any participles? What do they hang from?
4. Step five: the connectors, and what each joins.
5. Step six: what is left to look up? *(**ἀκολουθέω** = I follow; **οὐ μὴ**
   + a verb = emphatically "never"; **περιπατέω** = I walk about.)*
6. Translate.
:::

::: answers
1. **εἰμι**, **περιπατήσῃ**, **ἕξει** — three verbs, three clauses. (The
   second is a form the class has not met; they can see it is a verb from
   its position after **οὐ μή** and its ending.)
2. **ἐγώ** for the first; **ὁ ἀκολουθῶν**, *the one following*, for the
   second and third.
3. **ἀκολουθῶν**, present participle with the article — Lesson 22's *the
   person who* — serving as the subject of the second and third clauses.
   **ἐμοί** is its object: *following me*.
4. **·** (the raised point) separates the first clause from the rest;
   **ἀλλά** sets the third clause against the second — *not darkness, but
   light*.
5. **ἀκολουθῶν**, **περιπατήσῃ**, and perhaps **κόσμου** and **σκοτίᾳ** if
   Lesson 27 has faded. Everything else is owned: **ἐγώ, εἰμι, τὸ φῶς, τοῦ,
   ὁ, ἐμοί, οὐ, μή, ἐν, τῇ, ἀλλά, ἕξει, τῆς ζωῆς**. Seventeen of
   twenty-one words — nineteen, if Lesson 27 has held.
6. *I am the light of the world; the one who follows me will never walk in
   the darkness, but will have the light of life.*

The point to make at the end is the count in step five. A sentence they had
never seen was four-fifths theirs before they opened a lexicon. That is what
twenty-eight lessons have bought.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Choose your three"}
Before you ask Theodoros anything, write down the three questions you would
ask about your passage, and beside each one say *why that one* — what you
could not work out alone.
:::

::: answers
No key. Read what they wrote and look at the *why* column. A pupil whose
three questions are all vocabulary has not used the method; a pupil whose
three questions are all about connectors or aspect has. Say so, and let
them revise the list before they spend it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I am the light of the world.
2. The one who follows the teacher learns.
3. We do not know what the word means.
4. Read the passage aloud! *(to several people; use **ἀναγίγνωσκε**, "read",
   and **ἡ γραφή** from Lesson 3 for "passage")*
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐγώ εἰμι τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου.** — the Gospel's own words. The emphatic
   **ἐγώ**, written out, is the Koine of Lesson 23 and the point of the
   sentence.
2. **ὁ ἀκολουθῶν τῷ διδασκάλῳ μανθάνει.** — **ἀκολουθέω** takes the dative;
   give it. Article + participle as subject.
3. **οὐκ οἴδαμεν τί ὁ λόγος λέγει.** — or **τί λέγει ὁ λόγος**; indirect
   question, Lesson 25. **οἴδαμεν** is the Koine *we know*; Attic has
   **ἴσμεν** and either is acceptable if given.
4. **ἀναγιγνώσκετε τὴν γραφήν.** — present imperative plural: the reading
   is an activity. **ἀνάγνωτε**, the aorist, would be *read it through*,
   once; either is defensible and the choice is worth a sentence.
:::
:::

There is no single text this week. There is a list, and every passage on it
is in this book, and you have read every one of them with help. Next week
you read one of them without.

| | Passage | Lesson | Greek | What it tests |
|:--|:--|:--:|:--|:--|
| Homer | *Odyssey* 1.1 | 15 | Homeric | reading without the article |
| Homer | *Odyssey* 9.366–367 | 16 | Homeric | case endings carrying a trick |
| Protagoras | *Man the measure* | 12 | Attic | a genitive first; a predicate without an article |
| Plato | *Apology* 38a | 17 | Attic | a sentence with no verb and a **δέ** that matters |
| Plato | *Meno* 70a | 25 | Attic | an indirect question; a neuter predicate |
| The Psalms | Psalm 22 (LXX) | 20 | Septuagint | Hebrew showing through Greek |
| Matthew | The Beatitudes | 21 | Koine | the blessing formula; **ὅτι** as *because* |
| Luke | The Samaritan | 22 | Koine | participles carrying a story |
| Luke | The kingdom **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν** | 27 | Koine | a dilemma the grammar cannot settle |
| John | 1:1, 1:14, 1:5 | 6, 13, 14, 27 | Koine | the article; the imperfect; a verb with two meanings |
| John | 8:12 | 29 | Koine | reading unseen, by the method |
| Apion | A letter home | 28 | Koine | the formulas; a real person's Greek |

**How to choose.** Not the shortest. Not the one you like best. The one
whose *what it tests* is the thing you are least sure of — because that is
the one where preparing it will teach you something, and the one where
standing up and reading it will be an achievement rather than a
performance.

**How to prepare.** The seven steps, on your own, with the lexicon, before
you spend a single question. Then the four-column list. Then the three
questions — and spend them on what the method could not give you.

**What you will be asked, after you read.** Three things, which are the
three things this course has asked of every sentence since Lesson 1: what
it says; what one feature of its grammar is doing; and what, having read
it in Greek, you now know that you did not.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** John 8:12 in the exercise follows the standard
text (see *Sources*); check it against the edition in use.

**On the list.** It is a list of passages already in the book, by design.
The Great Reading is not a test of range but of independence: can a pupil
take a text they have met with scaffolding and stand it up without? Any
passage from the course may be added; passages from outside it should not
be, at this stage, unless a pupil asks for one and you have checked it.

**On the three questions.** The limit is the lesson. A pupil who could ask
unlimited questions would ask about vocabulary; a pupil who may ask three
has to decide what they cannot work out, which means working out what they
can. Hold the limit. Felix's cat question counts.

**On Marcus.** He has chosen, and he has not said what, and the scene
leaves it so. Lesson 30 answers it. If a pupil in your own class is doing
something similar — preparing, for the first time, and not saying so — the
right response is Theodoros's: notice, say nothing, wait.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. You were allowed three questions. Which three did you choose, and what
   does that say about where your Greek is weakest?
2. A sentence you had never seen was four-fifths yours before you opened
   the lexicon. What does that number say about what learning a language
   actually is?
3. Theodoros answers three questions and no more. Is a limit on help a
   kindness or a cruelty? Would unlimited help have taught you more?
:::

Felix asked, as his second question, whether Apion had ever got home.

"We do not know," said Theodoros. "The letter is all there is. He joined
the fleet, he wrote to his father, somebody kept the letter. Whether he saw
Egypt again, nobody can tell you."

::: teacheronly
A second letter from the same man does in fact survive — BGU II 632, to his
sister Sabina, written years later under his Roman name Antonius Maximus,
with a wife and children. It shows he lived and stayed in the service; it
does not say he went home. Theodoros, who could not have had either
papyrus, is allowed his answer.
:::

Felix considered this.

"I will read it," he said, "as if he did."

"That is not in the Greek."

"No," said Felix. "But he would have wanted it there, and I have one
question left, and I am not going to waste it finding out."
