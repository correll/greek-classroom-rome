# Commands, Advice, and Persuasion

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Marcus had asked for ten minutes and been given five, and he used them.

The argument, delivered standing and in Greek, ran roughly as follows.
Learning takes place in the soul. The soul is not in the house. Therefore
nothing that takes place in the house — specifically, the copying of
paradigms by lamplight — is learning. **οὐ δεῖ οὖν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν ἐν
τῷ οἴκῳ**: it is therefore not necessary for the pupils to write at home.
He sat down to something close to applause.

"Grammar first," said Theodoros. "Then the argument. Separately. Julia."

"The grammar is perfect," said Julia, and sounded as if it cost her. "**οὐ
δεῖ** with the accusative and infinitive. **οὖν** in second place. The
**γάρ** clauses all give reasons. He used **μέν … δέ** twice and both times
correctly. I cannot find anything."

"Nor can I. It is the best Greek anyone in this room has produced this
year, and I include myself." He turned. "Now the argument. Livia."

Livia had been waiting.

"The **γάρ** gives a reason," she said. "Every **γάρ** clause gives a reason.
The reasons are not reasons. *The soul is not in the house* — the soul is
wherever the pupil is, and the pupil is in the house. He has used the
connector that promises evidence and put a sentence after it that is not
evidence. Every one of his **γάρ**s is a false promise, in flawless Greek."

Marcus opened his mouth.

"She is right," said Theodoros, before he could. "And so are you, Marcus,
about one thing: you have shown that an argument can be grammatically
perfect and still wrong, which is a lesson I have been trying to teach since
Lesson 18, and you have taught it better by *being* the example. For that
you have my thanks." He paused. "The homework stands. **γράφετε.**"

It was, the class agreed afterwards, the best five minutes Marcus had ever
had, and he had lost.
:::

## The imperative

You have been reading commands since the first week — **γνῶθι**, **ἄειδε**,
**ἔννεπε**, **πορεύου**, **ποίει** — without the form being named. The
**imperative** is the mood of telling someone to do something, and it comes
in present and aorist, like everything else.

| | 2nd sg. | 2nd pl. | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Present** | **λέγε** | **λέγετε** | be saying; keep saying |
| | **ἄκουε** | **ἀκούετε** | be listening |
| **First aorist** | **γράψον** | **γράψατε** | write (now, once) |
| | **ἄκουσον** | **ἀκούσατε** | listen (now) |
| **Second aorist** | **εἰπέ** | **εἴπετε** | say |
| | **ἐλθέ** | **ἔλθετε** | come |
| | **μάθε** | **μάθετε** | learn |

The second-person plural of the present looks exactly like the indicative
— **λέγετε** is both *you say* and *say!* — and context decides. (**ἀγαπᾶτε
τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν** in Lesson 11 was a command, and you read it as one
without knowing why.)

## Aspect, for the last time

A present imperative asks for an *activity* — ongoing, repeated, a habit, a
rule of life. An aorist imperative asks for a *deed* — once, now, complete.

- **ἄκουε** — *listen* as a habit; be a listener.
- **ἄκουσον** — *listen!* — to this, now.
- **πορεύου καὶ σὺ ποίει ὁμοίως** — both present. *Go on your way and keep
  doing likewise.* Not an errand; a way to live.
- **εἰπέ μοι** — aorist. *Tell me.* One answer, now.

You have met this difference in the indicative (Lesson 14), the infinitive
(Lesson 16) and the participle (Lesson 22). The imperative is the fourth
and, for this course, the last place it lives, and it is the place where it matters most in daily
speech, because most of what people say to each other is commands.

## Saying *don't*

Greek forbids with **μή**, never with **οὐ**.

**μή** + present imperative: *stop doing that* or *don't be the kind of
person who does that*. **μὴ λέγε** — *don't say* (as a habit; or, stop).
**μὴ φοβοῦ** — *do not be afraid*, which the Gospels say to people who
already are.

For *don't* as a single act — *don't say that one thing* — Greek uses **μή**
with a form called the aorist subjunctive, which you have not learned.
Recognise **μή** + a verb as a prohibition and let the exact form wait.

::: note
**This is why μηδὲν ἄγαν has μηδέν and not οὐδέν.** You may have wondered in Lesson 2
why the maxim used the **μή** word for *nothing*. The answer is that it is
a command — *[do] nothing in excess* — and commands take the **μή** family.
There is no verb in the maxim; the **μ-** tells you one is understood.
:::

## Obligation

**δεῖ** — *it is necessary* — and **χρή** — *one ought* — are impersonal.
They have no subject in the ordinary sense; the person who must do the
thing goes in the accusative, and the thing to be done is an infinitive.

**δεῖ σε μανθάνειν.** — *It is necessary for you to learn.* *You must learn.*
**χρὴ τοὺς παῖδας ἀκούειν.** — *The children ought to listen.*

It is the construction of Lesson 16 with a one-word verb on the front.
Marcus's whole argument rested on **οὐ δεῖ** + accusative + infinitive, and
he built it correctly.

## Telling, advising, persuading

Greek keeps these apart with its verbs, not its moods.

- **κελεύω** — I order. The imperative's verb.
- **συμβουλεύω** — I advise. Takes the dative: advice is given *to*.
- **πείθω** — I persuade. And **πείθομαι**, the middle, is *I am persuaded*
  — which is to say, *I obey*. In Greek, to obey is to have been
  convinced.

That last one is worth a moment. The ordinary classical word for obeying
is simply the middle of the word for persuading. A Greek who **πείθεται**
has, by the grammar, agreed.

::: latinbridge
**Latin's imperative is simpler and says less.** *Dic*, *audi*, *scribe* —
one form, no aspect. Latin cannot tell you whether *audi* means *listen to
this now* or *be a listener*. Greek always does.

Latin prohibits with *nōlī* + infinitive — *nōlī dīcere*, literally *be
unwilling to say* — which is polite and roundabout. Greek says **μὴ λέγε**
and is done. And *oportet* with accusative and infinitive is exactly **δεῖ**;
you have had the construction in Latin for years and are now learning its
original.
:::
:::

- **κελεύω** — I order, I command

- **ζητέω** — I seek, I look for
- **εὑρίσκω** — I find *(aorist **εὗρον**)* *(Lesson 5)*

- **ὁ νόμος** — law; custom *(Lesson 4)*

::: note
**γνῶθι** is the one imperative in this lesson that does not fit the table.
It is the aorist imperative of **γιγνώσκω**, built on the bare root
**γνω-**, with an old ending **-θι** that survives in only a handful of
verbs. You have been saying **γνῶθι σαυτόν** since Lesson 1. It is aorist:
*come to know yourself* — not as a habit, but as a thing to be done, once,
completely, and the maxim does not say it is easy.

7. **χρὴ πάντας τοὺς ἀνθρώπους τὸν νόμον γιγνώσκειν.**
8. **εἰπέ μοι, ὦ Ἰουλία, τίς ἔλαβε τὸ βιβλίον.**
9. **μὴ κέλευε τὸν αἴλουρον· οὐ γὰρ πείθεται.** *(**πείθεται** = obeys)*
10. **πᾶς γὰρ ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει καὶ ὁ ζητῶν εὑρίσκει.** *(Matthew 7:8)*

::: answers
1. *Listen, Marcus, and do not speak.* — aorist **ἄκουσον**: listen *now*,
   to this; present **μὴ λέγε**: stop talking, or don't be talking. Both
   aspects in one sentence, and the sentence was said in this classroom
   more than once.
2. *The children must learn; for they do not become wise at home.* —
   **δεῖ** + accusative + infinitive; **γάρ** giving the reason. Ask whether
   the reason is a reason. (It is Marcus's own argument turned round, and
   it is no better.)
3. *The just man ought to speak the truth.* — **χρή** works like **δεῖ**.
4. *Go, and you do likewise.* — both present imperatives: a way of life, not
   an errand. The class read this in Lesson 22; now they can say why the
   form is what it is.
5. *Seek the truth, child, and do not lie.* — two present imperatives: a
   habit to keep and a habit to drop. **ζήτει** is **ζητέω**'s present
   imperative with the vowels run together, like **ποίει** in 4.
6. *Ask, and God gives to you.* — **αἴτει**, present: be an asker. The
   enclitic **σοι** has thrown its accent back onto **δίδωσί** (Lesson 25).
7. *All people ought to know the law.* — **χρή** + accusative + infinitive;
   **πάντας** agrees with **τοὺς ἀνθρώπους**.
8. *Tell me, Julia, who took the book.* — aorist **εἰπέ**: one answer, now;
   then an indirect question (Lesson 25), with **τίς** keeping its accent.
   **ἔλαβε** is the aorist of **λαμβάνω**.
9. *Do not give orders to the cat; for it does not obey.* — **μή** +
   present imperative: stop doing it, or do not be the kind of person who
   does. **πείθεται** is the middle of **πείθω**: the cat has not been
   persuaded. The **γάρ** here is a real reason.
10. *For everyone who asks receives, and the one who seeks finds.* —
    article + participle = *the one who* (Lesson 22); **πᾶς** in front of
    it, *everyone who*. The class will read the whole passage in this
    lesson's Reading.

Here is Marcus's conclusion with its reason.

**οὐ δεῖ οὖν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ· ἡ γὰρ ψυχὴ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῷ
οἴκῳ.**

1. Check the grammar. Is **οὖν** in the right place? Is the construction
   after **δεῖ** correct? Does **γάρ** stand where it should?
2. Now the argument. **γάρ** promises a reason. Is what follows it a reason?
3. Say in one sentence what Livia saw that the rest of the class did not.

Here are four more of Marcus's sentences, as Julia took them down. For
each: grammar first, then argument.

4. **οἱ μὲν παῖδες μανθάνουσιν ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ, ἡ δὲ ψυχὴ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῷ
   οἴκῳ.**
5. **χρὴ γὰρ τοὺς παῖδας ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ μανθάνειν· ἐκεῖ γάρ ἐστιν ὁ
   διδάσκαλος.** *(**τὸ διδασκαλεῖον** = the school room; **ἐκεῖ** =
   there)*
6. **ὁ δὲ διδάσκαλος οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ· οὐδεὶς οὖν διδάσκει ἐν τῷ
   οἴκῳ.**
7. **δεῖ οὖν τὸν διδάσκαλον μὴ κελεύειν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν.** — there are
   two accusatives here. Which one is the subject of the infinitive, and
   what tells you?
8. Write Livia's reply in Greek: *the soul is always with the child.*
9. Which single word of Marcus's conclusion (the sentence at the top)
   would you change to make it true? Is the result still an argument?
10. Theodoros's whole reply was **γράφετε.** Give its mood, aspect and
    number, and say why he did not say **γράψατε**.

::: answers
1. Yes to all three. **οὖν** second; accusative + infinitive after **δεῖ**;
   **γάρ** second in its clause. The Greek is faultless.
2. No. *The soul is not in the house* is offered as the reason the children
   need not write at home, and it is neither true (the soul is wherever the
   pupil is) nor, if it were true, relevant (writing is done by hands). The
   connector promises evidence and the clause does not deliver it.
3. That the connectors are claims. **γάρ** does not just join two clauses;
   it asserts that the second supports the first, and that assertion can be
   false even when every word is correct. Lesson 17 taught the class to
   read **γάρ**; this is the lesson where they learn to *audit* it.
4. Grammar: **μέν … δέ** both second in their clauses, the subjects
   balanced — perfect. Argument: the first clause is true and the second is
   false, and **δέ** has set them side by side as if they were two facts.
   The balance is doing the persuading.
5. Grammar: two **γάρ**s, each second in its clause, the second giving the
   reason for the first — correct. Argument: *the teacher is there* is a
   reason for learning in the school room, not a reason against learning
   anywhere else. The sentence proves something nobody disputed.
6. Grammar: **οὖν** second — correct. Argument: *nobody teaches at home*
   does not follow from *the teacher is not at home*; the tablet, the book
   and the pupil are all at home, and each of them teaches. A pupil who notices that **οὐδείς** is doing more work than the
   evidence allows has audited the connector.
7. **τὸν διδάσκαλον** is the subject of **κελεύειν** — it is the teacher
   who orders; **τοὺς παῖδας** is the object of **κελεύειν** and the subject
   of **γράφειν**. Nothing in the endings tells you; the sense does, and
   the order helps. Note **μή** with the infinitive, which is correct: the
   infinitive takes **μή**, not **οὐ**. Argument: this is the conclusion
   again in different words, and a conclusion repeated is not a reason.
8. **ἡ ψυχὴ ἀεὶ σὺν τῷ παιδί ἐστιν.** — accept **ἐν τῷ παιδί**, or **ὅπου
   ὁ παῖς**, if a pupil knows **ὅπου**. Note **παιδί ἐστιν**: the acute
   stays acute before the enclitic.
9. Remove **οὐ**: **δεῖ οὖν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ** — and now the
   reason that follows it is a reason against it, so the **γάρ** would have
   to go too. Changing one word to make the conclusion true destroys the
   argument, which shows that the argument was never holding the
   conclusion up.
10. Imperative, present, plural: *keep writing*, *write — as a habit*.
    **γράψατε** would be *write this, now, once*, and the homework is not
    one deed but a rule of life. (At the end of the lesson he says
    **γράψον** to Marcus alone, and means one evening's work, completely.)
:::

   infinitive.
4. **γνῶθι σαυτόν.** — and they should now be able to say that it is an
   aorist imperative, from the root **γνω-**, with **σαυτόν** the reflexive
   *yourself* in the accusative. Twenty-five lessons after they first
   copied it.
5. **ζήτησον τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — aorist, on the pattern of **γράψον**. A
   pupil who writes **ζήτει** has said *be a seeker*, which is advice for a
   life and not for now; accept it if they can say so.
6. **μὴ φοβοῦ.** — the Gospels' phrase, given in the New Greek. **οὐ
   φοβοῦ** is not Greek.
7. **δεῖ εὑρίσκειν τὴν ὁδόν.** — with no accusative, because English *one*

8. **λάβετε τὸ βιβλίον.** — second aorist imperative plural, like
   **εἴπετε**, **μάθετε**; the stem is **λαβ-**, as in **ἔλαβον**. A pupil
   who writes **λαμβάνετε** has told them to keep taking it.
9. **χρὴ πάντας γιγνώσκειν τὸν νόμον.** — or **δεῖ πάντας …**; **πάντας**
   alone is *everyone*.
10. **ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει.** — article + participle as subject (Lesson 22);
    the Gospel's own words, less **πᾶς**.

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Carved at Delphi; quoted by Plato, *Charmides* 164d–165a and *Protagoras* 343b"}
The two sentences you began with, for the third and last time. You copied
them in Lessons 1 and 2 before you could read a letter. You can now explain
every mark in them.

**γνῶθι σαυτόν**

- **γνῶθι** — know! *(aorist imperative, second singular, of
  **γιγνώσκω**; the root **γνω-** with the old ending **-θι**)*
- **σαυτόν** — yourself *(accusative; **σ-** for *you* + **αὐτόν**)*

*Know yourself.*

Aorist. Not *be always getting to know yourself* — which a present
imperative would say — but *come to know yourself*: arrive, once, at the
knowledge. The form implies that the thing can be done and that it has not
been done yet. Those are both claims, and the Greeks carved them in the
forecourt of their holiest temple.

**μηδὲν ἄγαν**

- **μηδέν** — nothing *(the **μή** word, because this is a command)*
- **ἄγαν** — too much, in excess

*Nothing in excess.*

No verb. The **μ-** on **μηδέν** is the only grammar in the sentence and
it tells you that a verb of doing is understood and that the whole thing
is an instruction, not a description. **οὐδὲν ἄγαν** would mean *there is
nothing in excess*, which would be false. **μηδὲν ἄγαν** means *do nothing
in excess*, which is advice.

**Three lessons in two words each.** Aspect — the aorist of **γνῶθι**.
The negative of command — the **μή** of **μηδέν**. The verb that need not
be written — the missing *do*. You could not have seen any of it in Lesson
1. You copied the letters. You can now read the grammar, and the grammar
turns out to have been saying something all along.

::: teacheronly
**On the maxims.** They are proverbial and the Delphic carving is attested
only by report; the Plato references are the course's sources for the
wording, as in Lessons 1 and 2, and should be given as such.

**On Marcus.** This is his best scene and the course's notes call it his
best argument of the year, and its flaw. Give him the grammar victory in
full — Julia's concession should sound like a concession — before Livia
takes the argument apart. The lesson fails if he is made ridiculous; it
works if he is made right about the Greek and wrong about the world, in
that order, and knows which is which by the end.

**On πείθομαι.** The observation that the ordinary word for obeying is the
middle of the word for persuading is worth a minute. (Koine has
**ὑπακούω** as well, which has nothing to do with persuasion; the point is
about **πείθομαι**, not about the whole language.) It is
also the answer to a question a thoughtful pupil may ask about why
**κελεύω** and **πείθω** are different verbs at all.
:::

**The original** (Nestle–Aland).

Αἰτεῖτε καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν, ζητεῖτε καὶ εὑρήσετε, κρούετε καὶ
ἀνοιγήσεται ὑμῖν· πᾶς γὰρ ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει καὶ ὁ ζητῶν εὑρίσκει καὶ τῷ
κρούοντι ἀνοιγ[ής]εται. ἢ τίς ἐστιν ἐξ ὑμῶν ἄνθρωπος, ὃν αἰτήσει ὁ υἱὸς <!--nocheck-->
αὐτοῦ ἄρτον, μὴ λίθον ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ; ἢ καὶ ἰχθὺν αἰτήσει, μὴ ὄφιν
ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ; εἰ οὖν ὑμεῖς πονηροὶ ὄντες οἴδατε δόματα ἀγαθὰ διδόναι
τοῖς τέκνοις ὑμῶν, πόσῳ μᾶλλον ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς δώσει
ἀγαθὰ τοῖς αἰτοῦσιν αὐτόν. Πάντα οὖν ὅσα ἐὰν θέλητε ἵνα ποιῶσιν ὑμῖν οἱ
ἄνθρωποι, οὕτως καὶ ὑμεῖς ποιεῖτε αὐτοῖς· οὗτος γάρ ἐστιν ὁ νόμος καὶ οἱ
προφῆται.

::: gloss
- **αἰτεῖτε** — ask! *(present imperative plural of **αἰτέω**)*
- **δοθήσεται ὑμῖν** — it will be given to you *(a future; gloss it whole)*
- **εὑρήσετε** — you will find *(future of **εὑρίσκω**)*
- **κρούετε** — knock!
- **ἀνοιγήσεται** — it will be opened *(future; gloss it whole. In verse 8
  Nestle–Aland prints **ἀνοιγ[ής]εται**: the brackets mean some manuscripts <!--nocheck-->
  have the present, **ἀνοίγεται**, it is opened)*
- **πᾶς ὁ αἰτῶν** — everyone who asks
- **τῷ κρούοντι** — to the one who knocks *(the participle in the dative)*
- **ἤ** — or
- **ἐξ ὑμῶν** — among you *(**ὑμῶν**: of you, plural)*
- **ὃν αἰτήσει … ἄρτον** — whom his son will ask for bread *(**αἰτέω** takes
  two accusatives: the person and the thing)*
- **ὁ υἱὸς αὐτοῦ** — his son
- **μή** — surely not …? *(the question expects* no*; Lesson 25)*
- **ὁ λίθος** — stone
- **ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ** — will hand him *(future of **δίδωμι** with a prefix)*
- **ὁ ἰχθύς** — fish *(accusative **ἰχθύν**)*
- **ὁ ὄφις** — snake *(accusative **ὄφιν**)*
- **εἰ** — if
- **ὑμεῖς** — you *(plural, written out: Koine)*
- **πονηροὶ ὄντες** — being bad *(**ὤν**, the participle of **εἰμί**)*
- **οἴδατε** — you know *(**οἶδα** in the Koine plural; Attic **ἴστε**)*
- **δόματα** — gifts
- **διδόναι** — to give *(the infinitive of **δίδωμι**)*
- **τοῖς τέκνοις ὑμῶν** — to your children
- **πόσῳ μᾶλλον** — how much more
- **ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς** — your father, the one in the
  heavens *(the article repeated to attach the phrase: Lesson 10)*
- **δώσει** — will give *(future of **δίδωμι**)*
- **τοῖς αἰτοῦσιν αὐτόν** — to those who ask him
- **ὅσα ἐάν** — whatever *(**ὅσα**: as many things as)*
- **θέλητε** — you want *(a subjunctive after **ἐάν**; the form is new,
  the verb is Lesson 23's)*
- **ἵνα ποιῶσιν** — that they should do *(Koine **ἵνα**, Lesson 23;
  **ποιῶσιν** a subjunctive — gloss it whole)*
- **οὕτως** — so, in this way
- **ποιεῖτε** — do! *(present imperative plural of **ποιέω**)*
- **οὗτος** — this
- **οἱ προφῆται** — the prophets; *the law and the prophets* is the whole
  of Scripture
- *(adapted version only)* **ἀνοίγει** — opens; **πολλῷ μᾶλλον** — by much
  more; **ταῦτα** — these things
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both versions aloud before you answer anything.

1. The first verse has three commands. Find them, name their aspect, and
   say what the aspect asks for.
2. **πᾶς ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει.** What is the article doing, and what does
   **πᾶς** add?
3. In the adapted version, *ask* is answered by *gives*. In the original it
   is answered by **δοθήσεται**. What has the adaptation lost?
4. **τίς ἐστιν ἐξ ὑμῶν ἄνθρωπος …;** Is **τίς** *who?* or *someone*? Give
   two reasons.
5. **μὴ λίθον ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ;** The question begins with **μή**. What answer
   does it expect, and how does the adapted version say the same thing?
6. **εἰ οὖν ὑμεῖς πονηροὶ ὄντες οἴδατε …** Why is **ὑμεῖς** written out,
   when **οἴδατε** already means *you know*? Which Greek does this?
7. Find the **οὖν** in the last verse. What has gone before that it draws
   a conclusion from?
8. **ποιεῖτε** in the last verse could be *you do* or *do!* Which is it,
   and what tells you?
9. What are *the law and the prophets*, and why does the adapted version
   leave the prophets out?
10. Translate the last verse of the original. Then say in one sentence
    why a passage about asking ends with a sentence about giving.
:::

::: answers
1. **αἰτεῖτε**, **ζητεῖτε**, **κρούετε** — all present imperatives: keep
   asking, be a seeker, go on knocking. Not one request but a habit. A
   pupil who says *aorist* has not looked at the endings; the aorist would
   be **αἰτήσατε**, **ζητήσατε**, **κρούσατε**.
2. The article turns the participle into a noun — *the one who asks*
   (Lesson 22); **πᾶς** makes it *everyone who asks*, without exception.
3. The promise. **δοθήσεται** is future and passive: *it will be given*,
   by someone unnamed, later. The adaptation's **ὁ θεὸς δίδωσιν** names the
   giver and makes it a present fact. The Gospel is more reticent and more
   hopeful at once; the adaptation is more certain and says less.
4. *Who?* — it has its accent and stands at the head of its clause, and
   the sentence ends with a question mark. (Both reasons are needed: in
   Lesson 25's **ἐάν τίς τι** the accent alone was not enough.)
5. *No* — nobody would. **μή** opens a question that expects a negative
   answer (Lesson 25's **μὴ σοφός ἐστιν;**). The adapted version asks
   **τίς δίδωσιν …;** and answers itself: **οὐδείς**.
6. For emphasis — *you, bad as you are* — and because Koine writes its
   pronouns out (Lesson 23). The emphasis is the argument: if even *you*
   give good gifts, how much more will God.
7. The whole passage: God gives to those who ask; therefore (**οὖν**) you,
   who have been given to, give. The connector claims that the rule of life
   follows from the picture of God, and here the claim is fair.
8. *Do!* — a command. The form alone cannot tell you (the lesson's point
   about **λέγετε**); what tells you is that the sentence is parallel to
   the three commands at the start, and that **οὕτως καὶ ὑμεῖς**, *so you
   too*, is how Greek introduces an instruction, not a report.
9. The Scriptures as a Jew of the first century named them: the books of
   Moses and the books of the prophets. The adaptation keeps **ὁ νόμος**
   because it is in this lesson's vocabulary and drops **οἱ προφῆται**
   because it is not — and in doing so loses the claim that the whole of
   Scripture comes to this one sentence. Say so.
10. *So everything that you want people to do for you, you also do for
    them; for this is the law and the prophets.* The passage began by
    telling you to ask and promising that you will receive; it ends by
    telling you to be, for other people, the one who gives. The grammar
    moves from the imperative *ask* to the imperative *do*, and the **οὖν**
    is the hinge.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. A command, a piece of advice, and a persuasive argument can all produce
   the same action. Which does Greek make easiest to say? Which does it make
   hardest?
2. **γνῶθι** is aorist: *come to know yourself*, once. Is that possible?
   Would the maxim be truer in the present imperative?
3. Marcus's Greek was perfect and his argument was not. Which would you
   rather be good at? Which is harder to fix?
:::

Marcus asked, at the end, whether he had at least been right that learning
takes place in the soul.

"You were," said Theodoros. "It does. And the soul, as Livia pointed out,
is wherever you are, and this evening it will be in your house, with a lamp
and a tablet, copying the aorist imperative until it knows it. **γράψον.**"

"That is aorist," said Marcus.

"It is. Once, now, and completely. I chose it on purpose."
