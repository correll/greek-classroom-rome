# Commands, Advice, and Persuasion

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Marcus had asked for ten minutes and been given five, and he used them.

The argument, delivered standing and in Greek, ran roughly as follows.
Learning takes place in the soul. The soul is not in the house. Therefore
nothing that takes place in the house — specifically, the copying of
paradigms by lamplight — is learning. **οὐ δεῖ οὖν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν ἐν
τῷ οἴκῳ**: it is therefore not necessary for the pupils to write at home.
He sat down to something close to applause.

"Grammar first," said Theodoros. "Then the argument. Separately. Julia."

"The grammar is perfect," said Julia, and sounded as if it cost her. "**οὐ
δεῖ** with the accusative and infinitive. **οὖν** in second place. The
**γάρ** clauses all give reasons. He used **μέν … δέ** twice and both times
correctly. I cannot find anything."

"Nor can I. It is the best Greek anyone in this room has produced this
year, and I include myself." He turned. "Now the argument. Livia."

Livia had been waiting.

"The **γάρ** gives a reason," she said. "Every **γάρ** clause gives a reason.
The reasons are not reasons. *The soul is not in the house* — the soul is
wherever the pupil is, and the pupil is in the house. He has used the
connector that promises evidence and put a sentence after it that is not
evidence. Every one of his **γάρ**s is a false promise, in flawless Greek."

Marcus opened his mouth.

"She is right," said Theodoros, before he could. "And so are you, Marcus,
about one thing: you have shown that an argument can be grammatically
perfect and still wrong, which is a lesson I have been trying to teach since
Lesson 18, and you have taught it better by *being* the example. For that
you have my thanks." He paused. "The homework stands. **γράφετε.**"

It was, the class agreed afterwards, the best five minutes Marcus had ever
had, and he had lost.
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The imperative

You have been reading commands since the first week — **γνῶθι**, **ἄειδε**,
**ἔννεπε**, **πορεύου**, **ποίει** — without the form being named. The
**imperative** is the mood of telling someone to do something, and it comes
in present and aorist, like everything else.

| | 2nd sg. | 2nd pl. | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Present** | **λέγε** | **λέγετε** | be saying; keep saying |
| | **ἄκουε** | **ἀκούετε** | be listening |
| **First aorist** | **γράψον** | **γράψατε** | write (now, once) |
| | **ἄκουσον** | **ἀκούσατε** | listen (now) |
| **Second aorist** | **εἰπέ** | **εἴπετε** | say |
| | **ἐλθέ** | **ἔλθετε** | come |
| | **μάθε** | **μάθετε** | learn |

The second-person plural of the present looks exactly like the indicative
— **λέγετε** is both *you say* and *say!* — and context decides. (**ἀγαπᾶτε
τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν** in Lesson 11 was a command, and you read it as one
without knowing why.)

## Aspect, for the last time

A present imperative asks for an *activity* — ongoing, repeated, a habit, a
rule of life. An aorist imperative asks for a *deed* — once, now, complete.

- **ἄκουε** — *listen* as a habit; be a listener.
- **ἄκουσον** — *listen!* — to this, now.
- **πορεύου καὶ σὺ ποίει ὁμοίως** — both present. *Go on your way and keep
  doing likewise.* Not an errand; a way to live.
- **εἰπέ μοι** — aorist. *Tell me.* One answer, now.

You have met this difference in the indicative (Lesson 14), the infinitive
(Lesson 16) and the participle (Lesson 22). The imperative is the fourth and
last place it lives, and it is the place where it matters most in daily
speech, because most of what people say to each other is commands.

## Saying *don't*

Greek forbids with **μή**, never with **οὐ**.

**μή** + present imperative: *stop doing that* or *don't be the kind of
person who does that*. **μὴ λέγε** — *don't say* (as a habit; or, stop).
**μὴ φοβοῦ** — *do not be afraid*, which the Gospels say to people who
already are.

For *don't* as a single act — *don't say that one thing* — Greek uses **μή**
with a form called the aorist subjunctive, which you have not learned.
Recognise **μή** + a verb as a prohibition and let the exact form wait.

::: note
**This is why μηδὲν ἄγαν has μηδέν and not οὐδέν.** You may have wondered in Lesson 2
why the maxim used the **μή** word for *nothing*. The answer is that it is
a command — *[do] nothing in excess* — and commands take the **μή** family.
There is no verb in the maxim; the **μ-** tells you one is understood.
:::

## Obligation

**δεῖ** — *it is necessary* — and **χρή** — *one ought* — are impersonal.
They have no subject in the ordinary sense; the person who must do the
thing goes in the accusative, and the thing to be done is an infinitive.

**δεῖ σε μανθάνειν.** — *It is necessary for you to learn.* *You must learn.*
**χρὴ τοὺς παῖδας ἀκούειν.** — *The children ought to listen.*

It is the construction of Lesson 16 with a one-word verb on the front.
Marcus's whole argument rested on **οὐ δεῖ** + accusative + infinitive, and
he built it correctly.

## Telling, advising, persuading

Greek keeps these apart with its verbs, not its moods.

- **κελεύω** — I order. The imperative's verb.
- **συμβουλεύω** — I advise. Takes the dative: advice is given *to*.
- **πείθω** — I persuade. And **πείθομαι**, the middle, is *I am persuaded*
  — which is to say, *I obey*. In Greek, to obey is to have been
  convinced.

That last one is worth a moment. The language does not have a word for
obedience that is not also a word for being persuaded. A Greek who obeys
has, by the grammar, agreed.

::: latinbridge
**Latin's imperative is simpler and says less.** *Dic*, *audi*, *scribe* —
one form, no aspect. Latin cannot tell you whether *audi* means *listen to
this now* or *be a listener*. Greek always does.

Latin prohibits with *nōlī* + infinitive — *nōlī dīcere*, literally *be
unwilling to say* — which is polite and roundabout. Greek says **μὴ λέγε**
and is done. And *oportet* with accusative and infinitive is exactly **δεῖ**;
you have had the construction in Latin for years and are now learning its
original.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἄκουε** — listen! *(present imperative; aorist **ἄκουσον**)*
- **λέγε** — speak! *(present imperative; aorist **εἰπέ**, say!)*
- **γνῶθι** — know! *(aorist imperative of **γιγνώσκω**; irregular)*
- **μή** — not *(with commands, wishes and prohibitions)*
- **δεῖ** — it is necessary *(+ accusative + infinitive)*
- **χρή** — one ought, one must *(+ accusative + infinitive)*
- **πείθω** — I persuade; *(middle **πείθομαι**)* I obey
- **κελεύω** — I order, I command

::: note
**γνῶθι** is the one imperative in this lesson that does not fit the table.
It is the aorist imperative of **γιγνώσκω**, built on the bare root
**γνω-**, with an old ending **-θι** that survives in only a handful of
verbs. You have been saying **γνῶθι σαυτόν** since Lesson 1. It is aorist:
*come to know yourself* — not as a habit, but as a thing to be done, once,
completely, and the maxim does not say it is easy.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros had them sort Marcus's sentences into *commands*, *reasons* and
*conclusions* before they were allowed to say whether any of them were
true.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ἄκουσον, ὦ Μάρκε, καὶ μὴ λέγε.** *(**ὦ Μάρκε** = Marcus, vocative)*
2. **δεῖ τοὺς παῖδας μανθάνειν· οὐ γὰρ σοφοὶ γίγνονται ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ.**
   *(**γίγνονται** = they become)*
3. **χρὴ τὸν δίκαιον λέγειν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
4. **πορεύου καὶ σὺ ποίει ὁμοίως.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Listen, Marcus, and do not speak.* — aorist **ἄκουσον**: listen *now*,
   to this; present **μὴ λέγε**: stop talking, or don't be talking. Both
   aspects in one sentence, and the sentence was said in this classroom
   more than once.
2. *The children must learn; for they do not become wise at home.* —
   **δεῖ** + accusative + infinitive; **γάρ** giving the reason. Ask whether
   the reason is a reason. (It is Marcus's own argument turned round, and
   it is no better.)
3. *The just man ought to speak the truth.* — **χρή** works like **δεῖ**.
4. *Go, and you do likewise.* — both present imperatives: a way of life, not
   an errand. The class read this in Lesson 22; now they can say why the
   form is what it is.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Grammar, then argument"}
Here is Marcus's conclusion with its reason.

**οὐ δεῖ οὖν τοὺς παῖδας γράφειν ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ· ἡ γὰρ ψυχὴ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῷ
οἴκῳ.**

1. Check the grammar. Is **οὖν** in the right place? Is the construction
   after **δεῖ** correct? Does **γάρ** stand where it should?
2. Now the argument. **γάρ** promises a reason. Is what follows it a reason?
3. Say in one sentence what Livia saw that the rest of the class did not.
:::

::: answers
1. Yes to all three. **οὖν** second; accusative + infinitive after **δεῖ**;
   **γάρ** second in its clause. The Greek is faultless.
2. No. *The soul is not in the house* is offered as the reason the children
   need not write at home, and it is neither true (the soul is wherever the
   pupil is) nor, if it were true, relevant (writing is done by hands). The
   connector promises evidence and the clause does not deliver it.
3. That the connectors are claims. **γάρ** does not just join two clauses;
   it asserts that the second supports the first, and that assertion can be
   false even when every word is correct. Lesson 17 taught the class to
   read **γάρ**; this is the lesson where they learn to *audit* it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Listen! *(to one person, now)*
2. Do not lie. *(use **ψεύδου**, the command form of **ψεύδομαι**)*
3. The children must learn.
4. Know yourself.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἄκουσον.** — aorist. **ἄκουε** is *be a listener*, which is also a
   good thing to say to a child, but is not what was asked.
2. **μὴ ψεύδου.** — **μή**, not **οὐ**. A pupil who writes **οὐ ψεύδου** has
   written something that is not Greek.
3. **δεῖ τὰ παιδία μανθάνειν.** — or **τοὺς παῖδας**. Accusative, then
   infinitive.
4. **γνῶθι σαυτόν.** — and they should now be able to say that it is an
   aorist imperative, from the root **γνω-**, with **σαυτόν** the reflexive
   *yourself* in the accusative. Twenty-five lessons after they first
   copied it.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Carved at Delphi; quoted by Plato, *Charmides* 164e and *Protagoras* 343b"}
The two sentences you began with, for the third and last time. You copied
them in Lessons 1 and 2 before you could read a letter. You can now explain
every mark in them.

**γνῶθι σαυτόν**

- **γνῶθι** — know! *(aorist imperative, second singular, of
  **γιγνώσκω**; the root **γνω-** with the old ending **-θι**)*
- **σαυτόν** — yourself *(accusative; **σ-** for *you* + **αὐτόν**)*

*Know yourself.*

Aorist. Not *be always getting to know yourself* — which a present
imperative would say — but *come to know yourself*: arrive, once, at the
knowledge. The form implies that the thing can be done and that it has not
been done yet. Those are both claims, and the Greeks carved them over the
door of their holiest place.

**μηδὲν ἄγαν**

- **μηδέν** — nothing *(the **μή** word, because this is a command)*
- **ἄγαν** — too much, in excess

*Nothing in excess.*

No verb. The **μ-** on **μηδέν** is the only grammar in the sentence and
it tells you that a verb of doing is understood and that the whole thing
is an instruction, not a description. **οὐδὲν ἄγαν** would mean *there is
nothing in excess*, which would be false. **μηδὲν ἄγαν** means *do nothing
in excess*, which is advice.

**Three lessons in two words each.** Aspect — the aorist of **γνῶθι**.
The negative of command — the **μή** of **μηδέν**. The verb that need not
be written — the missing *do*. You could not have seen any of it in Lesson
1. You copied the letters. You can now read the grammar, and the grammar
turns out to have been saying something all along.

::: teacheronly
**On the maxims.** They are proverbial and the Delphic carving is attested
only by report; the Plato references are the course's sources for the
wording, as in Lessons 1 and 2, and should be given as such.

**On Marcus.** This is his best scene and the course's notes call it his
best argument of the year, and its flaw. Give him the grammar victory in
full — Julia's concession should sound like a concession — before Livia
takes the argument apart. The lesson fails if he is made ridiculous; it
works if he is made right about the Greek and wrong about the world, in
that order, and knows which is which by the end.

**On πείθομαι.** The note that Greek has no word for obedience that is not
also a word for having been persuaded is accurate and worth a minute. It is
also the answer to a question a thoughtful pupil may ask about why
**κελεύω** and **πείθω** are different verbs at all.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. A command, a piece of advice, and a persuasive argument can all produce
   the same action. Which does Greek make easiest to say? Which does it make
   hardest?
2. **γνῶθι** is aorist: *come to know yourself*, once. Is that possible?
   Would the maxim be truer in the present imperative?
3. Marcus's Greek was perfect and his argument was not. Which would you
   rather be good at? Which is harder to fix?
:::

Marcus asked, at the end, whether he had at least been right that learning
takes place in the soul.

"You were," said Theodoros. "It does. And the soul, as Livia pointed out,
is wherever you are, and this evening it will be in your house, with a lamp
and a tablet, copying the aorist imperative until it knows it. **γράψον.**"

"That is aorist," said Marcus.

"It is. Once, now, and completely. I chose it on purpose."
