# The Final Examination

::: story
The courtyard had been swept, which it never was, and there were benches
in it, which there never were, and on the benches sat parents, an uncle who
was a deacon, a physician, a minor official, several household servants
who had found reasons to be passing, and — on the wall, at a distance it
had chosen itself — the cat.

Eight pupils read. In Greek. Aloud. To a courtyard that, for the most part,
did not understand a word.

Julia read the first line of the *Odyssey* and then, as she had promised
for fifteen lessons, pointed out to the courtyard that there was no article
in it, and explained why, and her father, who had no Greek, nodded as if
this were the most natural thing in the world to be told by his daughter.

Theodosius read the Beatitudes and stopped after **πτωχοί** to say what the
word meant and what the Latin had made of it, and his uncle the deacon went
very still, and afterwards asked him to say it again.

Sabina read the Samaritan as if it were a play, giving the priest a voice
and the Levite a different one, and the courtyard laughed in the right
place, which in Greek is an achievement.

Quintus read Socrates and did not editorialise, which cost him visibly.
Livia read two verses of Luke and one sentence of explanation and sat
down, and the explanation was the clearest thing anyone said all afternoon.
Aelia read the Psalm in the Greek of her grandmother's kitchen. Felix read
Apion's letter and, at the end, said *and he got home*, which was not in the
text, and nobody corrected him.

Marcus read last.

He had chosen five words. The courtyard, which had sat through Homer, looked
faintly puzzled at the brevity.

**καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

"I translated this wrongly," he said, "in Lesson 18, in front of the class,
and I did it beautifully, and I was sure. I said *God was the Word*. I
followed the order of the Greek. I thought that was the honest thing."

He pointed at the tablet.

"It is not. The order is not what carries it. **ὁ λόγος** has the article.
**θεός** does not. The article marks the subject — we learned that in
Lesson 3, and I knew it, and I did not look. The sentence is about the
Word. *The Word was God.* **θεός** is first because Greek puts a word first
to lean on it, and the article is there so that it can." He stopped. "Julia
told me this. I did not hear her, because I was listening to myself. I have
had three months to work out why I should have, and I have chosen this
sentence so that I would have to say so in front of people who do not know
Greek and will not know whether I am right. You will have to take it from
the grammar."

He sat down.

Theodoros, who had not said a word all afternoon, did not say one now. He
looked at Marcus for a moment, and then at Julia, and then at the courtyard,
and then he stood up and said, "That concludes the examination," and nothing
else, and it was the highest mark he gave anyone that year.
:::

## Everything

No new grammar. This is what you have.

| | You can | Since |
|:--|:--|:--:|
| **The noun** | read five cases and know what each does; decline the first and second declensions; recognise the third | 1–9 |
| **The article** | use it to find the subject; see what is definite; read attributive against predicate position; notice when it is *absent* | 2, 3, 7, 10, 18 |
| **The adjective** | make it agree; put it inside or outside the article group and know the difference | 10 |
| **The verb** | read the present, imperfect and aorist and say what the *aspect* is doing; recognise the future; use the infinitive in its two jobs; read the participle in a chain; give a command in either aspect | 5, 11, 13, 14, 16, 21, 22, 26 |
| **The sentence** | read word order as emphasis, not role; follow **μέν … δέ**, **γάρ**, **οὖν**; report speech with the infinitive or with **ὅτι**; build and read a relative clause; ask a question and tell it from a statement by its accent | 4, 16, 17, 25 |
| **The Greeks** | tell Homeric from Attic from Septuagint from Koine, and say what gave each away | 15, 20, 23, 24 |
| **The method** | put four questions to a sentence and five to a passage; ask what would have to be true for you to be wrong; know where the grammar stops and the page begins | 18, 24, 27, 29 |

Twenty-nine lessons. About a hundred and fifty words. Everything on the
list above is something you could not do at the start of the year and can
do now, and most of it is something most people never do at all.

## What the examination asks

Three things, of each of you, about the passage you read. They are the
three things the course has asked of every sentence since Lesson 1.

**What does it say?** A translation, defended — with the feature of the
Greek it rests on named aloud.

**What is one piece of its grammar doing?** Not all of it. One thing —
an article, an aspect, a connector, a participle — explained so that a
listener who has no Greek can see why it matters.

**What do you know now that you did not before you read it in Greek?**
Not what the passage means. What *reading it in Greek* gave you that the
Latin, or the English, had not. If the answer is *nothing*, say so and
say why; that is also an answer.

## And the fifth question, one last time

*What would have to be true in the Greek for my translation to be wrong?*

Marcus asked it of himself, three months late, about five words, and the
answer was *the article would have to be on the other noun*, and he checked,
and it was not. That is the whole method. It is what Theodoros has been
teaching since the first week, and it is the only thing he was ever
teaching, and it works on everything.

::: latinbridge
**A year ago, Latin was the thing you knew and Greek was the thing you
did not.** Now you have two languages, and each one shows you the other.
You know that Latin has no article because Greek does. You know that
Latin's perfect is doing two jobs because Greek gives each its own tense. You know that
*caritas* was a word for *expensive* because **ἀγάπη** never was.

That is what a second ancient language is for. Not to replace the first.
To make the first visible.
:::

::: investigation
The written part of the examination. Theodoros set it the day before the
courtyard, and said that anyone who could do it had nothing to fear from
an audience. The Retrieval came first, as it has every week; the
examination proper has four sections of ten.

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory, and quickly. Lessons 26 to 29; nothing from today.

1. **ἄκουσον** / **ἄκουε** — which asks for a deed and which for a habit?
2. **Ἀπίων Ἐπιμάχῳ χαίρειν.** — name the two cases, and the verb that is
   not written.
3. **ἦλθεν** — under which word would you look it up in a lexicon?
4. **τί δειλοί ἐστε;** — is **τί** *what?* here? What is it?
5. **δεῖ σε μανθάνειν.** — case of **σε**, and why.
6. **ἐγείρουσιν αὐτόν** / **ἤγειραν αὐτόν** — tense of each, and the
   translation of each.
7. **ἀσπάζομαι τὸν ἀδελφόν** / **γράφω τῷ ἀδελφῷ** — two cases for two
   verbs. Why?
8. **ὁ ἀκολουθῶν ἐμοί** — what has the article done to the participle?
9. **χρὴ τοὺς παῖδας ἀκούειν.** — translate; what case are the children in?
10. In the method of Lesson 29, what is step two, and why does it come
    before the lexicon?
:::

::: answers
1. **ἄκουσον** (aorist) a deed; **ἄκουε** (present) a habit (Lesson 26).
2. Nominative sender, dative recipient; **λέγει**, understood and dropped
   (Lesson 28).
3. **ἔρχομαι** — the stem **ἐλθ-** will not undress to anything else; it
   has to be known (Lesson 29).
4. No — *why?*; the neuter alone as an adverb (Lesson 27).
5. Accusative: the subject of the infinitive after **δεῖ** (Lesson 26). The
   likely wrong answer is *object*.
6. Present, *they wake him*; aorist, *they woke him* (Lesson 27).
7. Accusative: you greet *someone*. Dative: you write *to* someone. The
   letter formula uses the dative for the same reason (Lesson 28).
8. Made a noun of it: *the one following me* (Lessons 22 and 29).
9. *The children ought to listen.* Accusative — the person who must act,
   after **χρή** (Lesson 26).
10. Mark the finite verbs. Because the verbs tell you how many clauses
    there are, and until you know that you do not know what you are
    looking up (Lesson 29).
:::

::: {.exercise title="Unseen"}
Apply the method of Lesson 29, step by step, and then translate.

**ὁ θεὸς φῶς ἐστιν καὶ σκοτία ἐν αὐτῷ οὐκ ἔστιν οὐδεμία.** *(1 John 1:5)*

*(**οὐδεμία** = none, no — feminine, agreeing with **σκοτία**.)*

1. Step one. Read it aloud. How many clauses do you hear, and what word
   divides them?
2. Step two. The finite verbs.
3. Step three. The subject of each. What marks the first subject, and what
   is missing from the second?
4. Step four. Participles and infinitives?
5. Step five. The connectors.
6. Step six. What is left to look up?
7. Step seven. Translate.
8. The fifth question: what would have to be true in the Greek for *God is
   light* to be wrong — for the sentence to say *the light is God*?
9. **οὐκ ἔστιν οὐδεμία.** Two negatives. What do they do together in Greek,
   and what would they do in English?
10. Set beside it **ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν** (1 John 4:8, Lesson 19) and **θεὸς
    ἦν ὁ λόγος** (John 1:1, Lesson 18). What do the three sentences have in
    common, and which one is built the other way round?
:::

::: answers
1. Two; **καί**.
2. **ἐστιν**, **ἔστιν**.
3. **ὁ θεός**, marked by its article; **σκοτία**, with no article —
   *darkness*, not *the darkness*. The missing article is the point: there
   is not any darkness of any kind.
4. None — and *none* is the right answer; a pupil who hunts for one has not
   trusted step two.
5. **καί**, joining the two clauses; nothing else. The sentence is as plain
   as Greek gets.
6. **οὐδεμία** only, and it is given. Every other word is owned.
7. *God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all.*
8. **φῶς** would have to carry the article and **θεός** go without it:
   **τὸ φῶς θεός ἐστιν**. It is the other way; **φῶς** has no article and is
   the predicate, exactly as **ἀγάπη** was in Lesson 19 and **θεός** in
   Lesson 18 — the same rule, for the third time, and they should say so.
9. They reinforce; Greek piles its negatives and each one strengthens the
   last: *there is not any at all*. In English two negatives cancel —
   *there isn't no darkness* would mean there is some — which is why the
   translation needs *at all* to carry what the second Greek negative
   carried.
10. All three have a definite subject with the article (**ὁ θεός**, **ὁ
    λόγος**) and a predicate noun without one (**φῶς**, **ἀγάπη**,
    **θεός**). John 1:1 is the one turned round: the predicate stands
    first, before the verb, and the article has to do the work the order
    does not — which is Marcus's sentence, and the whole reason he chose
    it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="One feature"}
For the passage you read in the courtyard, write three sentences explaining
one grammatical feature so that someone with no Greek could follow. Then
write one sentence saying what would have to be different in the Greek for
your explanation to be wrong.

The ten features below are one for each passage on the list. Write on the
one that is yours; be able to say, for any of the others, what the three
sentences would have to contain.

1. Homer, *Odyssey* 1.1 — **ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα**: the missing article.
2. Homer, *Odyssey* 9 — **Οὖτις**: a name that is a trick of case.
3. Plato, *Apology* 38a — **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ**: a
   sentence with no verb, and a **δέ**.
4. Plato, *Meno* 70a — **ἆρα διδακτὸν ἡ ἀρετή;**: a neuter predicate for a
   feminine subject.
5. Psalm 22 — **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με**: no article on **Κύριος**, and why.
6. Matthew 5 — **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι …**: a blessing
   formula and an **ὅτι** that means *because*.
7. Luke 10 — **ἰδὼν … ἐσπλαγχνίσθη**: a participle carrying the story to
   its verb.
8. Luke 17 — **ἐντὸς ὑμῶν**: a preposition with a genitive and two
   meanings.
9. Apion — **Ἀπίων Ἐπιμάχῳ χαίρειν**: an infinitive whose verb nobody
   writes.
10. John 1:1 — **καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**: the article marking the subject
    when the order does not.
:::

::: answers
No key for the four sentences themselves; mark the *fourth* hardest. It is
the one that shows whether the pupil has understood that an explanation is
a claim about evidence, and a pupil who cannot write it has explained a
feeling. For each feature, what the three sentences must contain:

1. That Greek of Homer's time had no word for *the*; that **ἄνδρα** is
   *man* or *the man* alike; that the later language grew the article
   (Lesson 15). Wrong if: a **τόν** stood before **ἄνδρα**.
2. That **Οὖτις** is *Nobody* dressed as a name, and declines like one —
   **Οὖτις**, **Οὖτιν**; that in ordinary Greek the same sounds are **οὔ
   τις**, *not anyone*; that the pun works because the name changes its
   case exactly as the two words would (Lesson 16). Wrong if: the name
   did not decline — a **Οὖτις** that stayed **Οὖτις** in the accusative
   would be a foreign name, and no pun.
3. That the verb *is* is left out when the sentence is a maxim; that
   **δέ** marks this as the other half of a pair; that *not worth living*
   is one adjective, **βιωτός**, with **οὐ** (Lesson 17). Wrong if: an
   **ἐστίν** were present, or the **δέ** were **γάρ**.
4. That **διδακτόν** is neuter though **ἀρετή** is feminine; that Greek
   uses the neuter for *a teachable thing*, a category; that the sentence
   therefore asks what kind of thing virtue *is* (Lesson 25). Wrong if:
   it read **διδακτή**.
5. That **Κύριος** without the article is the Septuagint's name for God,
   standing for a name that was not pronounced; that a Greek reader would
   have expected **ὁ κύριος**; that the absence is a Hebrew habit showing
   through (Lesson 20). Wrong if: the article were there, in which case
   **κύριος** would read as a common noun, *the* lord or master, not the
   name.
6. That **μακάριοι** has no verb and needs none; that **οἱ πτωχοί** is the
   subject with the article; that **ὅτι** gives the reason, not a report
   (Lesson 21). Wrong if: **ὅτι** followed a verb of saying.
7. That **ἰδών** is *having seen*, aorist, and happens before the main
   verb; that the Samaritan sees and is then moved; that the participle
   does what English does with *when* (Lesson 22). Wrong if: it were
   **βλέπων**, present, *while seeing*.
8. That **ἐντός** takes the genitive; that **ὑμῶν** is plural; that the
   plural is what keeps *among* open (Lesson 27). Wrong if: it read
   **σοῦ**.
9. That **χαίρειν** is *to rejoice*; that the sender is nominative and the
   recipient dative; that **λέγει** is understood (Lesson 28). Wrong if:
   **Ἐπίμαχος** stood in the nominative — then he would be sending it.
10. That both nouns are nominative; that the article on **λόγος** marks it
    as the subject; that **θεός** stands first for emphasis and not for
    role (Lesson 18). Wrong if: the article were on **θεός** instead.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word
   was God.
2. The one who did the mercy was a Samaritan.
3. We know that the unexamined life is not worth living.
4. Felix to the teacher, greetings. I learned. Farewell. *(use **ἔμαθον**,
   the aorist of **μανθάνω**)*
5. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness did not overcome it.
   *(**φαίνει** = shines)*
6. Who is this? Even the wind and the sea obey him. *(**ὑπακούει** =
   obeys)*
7. Before all else I pray that my father is well.
8. Do not wake the teacher; he is sleeping. *(to several people)*
9. We do not know when he came.
10. Listen, children, and learn! Then say what you heard. *(to several; the
    first two commands once, now)*
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ
   λόγος.** — John 1:1, entire, from Lessons 6, 13 and 18. The article on
   **λόγος** in the third clause and not on **θεός**: Marcus's sentence,
   written correctly.
2. **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος Σαμαρίτης ἦν.** — the lawyer's phrase from Lesson
   22 as subject; **Σαμαρίτης** predicate, no article.
3. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν ἀνεξέταστον βίον οὐ βιωτὸν εἶναι.** — Socrates's
   sentence put through Lesson 16. **βίος** and **ἀνεξέταστος** both go to
   the accusative; **βιωτόν** follows them; **εἶναι** supplies the verb the
   original did without. A pupil who produces this has used Lessons 10,
   16 and 17 on one of the most famous sentences in Greek and should be
   told exactly that.
4. **Φῆλιξ τῷ διδασκάλῳ χαίρειν. ἔμαθον. ἔρρωσο.** — the formulas of Lesson
   28 around one aorist verb: *I learned* — once, completely, and the
   aspect is the claim.
5. **τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν.** — John
   1:5, from Lesson 27, and the second half of the day's Reading is built
   on it. Present for the shining, which goes on; aorist for the failure
   to overcome, which is over. **αὐτό** neuter, agreeing with **φῶς**.
6. **τίς ἐστιν οὗτος; καὶ ὁ ἄνεμος καὶ ἡ θάλασσα ὑπακούει αὐτῷ.** — Mark
   4:41, Lesson 27. **τίς** with its acute, first; **καὶ … καί**,
   *both … and* (or *even*, Lesson 2); singular verb with two
   subjects, as the class saw. A pupil who writes **ὑπακούουσιν** has
   written correct Greek and not Mark's.
7. **πρὸ μὲν πάντων εὔχομαι τὸν πατέρα ὑγιαίνειν.** — Apion's formula with a
   noun for its subject; accusative and infinitive (Lessons 16 and 28).
   Accept **τὸν πατέρα μου**.
8. **μὴ ἐγείρετε τὸν διδάσκαλον· καθεύδει γάρ.** — **μή** with the present
   imperative plural (Lesson 26); **γάρ** second in its clause, giving the
   reason (Lesson 17). A pupil who writes **οὐκ ἐγείρετε** has written a
   statement — *you are not waking him* — which is not what was asked.
9. **οὐκ οἴδαμεν πότε ἦλθεν.** — indirect question; **πότε** keeps its
   accent, because it is a question still (Lesson 25); a pupil who writes
   **ὅτε** has written *we do not know, when he came*, which is a
   different and stranger sentence (Lesson 28).
10. **ἀκούσατε, ὦ παῖδες, καὶ μάθετε· ἔπειτα εἴπετε τί ἠκούσατε.** — three
    aorist imperatives plural (Lesson 26), the first two for single acts
    *now*; **εἴπετε** from **εἶπον**; **ἔπειτα**, Lesson 14; **τί** with the
    acute in an indirect question (Lesson 25); **ἠκούσατε**, the aorist
    with its augment lengthening **α** to **η** (Lesson 14). Accept
    **ἀκούετε … μανθάνετε** from a pupil who says the listening and the
    learning are meant as habits.
:::

::: {.voice source="The pupils' readings"}
Eight passages, every one of them in this book, read aloud to people who
could not follow them, by pupils who could.

**Julia** — *Odyssey* 1.1. *Tell me of the man, Muse.* Chosen to prove, in
public, that Homer has no article, and to explain why — which she did, and
her father nodded.

**Theodosius** — the Beatitudes. Stopped at **πτωχοί** to say that the
Greek goes lower than the Latin. His uncle asked him to say it again.

**Sabina** — the Samaritan. Read as a play; the courtyard laughed at the
Levite, in Greek, which means she made the participles audible.

**Quintus** — *Apology* 38a. Read without comment, which was the hardest
thing he did all year, and the explanation he gave afterwards was about
the **δέ**.

**Livia** — Luke 17:20–21. Two verses, one sentence of explanation: *he is
answering Pharisees*. Nobody in the courtyard needed more.

**Aelia** — Psalm 22. In the Greek of Antioch, which is the Greek of the
Septuagint, which is the Greek her grandmother speaks, and she said so.

**Felix** — Apion's letter. He added *and he got home*, which is not in
the text, and the deacon, who knew it was not, let it stand.

**Marcus** — **καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.** Five words, the ones he had got wrong,
read correctly, with the grammar explained to people who could not check it
and had to take it from the article.

**What the eight have in common.** Every one is a text somebody wrote in
Greek because they had something to say that they could not say otherwise.
Every one was read, this afternoon, by a child who a year ago could not
read the letters. And every one was explained — not performed, explained —
with a feature of the grammar named aloud, so that the listener could see
the reasoning even where they could not see the Greek.

That is not a small thing. A great many grown people with a great deal of
Greek cannot do it.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** 1 John 1:5 in the written examination is quoted
from the standard text (see *Sources*); check it against the edition in use.

**On running the Great Reading.** Invite an audience. The point of the
exercise is that the pupils explain Greek to people who do not have it, and
that cannot be simulated; a class explaining to one another is a rehearsal.
Parents will do; so will another class; so will the school's kitchen staff,
who will be more attentive than you expect.

**On the three questions.** Ask them of every pupil, in order, and do not
let the first swallow the third. *What do you know now that you did not?*
is the one that tells you whether the course taught reading or decoding,
and a pupil who answers it well has usually surprised themselves.

**On Marcus.** His reading is the course's ending and it should be allowed
to land. He has chosen the sentence he got wrong, prepared it properly for
the first time, and said in public that Julia was right. Theodoros gives him
no praise in words because none would be adequate and because Marcus, by
this point, does not need it. If your own class has a Marcus, you will know
whether to speak. Mostly, do not.

**On the preface.** Now is the time to return to the question the course
opened with — why learn Greek — and to let the class answer it, since they
are now in a position to. The honest teacher's answer is in the preface:
pupils asked it two thousand years ago, in this very city, and the language
is still here, and so is the question, and so, now, are they.
:::

The Great Reading closes with the passage the course has been circling since
Lesson 6: the opening of John, whole, to the verse where the Word becomes
flesh. You have had its first sentence since Part I, its first five verses
since Lesson 18, and the last five words of its first verse since Marcus
got them wrong. The first
version is **adapted** — cut to its spine, with John the Baptist's verses
reduced to one sentence and no form you have not met; it is there so that you
can hear the shape of the whole in Greek you own, and it is short because the
original is long and is the one that matters. The second is the text of
Nestle–Aland, glossed beside it. Read both aloud; the second one slowly.


**Adapted.**

ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. ἐν αὐτῷ
ζωὴ ἦν, καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν. ἦλθε
μάρτυς τοῦ φωτός, καὶ ὄνομα αὐτῷ Ἰωάννης· οὐκ ἦν αὐτὸς τὸ φῶς. καὶ ὁ λόγος
ἄνθρωπος ἐγένετο, καὶ εἴδομεν τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ, πλήρη χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας.


**The original** (Nestle–Aland).

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.
οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν. πάντα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ χωρὶς αὐτοῦ
ἐγένετο οὐδὲ ἕν. ὃ γέγονεν ἐν αὐτῷ ζωὴ ἦν, καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν
ἀνθρώπων· καὶ τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ
κατέλαβεν.

Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος, ἀπεσταλμένος παρὰ θεοῦ, ὄνομα αὐτῷ Ἰωάννης· οὗτος ἦλθεν
εἰς μαρτυρίαν ἵνα μαρτυρήσῃ περὶ τοῦ φωτός, ἵνα πάντες πιστεύσωσιν δι᾽
αὐτοῦ. οὐκ ἦν ἐκεῖνος τὸ φῶς, ἀλλ᾽ ἵνα μαρτυρήσῃ περὶ τοῦ φωτός.

Ἦν τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινόν, ὃ φωτίζει πάντα ἄνθρωπον, ἐρχόμενον εἰς τὸν
κόσμον. ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ ἦν, καὶ ὁ κόσμος δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν
οὐκ ἔγνω. εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθεν, καὶ οἱ ἴδιοι αὐτὸν οὐ παρέλαβον. ὅσοι δὲ
ἔλαβον αὐτόν, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τέκνα θεοῦ γενέσθαι, τοῖς πιστεύουσιν
εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, οἳ οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων οὐδὲ ἐκ θελήματος σαρκὸς οὐδὲ ἐκ
θελήματος ἀνδρὸς ἀλλ᾽ ἐκ θεοῦ ἐγεννήθησαν.

Καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν, καὶ ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν
αὐτοῦ, δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός, πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας.

::: gloss
- **οὗτος** — this one; he *(Lesson 17; the pronoun picks up **ὁ λόγος**)*
- **πάντα** — all things *(neuter plural of **πᾶς**, Lesson 15, and so a
  singular verb follows: Lesson 7)*
- **δι᾽ αὐτοῦ** — through him *(**διά** + genitive)*
- **χωρὶς αὐτοῦ** — apart from him
- **οὐδὲ ἕν** — not even one thing *(**ἕν**, neuter of *one*)*
- **ὃ γέγονεν** — what has come to be *(a form you have not met; gloss it
  whole. Nestle–Aland reads it with the words that follow)*
- **φαίνει** — shines
- **ἀπεσταλμένος παρὰ θεοῦ** — sent from God *(a participle of
  **ἀποστέλλω**, Lesson 19, of a tense you have not met; gloss it whole)*
- **ὄνομα αὐτῷ Ἰωάννης** — his name John *(literally "a name to him";
  no verb)*
- **εἰς μαρτυρίαν** — for a witness; to bear witness *(the noun of
  **μάρτυς**, Lesson 4)*
- **ἵνα μαρτυρήσῃ** — so that he might bear witness *(a subjunctive after
  **ἵνα**; gloss it whole)*
- **ἵνα πάντες πιστεύσωσιν** — so that all might believe *(the same; gloss
  it whole)*
- **ἀλλ᾽ ἵνα** — but *(he came)* so that
- **τὸ ἀληθινόν** — the true, the real *(the adjective of **ἀλήθεια**)*
- **ὃ φωτίζει** — which gives light to *(**φῶς** turned into a verb)*
- **ἐρχόμενον εἰς τὸν κόσμον** — coming into the world *(a participle of
  **ἔρχομαι**, Lesson 22; neuter, with **τὸ φῶς**)*
- **ἔγνω** — knew, recognised *(aorist of **γιγνώσκω**; Lesson 27)*
- **εἰς τὰ ἴδια … οἱ ἴδιοι** — to his own *(things, home)* … his own
  *(people)*: neuter, then masculine
- **οὐ παρέλαβον** — did not receive *(**ἔλαβον**, Lesson 14, with a
  prefix: take to oneself)*
- **ὅσοι** — as many as; all who
- **ἔδωκεν** — he gave *(**ἔδωκα**, Lesson 16)*
- **ἐξουσίαν** — right, authority
- **τέκνα θεοῦ γενέσθαι** — to become children of God *(**γενέσθαι**, the
  infinitive of **ἐγένετο**; **τέκνον**, Lesson 1)*
- **τοῖς πιστεύουσιν εἰς** — to those who believe in *(**πιστεύω εἰς**,
  Lesson 19; the participle with the article, Lesson 22)*
- **οἳ … ἐγεννήθησαν** — who were born *(a passive; gloss it whole)*
- **οὐκ ἐξ αἱμάτων** — not of blood *(plural in Greek)*
- **ἐκ θελήματος σαρκός … ἀνδρός** — of the will of flesh … of a man
  *(**θέλημα**, the noun of **θέλω**, Lesson 23; **σάρξ**, flesh)*
- **σὰρξ ἐγένετο** — became flesh *(no article: the predicate)*
- **ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν** — pitched his tent among us; dwelt among us
- **ἐθεασάμεθα** — we beheld *(a form you have not met; gloss it whole)*
- **ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός** — as of an only son from a father
- **πλήρης** — full *(of, + genitive; the form does not change here)*
- *(adapted version only)* **εἴδομεν** — we saw *(**εἶδον**, Lesson 15,
  first plural)*; **πλήρη** — full *(accusative, agreeing with **δόξαν**)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both versions aloud before you answer anything. The original is set in
four paragraphs; the questions follow them in order.

1. Verse 1 has three clauses on **καί**. In which one does the predicate
   stand before the verb, and what marks the subject there?
2. **οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν.** Who is **οὗτος**, and what does the
   verse add to verse 1 — or does it only repeat it?
3. **πάντα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο.** Translate. Why is the verb singular?
4. **φαίνει … κατέλαβεν.** Name the tense of each and say what the
   difference does. Then give the two meanings of **κατέλαβεν** and say
   what the adapted version lost by keeping only that verb.
5. **ὄνομα αὐτῷ Ἰωάννης.** No verb. What case is **αὐτῷ**, and what has the
   adapted version added to make the clause stand?
6. **οὐκ ἦν ἐκεῖνος τὸ φῶς.** The predicate has the article. Set it beside
   **ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων** in verse 4 and **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** in
   verse 1. What is the article doing in each, and why does Marcus's rule
   — *the article marks the subject* — not decide this sentence?
7. **ὁ κόσμος αὐτὸν οὐκ ἔγνω.** **αὐτόν** is masculine. What has it been
   referring to for two verses, and what gender is that word? What has
   happened?
8. **εἰς τὰ ἴδια ἦλθεν, καὶ οἱ ἴδιοι αὐτὸν οὐ παρέλαβον.** The same
   adjective twice, neuter then masculine. Translate both and say what
   the change of gender does.
9. **ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον αὐτόν, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς …** Two verbs from Lessons 14 and
   16. **ὅσοι** is nominative; what is its verb? And why does the sentence
   then say **αὐτοῖς** — *to them* — when it has already named them?
10. **καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο.** The adapted version wrote **ἄνθρωπος**.
    What was lost? Then the fifth question, one last time: what would have
    to be true in the Greek for *the Word became flesh* to be wrong?
:::

::: answers
1. The third: **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**. The article on **ὁ λόγος**. In the
   second clause the subject stands first with its article, and in the
   first there is no predicate noun to confuse it with; in the third order
   and article part company and the article wins. This is Lesson 18 and Marcus's reading, and the class should be
   able to say it in two sentences by now.
2. **ὁ λόγος** — the pronoun picks up the last noun with the article. The
   verse gathers the first two clauses of verse 1 into one and says them
   again: *this one was, in the beginning, with God*. It is repetition,
   and the repetition is the Hebrew habit of saying a thing twice to fix it
   (Lesson 20); a pupil who calls it redundant has described it correctly
   and judged it by the wrong language.
3. *All things came to be through him.* **πάντα** is neuter plural, and a
   neuter plural subject takes a singular verb (Lesson 7). A pupil who
   wants **ἐγένοντο** has written the Greek of a different century.
4. **φαίνει** present: the shining goes on, now, as the reader reads.
   **κατέλαβεν** aorist: the attempt — whichever it was — happened once and
   failed. *Overcome* or *comprehend* (Lesson 27). The adapted version
   kept the verb and so kept both meanings; what it lost was **φαίνει**,
   the present tense that makes the darkness's failure a thing still
   going on.
5. Dative — *a name to him*, the dative of the possessor: *he had a name,
   John*. Greek leaves the verb out of such clauses as it does from
   proverbs. The adapted version supplied **καί** to join it to **ἦλθε**,
   and left the verb out as the original does, so the pupil who looked for
   one in either version should report that there was none to find.
6. In verse 1 the article is on the subject and the predicate has none. In
   verse 4 both have it — **ἡ ζωή** and **τὸ φῶς** — and the sentence
   identifies the two: *the life was the light*, and the second noun
   carries its article because it is the particular light already named.
   In verse 8 the subject is **ἐκεῖνος**, a pronoun, which needs no
   article to be a subject, and **τὸ φῶς** is again *the* light, the one
   under discussion, so it keeps its article as a predicate. Marcus's rule
   decides between two nouns of which one has the article and one has not;
   when both have it, or when the subject is a pronoun, the rule has
   nothing to say, and the reader falls back on sense and on what the
   writer has been talking about — which is Lesson 27's lesson, that the
   grammar permits and the page decides.
7. **τὸ φῶς**, neuter — **ὃ φωτίζει**, **αὐτό** in verse 5. In verse 10 the
   pronoun turns masculine without warning: the writer has stopped
   speaking of the light and begun speaking of the person, and the
   grammar changes gender before the sentence says why. A pupil who
   noticed the switch has read the Greek; a pupil who translated *him*
   without noticing has read the English they already knew.
8. *He came to his own* — things, home, what belonged to him — *and his
   own* — people — *did not receive him*. The neuter is a place; the
   masculine is the people in it. English has one *his own* for both and
   the sentence's turn — he came home and the household shut the door —
   is carried in Greek by a vowel.
9. **ὅσοι** is the subject of **ἔλαβον**, its own clause; it has no place
   in the main clause: the sentence begins with it and then abandons it,
   picking it up again as **αὐτοῖς** in the dative because **ἔδωκεν**
   needs a dative. Grammarians call it a hanging nominative; the Hebrew behind the Greek did it all
   the time (Lesson 20), and so does anyone talking: *the ones who took him
   in — he gave them the right …* Both verbs are the class's: **ἔλαβον**
   (Lesson 14) and **ἔδωκεν** (Lesson 16).
10. **σάρξ** is *flesh* — meat, the body as a body, the thing that is
    hungry and dies. **ἄνθρωπος** is *a human being*, a word with dignity
    in it. The adaptation kept the sense and lost the shock; the Gospel
    chose the lower word on purpose, and the Latin, *Verbum caro factum
    est*, kept it. For the translation to be wrong, the articles would
    have to change places — **ἡ σὰρξ λόγος ἐγένετο**, *the flesh became
    word*, which is nonsense and which the Greek does not say — or **ἐγένετο**
    would have to be **ἦν**, *was*, which is what verse 1 says of the Word and
    what verse 14 pointedly does not say of flesh. It is not; it is
    **ἐγένετο**, Lesson 14's *it happened* — and that difference, between
    what the Word *was* and what it *became*, is carried in two verbs the
    class has owned since Part III.
:::

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The original is the Nestle–Aland text; the
points to check are the full stop after **οὐδὲ ἕν** and the attachment of
**ὃ γέγονεν** to verse 4, where editions and translations divide, and
**πλήρης** in verse 14, which NA28 prints in the nominative. The adapted
tier has **ἄνθρωπος** for **σάρξ** and drops **ἐσκήνωσεν** altogether, so
that the exercise can ask what was lost; say so if a pupil objects to the
theology of the adaptation, which they may.

**On the ending.** The Reading, not the courtyard, is where Part V ends:
the same sentence that opened Lesson 6 read whole, in context, by a class
that can now say what every word of it is doing. Let the last question
land and do not add to it. Part VI begins with the voice they have been
glossing past all year — the middle — and the first thing the cat does in the
second term is get washed.
:::

::: question
1. A year ago you could not read the letters. Today you read aloud, in
   Greek, to a room. What else that you currently believe to be impossible
   is merely unlearned?
2. Marcus chose the sentence he had got wrong. Was that courage, penance,
   or simply the best way to learn it? Does it matter which?
3. Theodoros gave no verdict. The courtyard could not check the grammar.
   On what, in the end, was anyone's reading judged — and who was doing the
   judging?

::: story
Afterwards, when the benches had been carried back in and the courtyard was
a courtyard again, Felix found Theodoros putting the scrolls away.

"Sir. Was it worth it?"

"Was what worth it?"

"Greek. All of it. A year. My mother asked, this morning, what it was *for*,
and I did not have an answer."

Theodoros finished tying the Homer, which took some time, and set it on the
shelf next to the Psalms, where it had always lived.

"When I was your age," he said, "in Antioch, I asked my teacher the same
question, in the same words. He told me that his own teacher had been asked
it, and his before that, back as far as anyone could trace, and that the
question was older than the Gospels and older than Plato and that every
generation of pupils had asked it and every generation had gone on learning
the language anyway — which he said was the only answer the question had
ever had, and the only one it needed."

"That is not an answer."

"No," said Theodoros. "It is a fact. You have the Greek now. Make the answer
yourself. You have a fortnight to do it in, and then we begin again."

"Begin *what*? We have read everything."

"You have read everything that is done by somebody to something else.
Every verb you know looks outward. The Greeks had a whole voice of the
verb that looks back at its own subject — *I wash*, meaning myself; *I
am persuaded*, meaning I obey — and you have been reading round it all
year, naming it and stepping round it. **βούλομαι. δύναμαι. ἔρχομαι.**
You know a dozen of its forms already and have never been shown how they
hang together. That is
the second term, and the cat is in it." He put the last scroll on the
shelf. "**ἔρρωσο** — for a fortnight. After that, **ἔρρωσθε**, all of you,
and bring your tablets clean."

Felix went home. The cat, which had understood none of it, stayed on the
wall until dark, and was, in its own estimation, the **κύριος** of
everything it could see; and had no idea what the second term had in store
for it.
:::
