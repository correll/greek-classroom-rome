# The Familiar Words Return

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros wrote two words on the board and asked what they meant.

**ὁ κύριος**

"Lord," said Theodosius, before anyone else had finished reading it. He said
it the way a person says their own name.

"Good. In Athens, eight hundred years ago, what did it mean?"

Theodosius opened his mouth and shut it again.

"It meant," said Theodoros, "the man who is legally responsible for a
household. The one who signs for it. If your father died, your **κύριος**
was your uncle, or your eldest brother, or whoever the law said had charge
of you. And as an adjective it meant *valid* — a **κύριος** contract is one
that will hold up. It is the word for the man who owns the house and the
word for a lease that has been properly witnessed."

"But — "

"You have said it ten thousand times. *Dominus.* And here is the Greek
behind it, and the Greek is the word for a landlord."

Theodosius looked, for a moment, as though something had been taken from
him.

Sabina saw it. "That cannot be all it means."

"It is not all it means. It is what it meant *first*. When the Jews of
Alexandria translated their scriptures into Greek, more than six hundred years before
any of you were born, they came to the name of God — the name they did not
say aloud — and they had to put something. They did not invent a word. They
did not borrow one from the temples. They took the most ordinary word in the
language for *the one in charge* and they put it there, and it has stood
there ever since." He tapped the board. "That is not a loss of meaning. That
is the meaning. Every time you say it, you are calling God *the one who holds
the house*, and the people who chose the word meant you to."

Theodosius was quiet for a while.

"I thought I knew that word," he said eventually.

"You knew the Latin. You knew what it had come to mean. You are now going to
learn what it meant before that, and then what it meant in between, and by
the end of this part of the course you will know it the way the man who
wrote it down knew it." Theodoros picked up the chalk again. "This is going
to happen to you about six times in the next six lessons, and the first time
is the worst."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Welcome to Part IV

The Greek in this part of the book is mostly **Koine** — the "common" Greek
spoken across the eastern Mediterranean from about 300 BC onwards, after
Alexander's conquests spread the language far beyond the cities that had
made it. It is the Greek of the Septuagint, of the New Testament, and of the
streets of Theodoros's own Antioch.

It is the same language as the Attic you have learned. The grammar is
simpler in places, the vocabulary has shifted, and you will be told exactly
where, each time. Where a form or a usage is Koine and not Attic, it is
marked *(Koine)* in the vocabulary. Everything unmarked is the Greek you
already know.

## A word is a region, not a point

You have been treating vocabulary as a list of equations: **ὁ λόγος** =
*word*. That was right for a beginner and it is now wrong for you.

A word covers a *range* of meanings, and the range is different in every
language. **λόγος** covers *word*, *statement*, *account*, *argument*,
*reason*, *ratio*, *story*, *the principle behind a thing* — and no single
English word covers that ground. When you translate **λόγος** as *word* you
are choosing one corner of a large field and hoping it was the right corner.

That is not a failure of your Greek. It is what translation is.

## Words move

The range also shifts with time. A word in Homer, the same word in Plato,
and the same word in the Gospel of John may sit in three different places,
because four hundred years passed between each and people were using it the
whole while.

Three of this lesson's words show it clearly.

**χάρις.** In Attic: *grace* in the sense of charm, loveliness; also a
*favour* done for somebody; also the *thanks* you owe them for it. (English
keeps a shadow of this in *gracious* and *grateful*.) In the New Testament
the *favour* sense swallows the rest, and **χάρις** becomes a gift that has
not been earned.

**πίστις.** In Attic: *trust*, *credit*, a *pledge*, a *proof* — the thing
that makes you believe something. In the New Testament: *faith*, which is
the same thing seen from the inside.

**ἀγάπη.** Almost unknown in classical prose. The verb **ἀγαπάω** is old and
means *to be content with*, *to treat with affection*; the noun is
essentially a Septuagint and New Testament word. When you meet it you are
meeting a word the Greek Bible made.

::: note
**One Koine construction to notice now.** Attic says **πιστεύω** with the
dative: **πιστεύω τῷ διδασκάλῳ**, *I trust the teacher*. The New Testament
also says **πιστεύω εἰς** with the accusative — *I believe into* somebody —
which is not Attic at all. The grammar has stretched to carry an idea the
older language did not have a shape for.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin got here first, and that is the problem.** You know these words
already — *gratia*, *fides*, *caritas*, *dominus* — because you have had the
Latin Bible since you could read. That means you will meet each Greek word
with a meaning already attached, and the attached meaning is the Latin's,
not the Greek's.

*Caritas* is the clearest case. It is the ordinary Latin for *dearness* —
including high prices; *annonae caritas* is the cost of corn. The
translators used it for **ἀγάπη** because the obvious word, *amor*, carried
associations they did not want. So the Latin you know for *love* began as a word
for *expensive*. The Greek never did.

Knowing the Latin is a help. Trusting it is a mistake. Hold each word up
against the Greek and look for where they fail to overlap.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ κύριος** — master, the one in charge; lord *(the Septuagint's word for
  God)*
- **ἡ χάρις** — grace; favour; thanks *(genitive **χάριτος**)*
- **ἡ ἀγάπη** — love *(Koine)*
- **ἡ πίστις** — trust, faith *(genitive **πίστεως**)*
- **πιστεύω** — I trust, I believe *(with the dative; in the New Testament
  also **πιστεύω εἰς** + accusative)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — word; account; argument; reason *(Lesson 1, now with its
  full range)*

::: note
**ἡ χάρις** and **ἡ πίστις** are third-declension nouns. You can recognise
their genitives from the gloss; producing the rest of the paradigm is not
asked of you yet.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros said that two of these sentences were from the Gospels and the
rest were his, and that they should be able to tell which by the end.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.** *(1 John 4:8)*
2. **πιστεύω τῷ διδασκάλῳ· λέγει γὰρ τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ὁ κύριος τοῦ οἴκου ἀγαθὸς ἀνὴρ ἦν.**
4. **ἡ χάρις τοῦ θεοῦ δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
:::

::: answers
1. *God is love.* — and the class should do this one with Lesson 18 in
   hand. Both nouns are nominative; **θεός** has the article and **ἀγάπη**
   does not; so **ὁ θεός** is the subject and the sentence is about God. The
   grammar that ruled out *God was the Word* now rules *in* *God is love*
   and rules out *love is God*. Let them find that it is the same rule.
2. *I trust the teacher, for he speaks the truth.* — dative after
   **πιστεύω**; **γάρ** gives the reason.
3. *The master of the house was a good man.* — both senses of **κύριος** at
   once, which is the point. A pupil who writes *the Lord of the house* has
   let the Latin in through the back door; ask whether an Athenian would
   have heard anything religious in the sentence.
4. *The grace of God is a gift.* — accept *the favour of God*; the two
   English words mark the two ends of the word's range.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Which corner of the field?"}
**λόγος** appears in each sentence. For each, say which English word you
would choose for it and why that one.

1. **ὁ λόγος τοῦ διδασκάλου ἦν σοφός.**
2. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**
3. **οὐ δύναμαι τὸν λόγον τοῦ Σωκράτους μανθάνειν.** *(**τοῦ Σωκράτους** =
   of Socrates)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The teacher's statement* or *speech* or *account was wise* — something
   said, on one occasion. *Word* is too small.
2. Here most pupils will write *Word* with a capital, because they know the
   sentence, and this is the moment to ask them whether they are
   translating the Greek or the Latin. The honest answer is that no single
   English word does it and the capital letter is a confession of that.
3. *I cannot understand Socrates's argument.* — **λόγος** as a line of
   reasoning. A pupil who writes *word* here should be asked what it would
   mean to be unable to learn one word.

The exercise has no wrong answers and several bad ones. Mark the *reason*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. God is love.
2. I trust the teacher.
3. The master of the house was a good man.
4. We know that faith is a gift.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.** — the article on **θεός** and not on **ἀγάπη**.
   If a pupil writes **ἡ ἀγάπη θεός ἐστιν**, they have written *love is
   God*, and should be shown why.
2. **πιστεύω τῷ διδασκάλῳ.** — dative. Not **τὸν διδάσκαλον**.
3. **ὁ κύριος τοῦ οἴκου ἀγαθὸς ἀνὴρ ἦν.**
4. **γιγνώσκομεν τὴν πίστιν δῶρον εἶναι.** — accusative and infinitive from
   Lesson 16; **τὴν πίστιν** is the accusative of **ἡ πίστις**, and they
   can be given it.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Plato's idiom; Psalm 32:6 (LXX); John 1:1"}
One word, three centuries apart, three times.

**In Athens.** **λόγος** is what a citizen gives when he is called to
account — **λόγον διδόναι**, *to give an account*, is what you do before a
court or a questioner. And **λόγος** stands against **ἔργον**, *word*
against *deed*, in a contrast Greek writers never tire of. The word lives in
the law court and the argument.

**In Alexandria.** The Septuagint, Psalm 32:

**τῷ λόγῳ τοῦ κυρίου οἱ οὐρανοὶ ἐστερεώθησαν**

- **τῷ λόγῳ** — by the word *(dative — the means)*
- **τοῦ κυρίου** — of the Lord *(this lesson's word, in the Septuagint's
  sense)*
- **οἱ οὐρανοί** — the heavens *(Lesson 2, now plural)*
- **ἐστερεώθησαν** — were made firm *(an aorist passive; recognise it,
  do not produce it)*

*By the word of the Lord the heavens were made firm.*

Here **λόγος** is not an account or an argument. It is a thing God says that
makes something exist. The word has moved.

**In Ephesus, or wherever John wrote.**

**ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**

You have had this since Lesson 6. Read it now with the other two beside it.
It is the Psalm's word — the thing God says that makes the world — and it is
the Athenian's word, the reason behind things, and the writer has chosen it
because it is both. No translation into any language has ever managed to
keep both.

**That is what this part of the course is for.** Not to learn new words,
mostly. To learn that the old ones were larger than you thought.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The Psalm is 32:6 in the Septuagint's numbering
(33:6 in the Hebrew and in most English Bibles) and is quoted from memory of
the standard text; check it against the edition in use. The Plato material is
idiom, not quotation, and is presented as such; do not let it be written on
the board as a sentence "from Plato."

**On Theodosius.** His moment with **κύριος** is meant to be a real loss
followed by a real gain, in that order. Do not hurry the loss. A pupil who
discovers that a sacred word was once an ordinary one is entitled to a moment
of vertigo, and the recovery — that the ordinariness was chosen — is worth
more if it comes after.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Theodosius knew what the word meant in Latin and was wrong about the
   Greek. Is it harder to learn a word you have never met, or one you think
   you already know?
2. The translators of the Septuagint used an ordinary word for God. Was that
   a risk, a reverence, or both?
3. If a word covers a region rather than a point, can a translation ever be
   *the* right one — or only a defensible one?
:::

Felix wanted to know whether the household cat, which he had labelled with a
noun in the first week, was the **κύριος** of anything.

Theodoros said that the cat was the **κύριος** of the kitchen, the
courtyard, and Felix, in that order, and that the law of Athens would have
recognised all three.
