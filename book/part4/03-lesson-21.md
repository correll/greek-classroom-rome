# The Sermon on the Mount

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"You all know this," said Theodoros. "Every one of you. You have known it
since before you could read."

He wrote the first line on the board, in Greek, and eight people said the
Latin under their breath before they had finished reading it.

*Beati pauperes spiritu.*

"Good. Now read the Greek. Not the Latin that is already in your head —
the Greek. And tell me what is different."

There was a long silence, of the kind that meant everybody was reading the
Latin.

Theodosius put his hand up. He had been expecting to find something
*holier* in the Greek, Theodoros suspected — something the Latin had
flattened — and had been looking forward to it since the start of Part IV.

"**πτωχοί**," he said.

"Yes."

"That is not *pauperes*. *Pauper* is — my uncle's clerk is *pauper*. He has
a room and a bed. **πτωχός** is — " He stopped, and looked at the word
again. "It is the one who crouches. The one with his hand out. It is the man
at the gate."

"Yes."

"The Greek is *harsher*."

"The Greek is harsher. There is a Greek word for *poor* in your clerk's
sense, and it is **πένης**, and it is not the word here. The word here is
the beggar." Theodoros let it sit. "You expected the Greek to be loftier
than the Latin. It is lower. It goes further down than the Latin dared to
go, and the Latin, which you have said ten thousand times, has been softening
it for you the whole time."

Theodosius did not say anything for a while.

"That is not what I expected to find," he said at last.

"No. That is what I was hoping you would say." Theodoros turned back to the
board. "Eight lines. Every line has a word like that in it. Let us find
them."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The blessing formula

**μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί.** Two words, no verb.

You met this shape in Lesson 3, and again in the *Apology* in Lesson 17:
Greek does not need **ἐστίν** when the sentence is *X is Y*. Here it is
used as a formula. **μακάριος** goes first, in the nominative, agreeing with
whoever is being called blessed; then the article and the group. *Blessed
the poor.* The verb is understood, and the sentence is a pronouncement.

Each line then adds **ὅτι** — *because* — and a reason.

## ὅτι, again

In Lesson 16 you met **ὅτι** meaning *that*, introducing reported speech:
**λέγει ὅτι …**, *he says that …*. Here it means *because*. It is the same
word, and context decides, and you will meet both uses constantly in the
Gospels. A **ὅτι** after a blessing is *because*; a **ὅτι** after *he said*
is *that*.

## The article makes a noun out of anything

**οἱ πτωχοί** is an adjective with the article: *the poor ones*. You know
this from Lesson 10.

Some lines do the same thing with a form you have not had yet:

**οἱ πενθοῦντες** — *the mourning ones*, *those who mourn*. **πενθέω** is
*I mourn*, and **πενθοῦντες** is a **participle** — a verb turned into an
adjective — and the article turns the adjective into a noun. *The ones
mourning.* Participles are the subject of the next lesson. For now, treat
**οἱ** + a word ending in **-οντες**, **-οῦντες** or **-ῶντες** (and, once,
**-μένοι**) as *the people who are doing, or have had done to them, that*,
and move on.

## The dative of respect

**οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι** — *the poor in spirit*. The dative says *in what
respect*: poor *as to* spirit. **οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ** — the pure *in
heart*. You know the dative from Lesson 9 as the case of *to* and *for*;
here it answers *in what way?*

## αὐτοί

Most of the reasons begin **ὅτι αὐτοί** — *because they*. Greek, you
remember, does not need a word for *they*; the verb ending carries it. When
the word is put in anyway, it is for emphasis. **αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται**:
*they*, and nobody else, will be comforted. Each line is saying that the
blessing goes exactly to these and not to the others.

::: note
**The futures.** You will see **-σουσιν** and **-θήσονται** on the ends of
verbs in this passage. They are future tense, active and passive — *they
will inherit*, *they will be comforted*. You can recognise them by the
**σ**, which is the future's mark in Greek as it was the aorist's. You are
not asked to produce them.
:::

::: latinbridge
**The Latin is not wrong. It is gentler.** *Beati pauperes spiritu* is a
rendering by a translator who had a Latin word for the beggar — *mendicus*,
which he uses of Lazarus — and chose not to use it here. Every line has a choice like that in it. *Mites* for
**πραεῖς**; *misericordes* for **ἐλεήμονες**; *mundo corde* for **καθαροὶ τῇ
καρδίᾳ** — the dative of respect carried straight across into the Latin
ablative, which is the Latin that does that job.

The lesson is not that the Latin is bad. It is that you have been reading
the Latin as though it were the thing itself, and it is a translation, with
a translator's choices in it, and now you can see them.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **μακάριος** — blessed, fortunate; beyond the reach of trouble
- **ὁ πτωχός** — beggar; destitute *(not merely poor)*
- **ἡ βασιλεία** — kingdom, kingship
- **ἡ γῆ** — earth, land
- **καθαρός** — clean, pure
- **ἡ καρδία** — heart
- **αὐτός, αὐτή, αὐτό** — he, she, it; *(in the nominative)* emphatic: *they
  themselves*
- **τὸ πνεῦμα** — breath; wind; spirit *(neuter; dative **τῷ πνεύματι**)*
- **ὁ οὐρανός** — heaven, sky *(Lesson 2; the Gospel uses the plural,
  **οἱ οὐρανοί**)*
- **ὁ ὄχλος** — crowd
- **τὸ ὄρος** — mountain, hill *(third declension; dative **τῷ ὄρει**)*
- **ὁ μαθητής** — pupil, learner; disciple *(from **μανθάνω**, Lesson 11)*
- **ὁ προφήτης** — prophet; one who speaks for a god
- **οὕτως** — thus, so, in this way

::: note
**μακάριος** comes from a word older than any religion in this room. In
Homer **οἱ μάκαρες** (from **μάκαρ**, the root of our word), *the blessed
ones*, are the gods — the beings whom nothing can
touch. The word is also used of the dead, who are past harm. It does not
mean *lucky*; luck can turn. It means *in a state that trouble cannot
reach*. That is what is being said of the beggar and the mourner, and it is
a startling thing to say.

**τὸ πνεῦμα** is *breath* first, *wind* second, and *spirit* third, and all
three are alive in the Gospel's use of it. Latin *spiritus* has the same
range, which is why you did not notice.
:::
:::

From Lessons 17 to 20. Answer from memory.

1. **ὁ μὲν ποιμὴν ἄγει, τὰ δὲ πρόβατα ἀκούει.** Translate. Why is the
   second verb singular?
2. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.** Translate, and say what is un-Greek about it.
3. **ἔμενε** and **ἔμεινε** — which is imperfect and which aorist?
   Translate both.
4. Write *I trust God* in Attic, and then in the New Testament's other way.
5. **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.** Which word is missing, and
   how do you know one is?
6. **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.** Translate it literally, then as English says it.
7. **τὸ ἔλεος** is neuter in Koine. What gender is it in Attic? Give the
   Attic article.
8. **γιγνώσκομεν τὴν πίστιν δῶρον εἶναι.** Say it the Koine way, with
   **ὅτι**.
9. Give the genitive of **ὁ ποιμήν**, **ἡ χάρις**, **ὁ παῖς**.
10. **οὐδεὶς βλέπει τὸν θεόν.** What case is **οὐδείς**? What would the
    sentence mean with **οὐδένα** instead?
:::

::: answers
1. *The shepherd leads, and the sheep listen.* **πρόβατα** is neuter
   plural, and a neuter plural subject takes a singular verb (Lesson 7).
   A pupil who "corrects" it to **ἀκούουσι** has forgotten the rule, not
   found an error.
2. *The Lord shepherds me* — a verb where Greek wants a noun (*is my
   shepherd*), and **Κύριος** without the article, as a name.
3. **ἔμενε** imperfect (present stem **μεν-**), *he was staying*;
   **ἔμεινε** aorist (stem **μειν-**, the lengthened vowel doing the work
   of **σ**), *he stayed*.
4. **πιστεύω τῷ θεῷ**; **πιστεύω εἰς τὸν θεόν**.
5. **ἐστίν**. Two nominative groups, **ὁ βίος** and **βιωτός**, and no
   verb: the sentence must be *X is Y* with the verb understood, as in
   Lesson 3 and in this lesson's formula.
6. *Into the age*; *forever*.
7. Masculine: **ὁ ἔλεος**.
8. **γιγνώσκομεν ὅτι ἡ πίστις δῶρόν ἐστιν.** — the accusative becomes a
   nominative with its own verb. **ὅτι τὴν πίστιν** is the half-way house
   to watch for.
9. **ποιμένος**, **χάριτος**, **παιδός**.
10. Nominative, the subject. **οὐδένα** is accusative, so the sentence
    would become *[someone] sees no one*, with **τὸν θεόν** left over and
    nothing for it to do. The case of **οὐδείς** decides who is doing the
    seeing; that is the whole of Lesson 3, still working.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ.**
2. **μακάριος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
4. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ γῆ.**
5. **μακάριοι οἱ μαθηταί, ὅτι ἀκούουσι τοὺς λόγους τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
6. **ὁ προφήτης εἶπε τῷ ὄχλῳ τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ δὲ ὄχλος οὐκ ἤκουσεν.**
7. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἦλθεν εἰς τὸ ὄρος καὶ ἐδίδασκε τοὺς μαθητὰς ἐν τῷ
   ὄρει.**
8. **μακάριος ὁ πτωχὸς τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτοῦ ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία.**
9. **οὕτω γὰρ ἔλεγον οἱ προφῆται τοῖς ἀνθρώποις.**
10. **καθαρὰ ἡ καρδία τοῦ παιδός· αὐτὸς οὖν τὸν θεὸν βλέπει.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Blessed are the pure in heart.* — no verb; **τῇ καρδίᾳ** dative of
   respect.
2. *Blessed is the man who speaks the truth.* — composed for the lesson.
   **ὅς** from Lesson 17; **μακάριος** singular agreeing with **ἄνθρωπος**.
3. *The kingdom of heaven is a gift.* — **τῶν οὐρανῶν** is plural: *of the
   heavens*. The Gospel's idiom is plural throughout, and a pupil who
   translates it singular has done what every English Bible does, and
   should know they have done it.
4. *Theirs is the earth.* — **αὐτῶν** genitive, *of them*; the Gospel's own
   word order, with the possessor first for emphasis.
5. *Blessed are the disciples, because they hear the words of the
   teacher.* — the formula, then **ὅτι** *because*. **μαθητής** is
   **μανθάνω** with a person-ending; a pupil who guesses *learners* has
   earned it.
6. *The prophet told the crowd the truth, but the crowd did not listen.*
   — **εἶπε** aorist, one act; **ἤκουσεν** aorist, one refusal. **ὁ δέ**
   marks the turn.
7. *The teacher went up into the mountain and was teaching the disciples
   on the mountain.* — **ἦλθεν** aorist, **ἐδίδασκε** imperfect: he
   arrived once and taught for a while. **τῷ ὄρει** is the dative of
   **ὄρος**; they have it from the box.
8. *Blessed is the poor man in spirit, because his is the kingdom.* —
   singular throughout; **αὐτοῦ**, *of him*, in the Gospel's position.
9. *For so the prophets used to speak to the people.* — **οὕτω**, the
   form before a consonant;
   **γάρ** second; imperfect **ἔλεγον**, a habit. Watch for *I was
   saying*, which the ending allows and the subject forbids.
10. *The child's heart is pure; he himself, therefore, sees God.* — no
    verb in the first half (the formula's shape without **μακάριος**);
    **αὐτός** nominative, emphatic; **οὖν** second.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
**μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.**

1. Which word tells you there is no verb in the first half, and how?
2. What does **ὅτι** mean here, and what else could it mean? What decides?
3. Why is **αὐτοί** there at all, when the verb ending already says *they*?

Now the other lines.

4. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ.** What case is **τῇ καρδίᾳ**, and
   what question does it answer?
5. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.** Which word is the subject, and
   why is **αὐτῶν** first?
6. **μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς, ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν.** What tells
   you **κληρονομήσουσιν** is future and not present?
7. **υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται.** There is no article on **θεοῦ**. Does that
   make it *sons of a god*?
8. **οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες τὴν δικαιοσύνην.** What tells you these two
   words are *the people who …* and not verbs?
9. **ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης** in the eighth line, **τὴν δικαιοσύνην** in the
   fourth. Why is the case different?
10. The first and eighth lines end with the same four words. What does
    that tell you about the shape of the whole?
:::

::: answers
1. No single word does; the *absence* tells you. **μακάριοι** is nominative
   plural and so is **οἱ πενθοῦντες**, and there is nothing else, so the
   sentence must be *X [is] Y*, with **ἐστίν** understood. This is Lesson 3
   working again.
2. *Because.* It could mean *that*, as in Lesson 16; what decides is that
   there is no verb of saying or thinking for a *that* to hang from, and a
   blessing followed by a reason is the pattern of all eight lines.
3. Emphasis: *they* and not others. A pupil who says "it is just there" has
   missed the one thing in the sentence that is doing rhetorical work, and
   should be asked what the line would lose without it.
4. Dative; *in what respect?* — pure *as to* the heart. The error is to
   read it as *to the heart* (Lesson 9's dative) and produce *blessed are
   the pure to the heart*, which means nothing.
5. **ἡ βασιλεία**, nominative with the article. **αὐτῶν** is first because
   it is the point: *theirs*, and not anyone else's. Greek fronts the word
   it cares about (Lesson 3, Marcus's objection).
6. The **σ** before the ending: the present would be **κληρονομοῦσιν**.
   The grammar note gave them this; a pupil who says *because it must be
   future to make sense* is right and has not answered the question.
7. No — Lesson 18 again: a predicate noun, and a noun in a genitive chain,
   can drop the article and stay definite. **υἱοὶ θεοῦ** is a Hebrew-style
   chain (Lesson 20), and the Hebrew behind it has no indefinite sense.
8. The article **οἱ** in front, and the ending **-ντες**, which no finite
   verb has. **πεινῶσι** would be the verb, *they hunger*. (Lesson 22
   will build this; for now the rule of thumb is enough.)
9. **τὴν δικαιοσύνην** is the object of hungering and thirsting —
   accusative. **ἕνεκεν** is a preposition and takes the genitive, so
   **δικαιοσύνης**. Same word, two jobs, two cases.
10. That it is a ring: the first and last lines are the frame, and the six
    between them are held inside it. The pupil who notices that the two
    framing lines also both have **αὐτῶν ἐστιν** where the others have
    **αὐτοί** + future has seen the shape entirely.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Blessed are the pure.
2. Blessed is the man who speaks the truth.
3. The kingdom of the heavens is a gift.
4. Theirs is the earth.
5. Blessed are the disciples, because they hear the truth.
6. The prophet spoke to the crowd.
7. The teacher went up into the mountain. *(use **ἦλθεν**)*
8. Blessed is the child, because his heart is pure.
9. The kingdom of God is not of the earth.
10. So the prophets used to speak.
:::

::: answers
1. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροί.** — no verb. A pupil who adds **εἰσίν** has
   written correct Greek and missed the formula.
2. **μακάριος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
4. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ γῆ.** — the Gospel's order. Accept **ἡ γῆ αὐτῶν ἐστιν**;
   ask what moved and why the Gospel put **αὐτῶν** first.
5. **μακάριοι οἱ μαθηταί, ὅτι ἀκούουσι τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — no verb in the
   first half; **ὅτι** *because*. A pupil who writes **γάρ** has given a
   reason in Attic and lost the formula.
6. **ὁ προφήτης εἶπε τῷ ὄχλῳ.** — dative; **ὄχλος** is masculine. **τὸν
   ὄχλον** after a verb of speaking is the Latin *alloqui* coming through.
7. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἦλθεν εἰς τὸ ὄρος.** — **εἰς** with the accusative;
   **ὄρος** is neuter, so its accusative looks like its nominative. The
   Gospel's own verb, **ἀνέβη**, arrives in Lesson 23.
8. **μακάριος ὁ παῖς, ὅτι ἡ καρδία αὐτοῦ καθαρά ἐστιν.** — **αὐτοῦ**,
   genitive of this lesson's **αὐτός**, after its noun as the Gospel puts
   it. **καθαρά** feminine, agreeing with **καρδία**; **καθαρός** is the
   error of a pupil who has made it agree with the child.
9. **ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ οὐκ ἐκ τῆς γῆς ἐστιν.** — **ἐκ** + genitive for
   *of* in the sense of *from*. Accept **οὐκ ἔστι τῆς γῆς**, the bare
   genitive of belonging.
10. **οὕτως ἔλεγον οἱ προφῆται.** — imperfect for *used to*; **οὕτως**
    first, because it is the point. **εἶπον** is the aorist error: *so the
    prophets said*, once.
:::
:::

Eight lines. You know every one in Latin. Read the Greek.

**μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.**
*Blessed the beggars in spirit, because theirs is the kingdom of the
heavens.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.**
*Blessed those who mourn, because they will be comforted.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς, ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν.**
*Blessed the gentle, because they will inherit the earth.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες τὴν δικαιοσύνην, ὅτι αὐτοὶ
χορτασθήσονται.**
*Blessed those who hunger and thirst for justice, because they will be
filled.*

**μακάριοι οἱ ἐλεήμονες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ ἐλεηθήσονται.**
*Blessed the merciful, because they will be shown mercy.*

**μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ, ὅτι αὐτοὶ τὸν θεὸν ὄψονται.**
*Blessed the pure in heart, because they will see God.*

**μακάριοι οἱ εἰρηνοποιοί, ὅτι αὐτοὶ υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται.**
*Blessed the peacemakers, because they will be called sons of God.*

**μακάριοι οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία
τῶν οὐρανῶν.**
*Blessed those who have been persecuted for the sake of justice, because
theirs is the kingdom of the heavens.*

Words beyond the course list, glossed in order: **πενθέω** I mourn ·
**παρακαλέω** I comfort · **πραΰς** gentle, mild · **κληρονομέω** I inherit
· **πεινάω** I hunger · **διψάω** I thirst · **χορτάζω** I fill, I feed ·
**ἐλεήμων** merciful · **ὁράω** I see (future **ὄψομαι**) ·
**εἰρηνοποιός** peacemaker · **καλέω** I call · **διώκω** I persecute ·
**ἕνεκεν** for the sake of (with the genitive).

**Now look at the shape.** Eight lines; every one is *blessed — the group —
because — they*, and six of them end *they will*; the first and last end
*theirs is*, the same words, which
closes the whole thing like a bracket. This is built to be remembered by
people who could not read, and it was, and you are the proof.

**And look at the fourth line.** **τὴν δικαιοσύνην** — *justice*, from
Lesson 17, the thing Socrates was asked about. Here it is something you can
hunger for. The word has not changed. What people wanted from it has.

**And the eighth.** **ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης** — the same word, with no article.
*For the sake of justice*, not *of the justice*. Four lessons ago you learned
to notice an article's absence. This is what it is for.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text follows the standard Greek New
Testament (see *Sources*); check each verse against the edition in use, especially
the readings at 5:4–5, where some editions reverse the order of the two
lines, and the form **κληρονομήσουσιν**.

**On Theodosius.** His discovery that the Greek is *lower* than the Latin,
not higher, is the point of the scene and should not be softened. He came
looking for the Greek to confirm his reverence and it confronted him with a
beggar instead. That is a better outcome than confirmation, and he is old
enough to be told so.

**On the participles.** Three of the eight lines use them. Do not teach the
participle here; give the rule of thumb in the grammar section and let the
class read past them. Lesson 22 will build the form, and a class that has
already met four of them in a passage they know by heart will find the
lesson easy.
:::

μακάριοί ἐστε ὑμεῖς, ὅτε οἱ κακοὶ κακὰ λέγουσι περὶ ὑμῶν· ὁ γὰρ μισθὸς
ὑμῶν μέγας ἐστὶν ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς.

**The original** *(Matthew 5:1–12, NA28)*

Ἰδὼν δὲ τοὺς ὄχλους ἀνέβη εἰς τὸ ὄρος, καὶ καθίσαντος αὐτοῦ προσῆλθαν
αὐτῷ οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ· καὶ ἀνοίξας τὸ στόμα αὐτοῦ ἐδίδασκεν αὐτοὺς λέγων·

Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.
μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται. μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς,
ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν. μακάριοι οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες
τὴν δικαιοσύνην, ὅτι αὐτοὶ χορτασθήσονται. μακάριοι οἱ ἐλεήμονες, ὅτι
αὐτοὶ ἐλεηθήσονται. μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ, ὅτι αὐτοὶ τὸν θεὸν
ὄψονται. μακάριοι οἱ εἰρηνοποιοί, ὅτι αὐτοὶ υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται.
μακάριοι οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία
τῶν οὐρανῶν.

μακάριοί ἐστε ὅταν ὀνειδίσωσιν ὑμᾶς καὶ διώξωσιν καὶ εἴπωσιν πᾶν πονηρὸν
καθ᾽ ὑμῶν [ψευδόμενοι] ἕνεκεν ἐμοῦ. χαίρετε καὶ ἀγαλλιᾶσθε, ὅτι ὁ μισθὸς
ὑμῶν πολὺς ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς· οὕτως γὰρ ἐδίωξαν τοὺς προφήτας τοὺς πρὸ
ὑμῶν.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Ἰησοῦς** — Jesus *(genitive **Ἰησοῦ**)*
- **εἶδε** — saw *(aorist of **ὁράω**, I see; Lesson 22 has its participle)*
- **αὐτόν, αὐτούς, αὐτοῖς** — him, them, to them *(cases of this lesson's
  **αὐτός**)*
- **οἳ … ἔχουσιν** — *(the people)* who have … *(the relative with no noun
  in front of it: Greek lets it stand alone)*
- **εἰρήνην ἄγουσιν** — keep peace *(**ἡ εἰρήνη**, peace; the Attic idiom)*
- **ὑμεῖς, ὑμῶν, ὑμᾶς** — you *(plural)*; of you; you *(accusative)*
- **ὅτε** — when
- **ὁ μισθός** — wages, pay; reward
- **Ἰδών** — having seen *(aorist participle, Lesson 22)*
- **ἀνέβη** — went up *(aorist of **ἀναβαίνω**, Lesson 23)*
- **καθίσαντος αὐτοῦ** — when he had sat down *(two genitives making a
  clause of their own; take the phrase whole)*
- **προσῆλθαν** — came to *(a Koine spelling of **προσῆλθον**: the aorist
  of Lesson 14 with the ending of the first aorist)*
- **ἀνοίξας** — having opened *(aorist participle)*
- **τὸ στόμα** — mouth
- **λέγων** — saying *(present participle)*
- **ὅταν ὀνειδίσωσιν … διώξωσιν … εἴπωσιν** — whenever they insult …
  persecute … say *(forms you meet in Lesson 35; take the three whole)*
- **πᾶν πονηρόν** — every evil thing *(**πονηρός**, wicked)*
- **καθ᾽ ὑμῶν** — against you *(**κατά** with the genitive)*
- **[ψευδόμενοι]** — lying, falsely *(the brackets are the editors': the
  word is doubtful)*
- **ἕνεκεν ἐμοῦ** — for my sake
- **χαίρετε καὶ ἀγαλλιᾶσθε** — rejoice and be glad *(two commands; Lesson
  26)*
- **πολύς** — much, great *(the singular of **πολλοί**, Lesson 7)*
- **ἐδίωξαν** — they persecuted *(aorist of **διώκω**, I pursue)*
- **τοὺς πρὸ ὑμῶν** — the ones before you *(**πρό**, before, with the
  genitive)*
- For lines 3–10, see The Ancient Voice.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted text aloud. Then read the original aloud, and listen for
where it is longer.

1. In the adapted text, who is the subject of **εἶδε** and **ἦλθεν**, and
   who is the subject of **ἦλθον**? What tells you the number changed?
2. The original begins **Ἰδὼν δὲ τοὺς ὄχλους ἀνέβη.** Where has the
   adapted text's first verb, **εἶδε**, gone?
3. **καθίσαντος αὐτοῦ.** What case are both words? What does that tell you
   about who is the subject of **προσῆλθαν**?
4. **ἐδίδασκεν** — what tense, and what does it say about the teaching
   that **ἐδίδαξεν** would not?
5. Where the original has **οἱ πενθοῦντες** and **οἱ πεινῶντες**, the
   adapted text has clauses with **οἵ**. What did the adaptation have to
   add, and how many words did the participle save each time?
6. The adapted text has **οἱ μικροί** for **οἱ πραεῖς**. Is that a
   translation or a substitution? What is lost?
7. Where the original promises with futures (**παρακληθήσονται**,
   **χορτασθήσονται**), the adapted text has **δίδωσιν**. What does a
   blessing lose when its *will* becomes *does*?
8. **μακάριοί ἐστε.** Why does **μακάριοι** carry two accents here and one
   everywhere else?
9. **ὁ μισθὸς ὑμῶν πολὺς ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς.** Which word is missing, and
   how do you know?
10. **τοὺς προφήτας τοὺς πρὸ ὑμῶν.** What is the second **τούς** doing?
    Where did you first meet that device?
:::

::: answers
1. Jesus (**ὁ Ἰησοῦς**, singular) for the first two; the disciples (**οἱ
   μαθηταί**, plural) for the third. The ending: **ἦλθε(ν)** third
   singular, **ἦλθον** third plural — the same letters that in the first
   person mean *I came* (Lesson 13's warning).
2. Into **Ἰδών**, a participle: *having seen*. One main verb, **ἀνέβη**,
   with the seeing hung from it. This is Lesson 22 a week early, and it
   is why the original is shorter than the adaptation.
3. Genitive, both. So **αὐτοῦ** is not the subject of **προσῆλθαν** — the
   disciples are (**οἱ μαθηταί**, nominative). The genitive pair is a
   clause of its own, *he having sat down*; the pupils will build it in
   Part V, and can recognise it now by its case.
4. Imperfect: *he was teaching*, *he began to teach* — the teaching goes
   on through everything that follows. **ἐδίδαξεν** would make it one act
   and shut the door on the sermon.
5. It had to add a noun or let **οἵ** stand alone, and a finite verb with
   an object (**κακὰ ἔχουσιν** for **πενθοῦντες**). The participle saves
   three words for **πενθοῦντες**; for **πεινῶντες** the adaptation is
   actually shorter, because it dropped *thirsting* — the participle's
   gain is that it keeps the group a single noun.
6. A substitution. **πραΰς** is *gentle*, *mild*, *not asserting oneself*;
   **μικρός** is *small*. The adaptation kept the lowliness and lost the
   gentleness — and the class had no word for gentleness, which is the
   honest reason.
7. Its promise. *They will be comforted* is said about a future that has
   not come; *God gives them good things* claims it is already so. The
   Beatitudes are addressed to people for whom it is visibly not yet so,
   and the future tense is where that is admitted.
8. Because **ἐστε** is an enclitic — a word with no accent of its own that
   leans on the word before it — and a word ending in two unaccented
   syllables before an enclitic takes a second accent on its last
   syllable. In **αὐτῶν ἐστιν** the enclitic has lost its accent just the
   same, but **αὐτῶν** is accented on its last syllable already and has
   nowhere to put a second one.
9. **ἐστίν**. Nominative subject with the article, nominative adjective
   without, no verb: *your reward [is] great* — the formula of this lesson
   with **πολύς** in the place of **μακάριος**.
10. It puts **πρὸ ὑμῶν** inside the noun's article group — the
    attributive position of Lesson 10 (**ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός**): *the
    prophets, the ones before you*, as a single description, not *the
    prophets [were] before you*.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. **μακάριος** is often translated *blessed*, which sounds religious, and
   sometimes *fortunate*, which sounds like luck. The Greek word is older
   than both. Which English word loses less?
2. The Latin softened **πτωχός** to *pauper*. Was that a mistake, a mercy, or
   a translator's limit?
3. Every line gives a reason with **ὅτι**. Would the blessings be stronger
   or weaker without their reasons?
:::

Felix, who had been counting, announced that **μακάριοι** appeared eight
times and that he could now say it in his sleep.

"You could say it in your sleep before," said Theodoros. "In Latin. What you
can do now is say what it means."

"It means *happy*."

"It means the state the gods are in, and the dead, and the beggar at the
gate, all at once. Find me a Latin word for that and I will give you the
afternoon off."

Felix thought about it for the rest of the lesson and did not get the
afternoon off.
