# The Sermon on the Mount

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"You all know this," said Theodoros. "Every one of you. You have known it
since before you could read."

He wrote the first line on the board, in Greek, and eight people said the
Latin under their breath before they had finished reading it.

*Beati pauperes spiritu.*

"Good. Now read the Greek. Not the Latin that is already in your head —
the Greek. And tell me what is different."

There was a long silence, of the kind that meant everybody was reading the
Latin.

Theodosius put his hand up. He had been expecting to find something
*holier* in the Greek, Theodoros suspected — something the Latin had
flattened — and had been looking forward to it since the start of Part IV.

"**πτωχοί**," he said.

"Yes."

"That is not *pauperes*. *Pauper* is — my uncle's clerk is *pauper*. He has
a room and a bed. **πτωχός** is — " He stopped, and looked at the word
again. "It is the one who crouches. The one with his hand out. It is the man
at the gate."

"Yes."

"The Greek is *harsher*."

"The Greek is harsher. There is a Greek word for *poor* in your clerk's
sense, and it is **πένης**, and it is not the word here. The word here is
the beggar." Theodoros let it sit. "You expected the Greek to be loftier
than the Latin. It is lower. It goes further down than the Latin dared to
go, and the Latin, which you have said ten thousand times, has been softening
it for you the whole time."

Theodosius did not say anything for a while.

"That is not what I expected to find," he said at last.

"No. That is what I was hoping you would say." Theodoros turned back to the
board. "Eight lines. Every line has a word like that in it. Let us find
them."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The blessing formula

**μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί.** Two words, no verb.

You met this shape in Lesson 3, and again in the *Apology* in Lesson 17:
Greek does not need **ἐστίν** when the sentence is *X is Y*. Here it is
used as a formula. **μακάριος** goes first, in the nominative, agreeing with
whoever is being called blessed; then the article and the group. *Blessed
the poor.* The verb is understood, and the sentence is a pronouncement.

Each line then adds **ὅτι** — *because* — and a reason.

## ὅτι, again

In Lesson 16 you met **ὅτι** meaning *that*, introducing reported speech:
**λέγει ὅτι …**, *he says that …*. Here it means *because*. It is the same
word, and context decides, and you will meet both uses constantly in the
Gospels. A **ὅτι** after a blessing is *because*; a **ὅτι** after *he said*
is *that*.

## The article makes a noun out of anything

**οἱ πτωχοί** is an adjective with the article: *the poor ones*. You know
this from Lesson 10.

Some lines do the same thing with a form you have not had yet:

**οἱ πενθοῦντες** — *the mourning ones*, *those who mourn*. **πενθέω** is
*I mourn*, and **πενθοῦντες** is a **participle** — a verb turned into an
adjective — and the article turns the adjective into a noun. *The ones
mourning.* Participles are the subject of the next lesson. For now, treat
**οἱ** + a word ending in **-οντες** or **-οῦντες** as *the people who are
doing that*, and move on.

## The dative of respect

**οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι** — *the poor in spirit*. The dative says *in what
respect*: poor *as to* spirit. **οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ** — the pure *in
heart*. You know the dative from Lesson 9 as the case of *to* and *for*;
here it answers *in what way?*

## αὐτοί

Most of the reasons begin **ὅτι αὐτοί** — *because they*. Greek, you
remember, does not need a word for *they*; the verb ending carries it. When
the word is put in anyway, it is for emphasis. **αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται**:
*they*, and nobody else, will be comforted. Each line is saying that the
blessing goes exactly to these and not to the others.

::: note
**The futures.** You will see **-σουσιν** and **-θήσονται** on the ends of
verbs in this passage. They are future tense, active and passive — *they
will inherit*, *they will be comforted*. You can recognise them by the
**σ**, which is the future's mark in Greek as it was the aorist's. You are
not asked to produce them.
:::

::: latinbridge
**The Latin is not wrong. It is gentler.** *Beati pauperes spiritu* is a
faithful rendering by a translator who had no Latin word for **πτωχός** and
chose the nearest. Every line has a choice like that in it. *Mites* for
**πραεῖς**; *misericordes* for **ἐλεήμονες**; *mundo corde* for **καθαροὶ τῇ
καρδίᾳ** — the dative of respect carried straight across into the Latin
ablative, which is the Latin that does that job.

The lesson is not that the Latin is bad. It is that you have been reading
the Latin as though it were the thing itself, and it is a translation, with
a translator's choices in it, and now you can see them.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **μακάριος** — blessed, fortunate; beyond the reach of trouble
- **ὁ πτωχός** — beggar; destitute *(not merely poor)*
- **ἡ βασιλεία** — kingdom, kingship
- **ἡ γῆ** — earth, land
- **καθαρός** — clean, pure
- **ἡ καρδία** — heart
- **τὸ πνεῦμα** — breath; wind; spirit *(neuter; dative **τῷ πνεύματι**)*
- **ὁ οὐρανός** — heaven, sky *(Lesson 2; the Gospel uses the plural,
  **οἱ οὐρανοί**)*

::: note
**μακάριος** is older than any religion in this room. In Homer **οἱ
μάκαρες**, *the blessed ones*, are the gods — the beings whom nothing can
touch. The word is also used of the dead, who are past harm. It does not
mean *lucky*; luck can turn. It means *in a state that trouble cannot
reach*. That is what is being said of the beggar and the mourner, and it is
a startling thing to say.

**τὸ πνεῦμα** is *breath* first, *wind* second, and *spirit* third, and all
three are alive in the Gospel's use of it. Latin *spiritus* has the same
range, which is why you did not notice.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros took the first four lines and made them say, for each one, what
the Latin had changed.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ.**
2. **μακάριος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
4. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ γῆ.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Blessed are the pure in heart.* — no verb; **τῇ καρδίᾳ** dative of
   respect.
2. *Blessed is the man who speaks the truth.* — composed for the lesson.
   **ὅς** from Lesson 17; **μακάριος** singular agreeing with **ἄνθρωπος**.
3. *The kingdom of heaven is a gift.* — **τῶν οὐρανῶν** is plural: *of the
   heavens*. The Gospel's idiom is plural throughout, and a pupil who
   translates it singular has done what every English Bible does, and
   should know they have done it.
4. *Theirs is the earth.* — **αὐτῶν** genitive, *of them*; the Gospel's own
   word order, with the possessor first for emphasis.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
**μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.**

1. Which word tells you there is no verb in the first half, and how?
2. What does **ὅτι** mean here, and what else could it mean? What decides?
3. Why is **αὐτοί** there at all, when the verb ending already says *they*?
:::

::: answers
1. No single word does; the *absence* tells you. **μακάριοι** is nominative
   plural and so is **οἱ πενθοῦντες**, and there is nothing else, so the
   sentence must be *X [is] Y*, with **ἐστίν** understood. This is Lesson 3
   working again.
2. *Because.* It could mean *that*, as in Lesson 16; what decides is that
   there is no verb of saying or thinking for a *that* to hang from, and a
   blessing followed by a reason is the pattern of all eight lines.
3. Emphasis: *they* and not others. A pupil who says "it is just there" has
   missed the one thing in the sentence that is doing rhetorical work, and
   should be asked what the line would lose without it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Blessed are the pure.
2. Blessed is the man who speaks the truth.
3. The kingdom of the heavens is a gift.
4. Theirs is the earth.
:::

::: answers
1. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροί.** — no verb. A pupil who adds **εἰσίν** has
   written correct Greek and missed the formula.
2. **μακάριος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
4. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ γῆ.** — the Gospel's order. Accept **ἡ γῆ αὐτῶν ἐστιν**;
   ask what moved and why the Gospel put **αὐτῶν** first.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 5:3–10"}
Eight lines. You know every one in Latin. Read the Greek.

**μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.**
*Blessed the beggars in spirit, because theirs is the kingdom of the
heavens.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται.**
*Blessed those who mourn, because they will be comforted.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς, ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν.**
*Blessed the gentle, because they will inherit the earth.*

**μακάριοι οἱ πεινῶντες καὶ διψῶντες τὴν δικαιοσύνην, ὅτι αὐτοὶ
χορτασθήσονται.**
*Blessed those who hunger and thirst for justice, because they will be
filled.*

**μακάριοι οἱ ἐλεήμονες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ ἐλεηθήσονται.**
*Blessed the merciful, because they will be shown mercy.*

**μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ, ὅτι αὐτοὶ τὸν θεὸν ὄψονται.**
*Blessed the pure in heart, because they will see God.*

**μακάριοι οἱ εἰρηνοποιοί, ὅτι αὐτοὶ υἱοὶ θεοῦ κληθήσονται.**
*Blessed the peacemakers, because they will be called sons of God.*

**μακάριοι οἱ δεδιωγμένοι ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης, ὅτι αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία
τῶν οὐρανῶν.**
*Blessed those who have been persecuted for the sake of justice, because
theirs is the kingdom of the heavens.*

Words beyond the course list, glossed in order: **πενθέω** I mourn ·
**παρακαλέω** I comfort · **πραΰς** gentle, mild · **κληρονομέω** I inherit
· **πεινάω** I hunger · **διψάω** I thirst · **χορτάζω** I fill, I feed ·
**ἐλεήμων** merciful · **ὁράω** I see (future **ὄψομαι**) ·
**εἰρηνοποιός** peacemaker · **καλέω** I call · **διώκω** I persecute ·
**ἕνεκεν** for the sake of (with the genitive).

**Now look at the shape.** Eight lines; every one is *blessed — the group —
because — they — will*. The first and last end with the same words, which
closes the whole thing like a bracket. This is built to be remembered by
people who could not read, and it was, and you are the proof.

**And look at the fourth line.** **τὴν δικαιοσύνην** — *justice*, from
Lesson 17, the thing Socrates was asked about. Here it is something you can
hunger for. The word has not changed. What people wanted from it has.

**And the eighth.** **ἕνεκεν δικαιοσύνης** — the same word, with no article.
*For the sake of justice*, not *of the justice*. Four lessons ago you learned
to notice an article's absence. This is what it is for.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text is quoted from memory of the standard
Greek New Testament; check each verse against the edition in use, especially
the readings at 5:4–5, where some editions reverse the order of the two
lines, and the form **κληρονομήσουσιν**.

**On Theodosius.** His discovery that the Greek is *lower* than the Latin,
not higher, is the point of the scene and should not be softened. He came
looking for the Greek to confirm his reverence and it confronted him with a
beggar instead. That is a better outcome than confirmation, and he is old
enough to be told so.

**On the participles.** Three of the eight lines use them. Do not teach the
participle here; give the rule of thumb in the grammar section and let the
class read past them. Lesson 22 will build the form, and a class that has
already met four of them in a passage they know by heart will find the
lesson easy.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. **μακάριος** is often translated *blessed*, which sounds religious, and
   sometimes *fortunate*, which sounds like luck. The Greek word is older
   than both. Which English word loses less?
2. The Latin softened **πτωχός** to *pauper*. Was that a mistake, a mercy, or
   a translator's limit?
3. Every line gives a reason with **ὅτι**. Would the blessings be stronger
   or weaker without their reasons?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Felix, who had been counting, announced that **μακάριοι** appeared eight
times and that he could now say it in his sleep.

"You could say it in your sleep before," said Theodoros. "In Latin. What you
can do now is say what it means."

"It means *happy*."

"It means the state the gods are in, and the dead, and the beggar at the
gate, all at once. Find me a Latin word for that and I will give you the
afternoon off."

Felix thought about it for the rest of the lesson and did not get the
afternoon off.
:::
