# Attic and Koine Face to Face

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Felix had been waiting twenty-two lessons to be right about something, and
when it came he very nearly missed it.

Theodoros was reading from the Gospel of John, as he had been for a week,
and he had slipped — as he sometimes did when he was reading rather than
teaching — out of the careful, measured Greek he used in the classroom and
into something quicker, flatter, with the vowels run together.

Felix sat up.

"That is how you talk at home."

Theodoros stopped reading.

"I beg your pardon?"

"When you are angry. When you shouted at the carter last month. You said
**βλέπε** and **ὕπαγε** and **θέλω** — not **ἐθέλω** — and you said *and,
and, and*. You sounded like the Psalm. You sounded like *this*." He pointed
at the scroll. "You do not teach us that Greek. You teach us Plato's. But
that is the Greek you *speak*."

The silence was of a different quality from usual.

"Yes," said Theodoros, after a moment. "It is."

"Then why — "

"Because you cannot see a difference without a standard. I told you that in
Lesson 15, about Homer, and it is true of this as well." He put the scroll
down. "The Greek I grew up speaking in Antioch is closer to this Gospel than
to anything in Plato. It is the Greek of the harbour and the market and my
mother's kitchen. If I had taught you that first you would have learned it
well and never known it was one kind of Greek among several. I taught you
Attic so that when you met the common speech you would be able to *hear* it
as common speech — and Felix, who has the best ear in the room and the
worst habits, has just done exactly that."

Felix looked as if he did not know whether he had been praised.

"You have," said Theodoros. "It will not happen often. Enjoy it."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Two varieties, one language

**Attic** is the Greek of Athens in its great century, polished by Plato and
the orators into the standard every educated Greek afterwards learned to
write. **Koine** — *common* — is what happened to Greek when Alexander's
armies carried it from Egypt to India and millions of people whose first
language was something else learned to speak it. It is simpler in some ways,
looser in others, and it is the Greek of nearly everything written between
300 BC and AD 300 that was not trying to sound like Plato.

Neither is better. They are the same language at two moments, used by
different people for different ends. Here is where they part.

| | Attic | Koine |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Reported speech** | accusative + infinitive: **λέγει τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἀγαθὸν εἶναι** | **ὅτι** + ordinary verb: **λέγει ὅτι ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἀγαθός ἐστιν** |
| **Purpose** | **ἵνα**, **ὅπως** + subjunctive (a mood called the optative after a past tense) | **ἵνα** + a verb; the optative nearly gone |
| **Wish, command** | infinitive: **βούλομαι** + infinitive | **θέλω ἵνα** + a verb, or an infinitive |
| **Sentence shape** | long, subordinate, held together by **μέν … δέ, γάρ, οὖν** | short, coordinated, strung on **καί** and **δέ**; **μέν** rare |
| **Pronouns** | dropped — the ending carries them | often written: **ἐγώ**, **σύ**, **ὑμεῖς** |
| **The dative** | in full use | receding; **ἐν** and **εἰς** doing its work |
| **"I want"** | **βούλομαι**, **ἐθέλω** | **θέλω** |
| **"I see"** | **ὁράω**; **βλέπω** = *look at* | **βλέπω** = *see* |
| **"I go"** | **ἔρχομαι**, **εἶμι** | **ὑπάγω**, **πορεύομαι** |
| **"I speak"** | **λέγω** | **λέγω**, and **λαλέω**, which in Attic meant *chatter* |
| **Diminutives** | rare, affectionate | everywhere, and flat: **παιδίον** *child*, **πλοιάριον** *boat* |
| **Spelling** | **θάλαττα**, **πράττω** | **θάλασσα**, **πράσσω** |
| **The dual** | present | gone |

## Three to understand, not just notice

**One. ὅτι replaces the infinitive.** The construction you built in Lesson
16 — *he says the man to be good* — is still available in Koine but is
losing ground to **ὅτι** + an ordinary verb, which is how English does it
too. A Koine writer can do both. Attic writers strongly preferred the
infinitive.

**Two. ἵνα spreads.** In Attic prose **ἵνα** means *in order that* and
almost nothing else. In Koine it spreads to cover *that* after verbs of wanting, asking,
commanding — **θέλω ἵνα**, *I want that*, where Attic would have **βούλομαι**
plus an infinitive. You will meet it constantly in the Gospels. Recognise
it; the verb form after it is one you have not learned.

**Three. The dative is under pressure.** Attic says **πιστεύω τῷ θεῷ**; Koine
also says **πιστεύω εἰς τὸν θεόν**. Attic says **ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ** and so does
Koine, but Koine says it more, because the bare dative is retreating and
prepositions are moving in to hold its ground. This is a language in the
middle of a change you can watch.

::: note
**Why the change?** Because Koine was being spoken by people learning it
as adults. Second-language speakers simplify: they drop forms with no
obvious job (the dual), they prefer one way of saying a thing to three (the
optative goes), they say the pronoun because the ending is hard to hear in a
noisy market. Attic was a language of native speakers writing for one
another. Koine was a language of everyone.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin had the same two registers and you already know both.** Cicero's
periods and the Vulgate's *et … et … et* are the Latin of the senate and the
Latin of the street. The Old Latin translators chose the street on
purpose, and Jerome, revising them, kept it, for the same reason the
Gospel writers did: so that the people the book was for could
read it.

So you are not learning a new distinction. You are learning that Greek has
it too, and learning to hear it — which Felix, who has never once construed
a Ciceronian period correctly, can apparently do by instinct.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **θέλω** — I want *(Koine; Attic **ἐθέλω**, Lesson 11)*
- **λαλέω** — I speak, I talk *(Koine; in Attic, "I chatter")*
- **ὑπάγω** — I go, I go away *(Koine)*
- **ἵνα** — in order that; *(Koine)* that
- **ἡ θάλασσα** — sea *(Attic **θάλαττα**)*
- **τὸ παιδίον** — child, little child *(a diminutive of **παῖς**)*
- **ἀναβαίνω** — I go up *(aorist **ἀνέβη**, he went up — Matthew's verb
  in Lesson 21; participle **ἀναβαίνων**)*
- **καταβαίνω** — I go down *(imperfect **κατέβαινεν**, Lesson 22's first
  verb)*
- **ἐπεί** — when; since
- **ὡς** — as, like; *(after a verb of saying or accusing)* that, how
  *(**ὥσπερ**, Lesson 20, is **ὡς** made emphatic)*
- **ὁ στρατηγός** — general, commander *(**στρατός**, army, + **ἄγω**)*
- **ὁ Ἕλλην** — a Greek *(genitive **Ἕλληνος**; plural **οἱ Ἕλληνες**)*
- **ὁ ἄρχων** — ruler, commander *(genitive **ἄρχοντος**; a participle of
  **ἄρχω**, I rule, turned into a noun — compare **ἡ ἀρχή**, Lesson 6)*
- **ἀγαπητός** — beloved *(the adjective of **ἀγαπάω**, Lesson 19)*

::: note
Every word in the first six of this list is a word you could have met in Attic with a
slightly different shape or sense. None is new in the way **σπλαγχνίζομαι**
was new. That is what a change of variety looks like from the inside: not
new words, mostly, but old ones that have shifted a step.

The other eight belong to both Greeks without change. **ἀναβαίνω** opens
Xenophon's march and Mark's baptism in the same breath; **ὡς** is in both
of this lesson's readings, doing slightly different work; **ὁ ἄρχων** is
what Lesson 22's participle looks like once it has settled down and become
a noun.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="20 min"}
Theodoros gave them sentences and asked them to say, for each, *which Greek
it was* — and what told them. But first the ten from memory, in which the
two Greeks were already mixed, as they had been all along.

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From Lessons 19 to 22. Answer from memory.

1. **ὁ ἱερεὺς ἰδὼν τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἀντιπαρῆλθεν.** Which word is the
   participle? Present or aorist? Translate.
2. **ἦγεν** and **ἤγαγεν** — which is imperfect and which aorist? Translate
   both.
3. **πιστεύω εἰς τὸν θεόν.** Which Greek says it this way? Say it the
   Attic way.
4. **ἐπὶ τῆς τραπέζης** and **ἐπὶ τὴν τράπεζαν.** Translate both. What has
   the case changed?
5. **μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες.** How do you know **πενθοῦντες** is not a
   verb?
6. **αὐτῶν ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν.** Translate. Why is **οὐρανῶν**
   plural?
7. **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος.** Translate. What has the article done to the
   participle?
8. Give the genitive of **ὁ ἱερεύς**, **ἡ πίστις**, **ὁ αἰών**.
9. **γιγνώσκομεν ὅτι ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.** Say it the Attic way, with the
   infinitive. Which one word in it would Plato not have known?
10. **οἱ λῃσταὶ ἀπῆλθον, ὁ δὲ ἀνὴρ ἔμενεν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ.** Which verb is
    aorist and which imperfect, and why has the writer chosen each?
:::

::: answers
1. **ἰδών**; aorist — *having seen*, the step before the passing by.
   *The priest, having seen the man, passed by on the other side.*
2. **ἦγεν** imperfect (*he was leading*; the augment on the present stem
   **ἀγ-**); **ἤγαγεν** aorist (*he led*; the doubled stem **ἀγαγ-** is
   the mark of this verb's aorist, Lesson 22's Voice). A pupil who
   calls **ἤγαγεν** the imperfect because it is longer has reasoned from
   length.
3. The New Testament's. **πιστεύω τῷ θεῷ** — the bare dative, Lesson 19.
4. *On the table* (where something is); *on to the table* (where it is
   going). Genitive for rest, accusative for motion — Lesson 20's **ἐπί**.
5. The article **οἱ** in front of it and the ending **-ντες**, which no
   finite verb has (Lesson 21, now Lesson 22). *Blessed are those who
   mourn.*
6. *Theirs is the kingdom of the heavens.* Because the Gospel's idiom is
   plural: Hebrew says *heavens*, and the Greek followed it (Lesson 21).
7. *The one who did the mercy.* Turned it into a noun — the lawyer's
   answer, which names the Samaritan by his act.
8. **ἱερέως**, **πίστεως**, **αἰῶνος**.
9. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν θεὸν ἀγάπην εἶναι.** — the subject of the infinitive
   goes into the accusative, and so does what is said of it. **ἀγάπη**,
   the word that barely exists outside the Greek Bible (Lesson 19); Plato
   would have written **φιλία** or **ἔρως** and meant something else.
10. **ἀπῆλθον** aorist — the bandits leave, once, and are gone;
    **ἔμενεν** imperfect — the man stays, and goes on staying, so that the
    priest can find him there. The aorist closes a door; the imperfect
    leaves one open for the next person to walk through.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Attic or Koine?"}
1. **λέγει ὅτι ὁ διδάσκαλος ἀγαθός ἐστιν.**
2. **λέγει τὸν διδάσκαλον ἀγαθὸν εἶναι.**
3. **ἐγὼ θέλω μανθάνειν.**
4. **ὁ μὲν ἀνὴρ ἐθέλει μανθάνειν, ὁ δὲ παῖς οὔ.**
5. **ἡ θάλαττα μεγάλη ἐστίν.**
6. **ὁ Ἰησοῦς ὑπάγει εἰς τὴν Γαλιλαίαν.**
7. **ὁ στρατηγὸς τοὺς Ἕλληνας ἄγει ἐπὶ τὴν θάλατταν.**
8. **θέλω ἵνα τὸ παιδίον μανθάνῃ.** *(**μανθάνῃ** is the form **μανθάνει**
   takes after **ἵνα**)*
9. **ἐπεὶ δὲ ἀπῆλθεν ὁ ἱερεύς, ὁ Σαμαρίτης ἦλθεν· ἐλθὼν δὲ εἶδε τὸν
   ἄνθρωπον.**
10. **καὶ ἐλάλησεν αὐτοῖς, καὶ πάντες ἤκουσαν αὐτοῦ.**
:::

::: answers
1. Koine, most likely: **ὅτι** + verb for reported speech. (Attic *can*
   do this; it mostly does not.)
2. Attic: accusative and infinitive.
3. Koine: **θέλω** for **ἐθέλω**, and **ἐγώ** written out where the ending
   already says *I*.
4. Attic: **μέν … δέ**, **ἐθέλω**, no pronouns.
5. Attic, from the spelling **θάλαττα** alone. A Koine writer would have
   **θάλασσα**.
6. Koine: **ὑπάγω** for *go*, which in Attic meant *lead away* or
   *withdraw*; and the subject is a Gospel's.
7. Attic, from **θάλατταν**; everything else in the sentence — **ἄγω**,
   **ἐπί** with the accusative, the article — is common to both. One
   letter decides. Note the box's **στρατηγός** and **Ἕλληνες** at work.
8. Koine: **θέλω ἵνα** where Attic would have **βούλομαι** with an
   infinitive — and **παιδίον**, the flat diminutive.
9. Neither can be told — and the pupil should say so. **ἐπεί**, the
   aorist, the participle **ἐλθών** hanging from **εἶδε**, **δέ** in
   second place: every feature is common property. Xenophon and Luke both
   write sentences like this one. The exercise's title has an answer
   missing from it, and that is the lesson.
10. Koine: **λαλέω** for *speak*, **καί … καί** stringing two clauses
    where Attic would subordinate one, and **αὐτοῖς**, **αὐτοῦ** — the
    pronouns said aloud. A pupil who adds *and **πάντες** without an
    article* has found a fourth mark, though not a decisive one.

Both varieties are grammatical Greek, and a pupil who says *this one is
wrong* about any of them should be stopped. The question is *which*, not
*whether*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
Here is the same idea twice.

- **βούλεται τὸν υἱὸν σοφὸν εἶναι.**
- **θέλει ἵνα ὁ υἱὸς σοφὸς ᾖ.**

*(**ᾖ** is a form of **εἰμί** you have not met; it means "be" after
**ἵνα**.)*

1. Translate both.
2. Name every feature that differs.
3. Which has more words, and which is easier to say aloud in a crowd?

Now a second pair, one from each of this lesson's readings.

- **ἀναβαίνει οὖν ὁ Κῦρος λαβὼν Τισσαφέρνην ὡς φίλον.**
- **καὶ εὐθὺς ἀναβαίνων ἐκ τοῦ ὕδατος εἶδεν τοὺς οὐρανούς.**

4. Both have a form of **ἀναβαίνω**. In which sentence is it the main verb,
   and in which the participle? Who goes up, in each?
5. **ἀναβαίνει** is present, but Cyrus went up some thirty years before
   Xenophon wrote. What is Xenophon doing? Would Mark do it?
6. **οὖν** in the first; **καὶ εὐθύς** in the second. What does each
   connector tell you about the sentence before it?

And a third.

- **πιστεύω τῷ θεῷ.**
- **πιστεύω εἰς τὸν θεόν.**

7. Translate both. Which case has gone, and what has come in to do its
   work?
8. The second looks like the Latin *credo in Deum*. Does that make the
   Koine Latin-influenced, or the Latin Greek-influenced — or neither?

And a fourth, the shortest.

- **ὁ παῖς εἶπεν.**
- **τὸ παιδίον ἐλάλησεν.**

9. Name every feature that differs.
10. What did **λαλέω** mean in Attic? What would an Athenian have heard in
    the second sentence that a Galilean would not?
:::

::: answers
1. Both: *He wants his son to be wise.*
2. **βούλεται** / **θέλει**; infinitive / **ἵνα** + verb; the son in the
   accusative as subject of the infinitive / the son in the nominative as
   subject of his own verb; **σοφόν** following the accusative / **σοφός**
   following the nominative.
3. The Koine has more words and is easier to hear: every word in it carries
   its own accent and its own ending, and the listener does not have to
   hold an accusative in mind waiting for the infinitive to arrive. That is
   not an accident. It is the whole history of the language in one
   sentence.
4. In Xenophon's it is the main verb, **ἀναβαίνει**, with **λαβών** hanging
   from it; Cyrus goes up, from his province towards the king. In Mark's
   it is the participle, **ἀναβαίνων**, hanging from **εἶδεν**; Jesus goes
   up, out of the water. Same verb, opposite jobs — and the Reading will
   show that both passages begin with a going-up.
5. Telling a past story in the present tense, to make it vivid — the
   *historic present*, which Attic narrative uses constantly and which the
   class should recognise by the mismatch between tense and sense. Mark
   does it too, more than any other Gospel: *and he says to them*. It is
   one of the few things the two Greeks share that English does not.
6. **οὖν** says *therefore*: what follows is a consequence of what went
   before, and the reader is expected to hold the argument. **καὶ εὐθύς**
   says *and at once*: what follows comes next, and the hearer need hold
   nothing. Attic connects by logic; Koine by sequence.
7. Both: *I trust God*, *I believe in God*. The dative has gone, and
   **εἰς** with the accusative has come in — the preposition doing the
   case's work (the table's row on the dative).
8. The second: the Latin *credo in Deum* was made by Christians
   translating this Greek, so the Latin is Greek-influenced; the Greek
   itself grew out of the Septuagint's Hebrew. The resemblance to Latin
   is real and tells you about the translators, not about Koine. A pupil
   who says "it is just like Latin, so it must be later" has the
   direction of influence backwards.
9. **παῖς** / **παιδίον** — the plain noun against the diminutive, which in
   Koine has gone flat and simply means *child*; **εἶπεν** / **ἐλάλησεν**
   — Attic's verb of saying against Koine's **λαλέω**; **ὁ** / **τό** —
   the gender has changed with the ending, because diminutives in **-ιον**
   are neuter. Three differences in three words.
10. *Chatter*, *babble* — the noise of birds and children. An Athenian
    would have heard *the little one babbled*, faintly comic; a Galilean
    heard *the child spoke*, which is what it means in every Gospel. Same
    word, two centuries, a step sideways.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek, twice"}
Write each sentence in Attic and then in Koine.

1. He says that the man is good.
2. I want to learn.
3. The sea is great.
4. You will know the truth. *(use **γνώσεσθε**, the future of **γιγνώσκω**)*
5. The child goes away.
6. He says that the sea is great.
7. I spoke to the children.
8. The general wants the Greeks to go up.
9. The man was going down the road, and the bandits saw him.
10. We know that the Greeks are wise.
:::

::: answers
1. Attic **λέγει τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἀγαθὸν εἶναι.** Koine **λέγει ὅτι ὁ ἄνθρωπος
   ἀγαθός ἐστιν.**
2. Attic **βούλομαι μανθάνειν.** Koine **θέλω μανθάνειν.** — Koine keeps
   the infinitive here; **ἵνα** is an alternative, not a requirement.
3. Attic **ἡ θάλαττα μεγάλη ἐστίν.** Koine **ἡ θάλασσα μεγάλη ἐστίν.** — one
   sound's difference, and a Greek of the fourth century AD would have
   heard it as the difference between a book and a person.
4. **γνώσεσθε τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — the same in both. Not everything changed,
   and the class should notice that this sentence, which is John 8:32, is
   Greek that Plato would have written without altering a letter.
5. Attic **ὁ παῖς ἀπέρχεται.** Koine **τὸ παιδίον ὑπάγει.** — two words
   changed out of three, and the article with them. Accept **ἀπέρχεται**
   in the Koine too; **ὑπάγει** is the Gospels' favourite, not their only
   word.
6. Attic **λέγει τὴν θάλατταν μεγάλην εἶναι.** Koine **λέγει ὅτι ἡ
   θάλασσα μεγάλη ἐστίν.** — the pupil must change the construction *and*
   the spelling; one without the other is half a sentence in each Greek.
7. Attic **εἶπον τοῖς παισίν.** Koine **ἐλάλησα τοῖς παιδίοις.** — the
   dative plural of **παῖς** is **παισί(ν)**, with the **δ** gone before
   **σ**, as in **ποιμέσι**; that is the error to expect.
8. Attic **ὁ στρατηγὸς βούλεται τοὺς Ἕλληνας ἀναβαίνειν.** Koine **ὁ
   στρατηγὸς θέλει τοὺς Ἕλληνας ἀναβαίνειν**, or **θέλει ἵνα οἱ Ἕλληνες
   ἀναβαίνωσιν** (the **ἵνα**-form of **ἀναβαίνουσιν**; give it if asked).
   **Ἕλληνας** accusative plural of the third declension, from the box.
9. Attic **τὸν ἄνδρα καταβαίνοντα οἱ λῃσταὶ εἶδον.** Koine **καὶ ὁ
   ἄνθρωπος κατέβαινεν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ, καὶ εἶδον αὐτὸν οἱ λῃσταί.** — the
   Attic hangs the going-down from the seeing as a participle, in the
   accusative because it agrees with the man who is seen; the Koine
   gives it a clause of its own and strings the two on **καί**, and says
   **αὐτόν** where the Attic does not need to. **ἀνήρ** against
   **ἄνθρωπος** is the Koine's preference, not a rule; accept either in
   either. This is Luke 10:30's own shape.
10. Attic **γιγνώσκομεν τοὺς Ἕλληνας σοφοὺς εἶναι.** Koine **γινώσκομεν ὅτι
    οἱ Ἕλληνες σοφοί εἰσιν.** — and the Koine spelling of the verb,
    **γινώσκω** without its second **γ** (Lesson 19's gloss), is a third
    mark if the pupil remembers it. Reward the pupil who does.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Plato, *Apology* 38a; John 8:32"}
Two sentences about truth and how to live, nearly five hundred years
apart, each in its own Greek.

**ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.**

You read this in Lesson 17. Attic: no verb, a postpositive **δέ** tying it
to an argument, a dative of the person at the end, a long adjective built
with the **ἀ-** prefix. It is a sentence that expects to be read slowly by
somebody who has just read the sentence before it.

**γνώσεσθε τὴν ἀλήθειαν, καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια ἐλευθερώσει ὑμᾶς.**

- **γνώσεσθε** — you will know *(future of **γιγνώσκω**, Lesson 5; plural)*
- **τὴν ἀλήθειαν** — the truth *(Lesson 4)*
- **καί** — and
- **ἡ ἀλήθεια** — the truth, again, now as subject
- **ἐλευθερώσει** — will set free *(future; **ἐλεύθερος** is *free*)*
- **ὑμᾶς** — you *(accusative plural)*

*You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.*

Koine: two short clauses joined by **καί** where Attic might have
subordinated one to the other; the key word repeated rather than replaced
by a relative; verbs in the future, plain and concrete. It is
a sentence that expects to be heard once, by a crowd, and remembered.

**Now set them side by side.** Both are about truth and a life. Both are
short. Both have outlived everything around them.

Socrates says that a life without examining is not a life. John says that
knowing the truth frees you. Those are not the same claim, and the class
will be tempted to say they are. Socrates's good is in the *examining* —
the activity, the daily questioning, never finished. John's good is in a
truth that can be *known*, arrived at, and that then does something to you.
One is a process with no end; the other is a result. You may think both are
true. You may not say they are one thought.

**And listen to the Greek.** The Attic sentence is built like an argument:
hinge, subject, denial, qualification. The Koine sentence is built like a
promise: you will, and it will. Each variety is doing what it was shaped to
do, by the people who shaped it.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** Both quotations follow the standard texts (see
*Sources*) and should be checked against the editions in use. John 8:32
is stable across editions; confirm the accent on **ἐλευθερώσει**.

**On the comparison.** The temptation to harmonise Socrates and John is
strong and should be resisted in front of the class, because Lesson 24
depends on their having seen it resisted. The two sentences are a good pair
precisely because they *sound* alike and are not. If a pupil insists that
they agree, ask them to say what Socrates would make of a truth that could
be known once and for all, and let them discover he would have examined it.

**On Felix.** His vindication is real and should be given its weight.
Twenty-two lessons of being the pupil who guesses from sound have ended with
sound being, for once, exactly the right instrument. Do not undercut it with
a reminder of his usual habits; the story does that already.
:::

::: {.reading time="20 min" title="Two beginnings" source="Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.1.1–3 (Marchant's Oxford text) and Mark 1:9–11 (NA28), each with an adapted tier"}
Two openings, four hundred and fifty years apart. Xenophon begins the story
of a march with a king's two sons; Mark begins the story of a ministry with
a man coming up out of a river. Both begin with a going-up — **ἀνέβη**,
**ἀναβαίνων** — and after that they could not be less alike. Read each
first in the Greek you have, then as its author wrote it, and keep the
table from The New Greek open beside you.

**Xenophon, adapted** *(composed for this lesson from Anabasis 1.1.1–3, in
the words and forms you have)*

Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος δύο παῖδες ἦσαν, ὁ μὲν πρῶτος Ἀρταξέρξης, ὁ δὲ
νέος Κῦρος. ἐπεὶ δὲ Δαρεῖος κακῶς εἶχε καὶ τὸ τέλος τοῦ βίου ἔβλεπεν,
ἤθελε τοὺς δύο παῖδας πρὸς αὐτὸν ἐλθεῖν. ὁ μὲν οὖν Ἀρταξέρξης ἐκεῖ ἦν·
τῷ δὲ Κύρῳ ἐπιστολὴν ἔπεμψεν ἀπὸ τῆς ἀρχῆς· ἐκείνης γὰρ ἄρχοντα αὐτὸν
ἐποίησεν. ὁ οὖν Κῦρος ἀνέβη πρὸς τὸν πατέρα, ἔχων Ἕλληνας τριακοσίους καὶ
ἄρχοντα αὐτῶν Ξενίαν.

ἐπεὶ δὲ Δαρεῖος οὐκέτι ἦν καὶ Ἀρταξέρξης τὴν βασιλείαν εἶχε, Τισσαφέρνης
κακὰ εἶπε περὶ τοῦ Κύρου πρὸς τὸν ἀδελφόν· ἔλεγε γὰρ τὸν Κῦρον κακὰ
ἐθέλειν ποιεῖν αὐτόν. ὁ δὲ ἐπίστευσε καὶ ἔλαβε τὸν Κῦρον, ἀποκτείνειν
ἐθέλων· ἡ δὲ μήτηρ πολλὰ εἰποῦσα ἔλαβε τὸν υἱὸν ἀπὸ τοῦ θανάτου καὶ πάλιν
ἔπεμψεν αὐτὸν εἰς τὴν ἀρχήν.

**Xenophon, the original** *(Anabasis 1.1.1–3, Marchant)*

Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος γίγνονται παῖδες δύο, πρεσβύτερος μὲν Ἀρταξέρξης,
νεώτερος δὲ Κῦρος· ἐπεὶ δὲ ἠσθένει Δαρεῖος καὶ ὑπώπτευε τελευτὴν τοῦ βίου,

**Mark, adapted** *(composed for this lesson from Mark 1:9–11, in the words
and forms you have)*

καὶ ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις ἦλθεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς ἀπὸ Ναζαρὲτ τῆς Γαλιλαίας πρὸς

**Mark, the original** *(Mark 1:9–11, NA28)*

::: gloss
- **Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος** — of Darius and Parysatis *(the Persian king
  and queen; genitives: the sons are theirs)*
- **Ἀρταξέρξης, Κῦρος** — Artaxerxes, Cyrus *(the two sons; accusative
  **Κῦρον**, dative **Κύρῳ**)*
- **Τισσαφέρνης** — Tissaphernes, a Persian governor
- **κακῶς εἶχε** — was ill *(the Attic idiom: **ἔχω** with an adverb,
  literally "was having badly")*
- **ἐλθεῖν** — to come *(aorist infinitive of **ἦλθον**)*
- **ἐκείνης … ἄρχοντα** — ruler of it *(**ἄρχων** takes the genitive of
  what is ruled)*
- **τὸν πατέρα** — his father *(**ὁ πατήρ**, Lesson 28)*
- **τριακοσίους** — three hundred
- **Ξενίαν** — Xenias, a Greek captain *(**ξένος** inside the name)*
- **τὸν ἀδελφόν** — his brother *(**ὁ ἀδελφός**, Lesson 28)*
- **ποιεῖν** — to do *(present infinitive of **ποιέω**, a contract verb,
  Lesson 34; take it whole)*
- **ἀποκτείνειν** — to kill *(**ἀποκτείνω**)*
- **ἡ μήτηρ** — his mother *(Lesson 28)*
- **γίγνονται** — are born *(a present of **γίγνομαι**, whose aorist you
  know as **ἐγένετο**; a present tense telling a past story — see
  exercise 2)*
- **πρεσβύτερος, νεώτερος** — the elder, the younger *(**-τερος** makes a
  comparison; **νέος** is Lesson 10's)*
- **ἠσθένει** — was ill *(imperfect of **ἀσθενέω**, I am weak)*
- **ὑπώπτευε τελευτήν** — suspected the end *(imperfect of **ὑποπτεύω**;
  **ἡ τελευτή**, end, is the noun behind **ἐτελεύτησε** below)*
- **ἐβούλετο** — he wanted *(imperfect of **βούλομαι**, Lesson 16)*
- **τὼ παῖδε ἀμφοτέρω** — both his sons *(the **dual**: a number for
  exactly two, which Attic still has and Koine lost — the table's last
  row)*
- **παρεῖναι** — to be present *(infinitive of **πάρειμι**: **παρά** +
  **εἰμί**)*
- **παρὼν ἐτύγχανε** — happened to be present *(participle of **πάρειμι**
  with **τυγχάνω**, I happen)*
- **μεταπέμπεται** — sends for *(a middle form of **πέμπω**, Lesson 31;
  another present for the past)*
- **ἧς** — of which *(the relative has been pulled into the case of the
  word before it; read it as **ἥν**)*
- **σατράπην** — satrap: a Persian provincial governor
- **ἀπέδειξε** — appointed *(aorist of **ἀποδείκνυμι**)*
- **πάντων ὅσοι** — of all who *(**ὅσος**, as many as)*
- **ἐς Καστωλοῦ πεδίον** — to the plain of Castolus *(**ἐς** is the older
  spelling of **εἰς**, which Xenophon keeps here)*
- **ἁθροίζονται** — muster, are gathered *(passive)*
- **λαβών** — having taken *(aorist participle of **λαμβάνω**: **ἔλαβον**,
  Lesson 14)*
- **ὁπλίτας** — hoplites, heavy-armed infantry
- **Παρράσιον** — of Parrhasia, in Arcadia
- **ἐτελεύτησε** — died *(aorist of **τελευτάω**, I come to an end)*
- **κατέστη εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν** — was established in the kingship
  *(aorist of **καθίστημι**; Lesson 36)*
- **διαβάλλει** — slanders *(the verb behind **διάβολος**, the slanderer —
  the devil; another present for the past)*
- **ὡς ἐπιβουλεύοι αὐτῷ** — saying that he was plotting against him
  *(**ὡς** + a mood called the optative, which Koine lost; take the phrase
  whole)*
- **πείθεται** — is persuaded, believes it *(middle of **πείθω**, Lesson
  26)*
- **συλλαμβάνει** — arrests *(**σύν** + **λαμβάνω**: takes him together)*
- **ὡς ἀποκτενῶν** — meaning to kill him *(**ὡς** + a future participle of
  **ἀποκτείνω**: purpose; take it whole)*
- **ἐξαιτησαμένη** — having begged him off *(aorist middle participle of
  **ἐξαιτέομαι**)*
- **ἀποπέμπει** — sends back *(**ἀπό** + **πέμπω**, Lesson 9)*
- **Ναζαρὲτ τῆς Γαλιλαίας** — Nazareth of Galilee *(the town, then its
  region in the genitive)*
- **Ἰωάννης** — John, the Baptist *(accusative **Ἰωάννην**, genitive
  **Ἰωάννου**)*
- **ἐβάπτισεν** — dipped, baptised *(aorist of **βαπτίζω**)*
- **ὁ Ἰορδάνης** — the Jordan
- **περιστεράν** — dove
- **ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν** — on to him *(**ἐπί** with the accusative, Lesson 20)*
- **μου** — my *(genitive of **ἐγώ**, with no accent of its own)*
- **ἐν σοί** — in you *(**σοί**, dative of **σύ**; it keeps its accent
  after a preposition)*
- **ἐγένετο … ἦλθεν** — it came to pass … came *(Lesson 14's **ἐγένετο**
  opening the sentence as the Septuagint does, Lesson 20)*
- **ἐβαπτίσθη** — was baptised *(aorist passive of **βαπτίζω**; Lesson 31)*
- **εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην** — in the Jordan *(**εἰς** where Attic would write
  **ἐν** — the table's row on the dative)*
- **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου** — by John *(**ὑπό** with the genitive marks who did it)*
- **σχιζομένους** — being torn open *(present passive participle of
  **σχίζω**, whose noun gives English *schism*; take it whole)*
- **εὐδόκησα** — I am well pleased *(aorist of **εὐδοκέω**: **εὖ** +
  **δοκέω**, it seems good; the aorist for a settled state)*

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read each adapted text aloud, then its original. Keep the table open.

1. Both passages have a going-up. Find the three forms of **ἀναβαίνω** in
   the originals, say what each is (tense; verb or participle), and say
   who goes up, from where.
2. **γίγνονται παῖδες δύο.** The verb is present; the sons were born long
   before Xenophon wrote. What is he doing? Find three more presents in
   his passage that do the same thing.
3. **τὼ παῖδε ἀμφοτέρω.** What number is this, and which row of the table
   does it belong to? What did the adapted text put in its place?
4. **ἐβούλετο τὼ παῖδε ἀμφοτέρω παρεῖναι.** Which construction of Lesson
   16 is this? How would a Koine writer have been likely to say it?
5. **ὡς φίλον**, **ὡς ἐπιβουλεύοι**, **ὡς ἀποκτενῶν**, **ὡς περιστεράν**.
   Four **ὡς**. Which two are the plain *as, like* of the vocabulary box?
6. Count the **καί**s in Mark's three verses. Then count **μέν**, **δέ**,
   **οὖν** and **γάρ** in Xenophon's three sentences. Which row of the
   table is each writer sitting in?
7. **ἐβαπτίσθη εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην.** What case follows **εἰς**, and what
   would an Attic writer have put for *in the Jordan*? Which row of the
   table is this?
8. **σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός.** Why is **σύ** written out when **εἶ**
   already says *you*? Where have you seen the shape **ὁ … ὁ …** before,
   and what is it doing here?
9. **Τισσαφέρνης διαβάλλει τὸν Κῦρον … ὡς ἐπιβουλεύοι αὐτῷ.** The adapted
   text had **ἔλεγε γὰρ τὸν Κῦρον κακὰ ἐθέλειν ποιεῖν αὐτόν.** What did the
   adaptation change, and which mood has gone missing between Xenophon and
   the Gospels?
10. In the adapted Xenophon, **ἤθελε** is the imperfect of **θέλω** — and
    also of Lesson 11's **ἐθέλω**. How can one form belong to both verbs?
    What does that tell you about where Koine's **θέλω** came from?
:::

::: answers
1. **ἀνέβη** in Xenophon — aorist, a main verb: Cyrus went up from his
   province inland towards the king (*up* because away from the sea, which
   is what the *Anabasis*, the Going-Up, means). **ἀναβαίνων** in Mark —
   present participle, hanging from **εἶδεν**: Jesus going up out of the
   water. The pupil should notice that Xenophon also has **ἀναβαίνει**,
   the present, in the sentence before — see the next question.
2. Telling the past in the present tense — the historic present, as in
   The Investigation. **μεταπέμπεται**, **διαβάλλει**, **πείθεται**,
   **συλλαμβάνει**, **ἀποπέμπει**, **ἀναβαίνει**: six in all; any three.
   The pupil who notices that the aorists **ἐποίησε**, **ἀπέδειξε**,
   **ἀνέβη**, **ἐτελεύτησε** sit among them has seen that Xenophon
   switches tense within a sentence, for pace, and that this is normal.
3. The dual — a number for exactly two, beside singular and plural; the
   table's last row, *present* in Attic, *gone* in Koine. The adapted text
   had **τοὺς δύο παῖδας**, plural, which is how Koine (and, by the fourth
   century AD, nearly all Greek) says it.
4. Accusative and infinitive after a verb of wanting: *he wanted both his
   sons to be present*. Koine would be likely to write **ἤθελεν ἵνα οἱ δύο
   υἱοὶ αὐτοῦ παρῶσιν** — **θέλω ἵνα** with a verb — or keep the
   infinitive, as the exercise above allowed. Either is possible; the
   dual is not.
5. **ὡς φίλον** — *as a friend*; **ὡς περιστεράν** — *like a dove*. The
   other two take verb forms the class has not met: **ὡς ἐπιβουλεύοι** is
   *(alleging) that he was plotting*, and **ὡς ἀποκτενῶν** is *as (one)
   going to kill* — purpose. Four jobs for one small word, and the box
   gives the two the class can use.
6. Mark: five **καί** in three verses, the first of them **καὶ ἐγένετο**,
   the Septuagint's opening. Xenophon: **μέν** twice, **δέ** eight times,
   **οὖν** twice, **γάρ** none in the original (two in the adaptation).
   Mark is in the Koine column of *sentence shape*: short, coordinated,
   strung on **καί**. Xenophon is in the Attic column: clauses held
   together by **μέν … δέ** and **οὖν**, each sentence carrying the
   last.
7. Accusative, **τὸν Ἰορδάνην**. Attic would have **ἐν τῷ Ἰορδάνῃ**,
   dative, which is what the adapted text wrote. The table's row on the
   dative: *receding; **ἐν** and **εἰς** doing its work* — and here
   **εἰς** has gone a step further and taken over **ἐν**'s job as well.
8. Emphasis, and Koine's habit of saying the pronoun: *you* — this one,
   out of the water — *are my son*. **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός**, Lesson 10: the
   article repeated puts the adjective in attributive position, so that
   **ὁ ἀγαπητός** is part of the name — *my son, the beloved one* — and
   not a separate remark about him.
9. The adaptation turned an accusation into reported speech, with Lesson
   16's accusative and infinitive (**τὸν Κῦρον … ἐθέλειν**). Xenophon has
   **ὡς** with the optative, **ἐπιβουλεύοι**, a mood used after past
   tenses for what someone said or thought. The optative is the mood
   Koine lost almost entirely — the table's row on *purpose* — and the
   Gospels get by with **ὅτι**, **ἵνα** and the forms you will meet in
   Lesson 35.
10. Because **θέλω** is **ἐθέλω** with its first vowel worn off, and the
    imperfect of **ἐθέλω** already began with **ἠ-** (the augment
    lengthening the **ε**): **ἤθελον**. When the present lost its **ἐ-**,
    the imperfect had nothing to lose. So the past tense never changed,
    and only the present tells you which Greek you are reading. Koine's
    **θέλω** is not a new word: it is an Attic word shortened in the
    mouth, which is what most of the table's differences turn out to be.
:::

1. Koine is often described as *simpler* than Attic. Simpler for whom? Is a

2. Theodoros speaks Koine and taught Attic. Was that honest? Was it kind?
3. Socrates and John both wrote one unforgettable sentence about truth.
