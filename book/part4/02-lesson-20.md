# The Greek of the Septuagint

::: story
Julia read the Psalm through twice, and then said what she thought, which
she had been doing more often since Lesson 18.

"This is not good Greek."

The room went still in the way it did when somebody said something true in
the wrong place.

"Go on," said Theodoros.

"It says **καί** at the start of every line. It says *the Lord shepherds me*
when any Greek would say *the Lord is my shepherd*. It says *the shadow of
death* when it means *a deadly shadow*. It says *to length of days* when it
means *as long as I live*. It — " She looked at her tablet. "It is all grammatical. I
cannot find a mistake. But Plato would not write one line of it."

"No. He would not."

"Then why is it like this?"

Theodoros sat on the edge of the table, which he did about twice a year.

"Because the men who wrote it were translating," he said. "They had a
Hebrew text in front of them, and every word in it was holy, and they had to
turn it into Greek for people who could no longer read the Hebrew. And at
every line they had a choice. They could write the Greek that Plato would
have written, and lose the shape of the Hebrew. Or they could keep the shape
of the Hebrew and write Greek that no Athenian would recognise."

"And they chose the second."

"At nearly every line. **καί, καί, καί** — because the Hebrew says *and,
and, and*. *The Lord shepherds me* — because the Hebrew has a verb there, not
a noun. *To length of days* — because the Hebrew idiom for *all my life*
is a phrase about time, and they kept the phrase." He looked round the room. "It is not
bad Greek. It is Greek with another language showing through it, on
purpose, because the other language mattered more to them than sounding
well."

Theodosius had been listening with his tablet forgotten in his lap.

"So the strangeness is reverence," he said.

"The strangeness is reverence. And in three hundred years it becomes a
style, and the men who write the Gospels have grown up hearing it read
aloud, and when they come to write about God they write *this* Greek,
because it is the Greek that God has been spoken of in." Theodoros stood.
"Julia is right. It is not Plato. The question is whether that is a defect
or a decision, and you are now going to find the evidence."
:::

::: newgreek
## Translation Greek

The Septuagint — the Greek Old Testament, made in Alexandria from about 250
BC — is translation. Most of it is translation done with great care by
people who thought the shape of the original was part of its meaning. The
result is Greek that is **grammatical and unidiomatic**: every sentence
obeys the rules, and almost no sentence is what a Greek would have said.

Learn to see the Hebrew through it. Five things show.

**One. καί, καί, καί.** Hebrew joins clauses with a single *and*, over and
over. Greek prose prefers subordination — *when this, then that* — and the
connectors of Lesson 17. The Septuagint keeps the *and*. A page of it is a
chain of short clauses linked with **καί**, and that chain is the single
most recognisable feature of biblical Greek.

**Two. "And it came to pass."** **καὶ ἐγένετο** — you had **ἐγένετο** in
Lesson 14 — opens a narrative the way Hebrew opens one. It means roughly
*and it happened that*, and Attic never begins a story with it.

**Three. A verb where Greek wants a noun.** *The Lord shepherds me* for
*the Lord is my shepherd*. Hebrew likes verbs; the translators kept them.

**Four. Genitive for adjective.** *The house of the Lord* for *the Lord's
house*; *the shadow of death* for *a deadly shadow*. (That second one the
translators read as two Hebrew words, *shadow* and *death*; the Hebrew may
be one word meaning *deep darkness*. The Greek has the chain either way.)
Hebrew builds
descriptions out of chains of nouns, and the Septuagint builds them out of
chains of genitives. You know the genitive from Lesson 8; here it is doing
an adjective's work.

**Five. Idioms kept whole.** **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα**, *unto the age*, for
*forever*. **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου**, *all the days of my life*,
for *as long as I live*. The phrase is translated and the idiom is carried
across intact, so the Greek reader learns to think in Hebrew pictures.

::: note
**One more, which you have the grammar for.** The Septuagint writes
**Κύριος** without the article when it stands for the name of God — the
way a name has no article. **ὁ κύριος** is *the master*; **Κύριος** on its
own, at the head of a sentence, is a name. You learned in Lesson 3 that the
article marks what is definite; here its absence marks something more
definite still.
:::

## The Attic for comparison

Here is roughly what an Athenian would have written to say the first line
of the Psalm. It is composed for this lesson, not quoted:

**ὁ θεὸς ὥσπερ ποιμὴν ἐπιμελεῖταί μου.**

*God looks after me like a shepherd.* (**ὥσπερ**, *just like*;
**ἐπιμελεῖταί μου**, *looks after me* — the verb takes the genitive.) A simile where the Hebrew has a
metaphor; a verb of caring where the Hebrew has the verb for herding;
**ὁ θεός** with the article where the Septuagint has a name. Fluent, clear,
and the picture is gone.

::: latinbridge
**You already know translation Greek. You know it in Latin.** The Vulgate
did to Latin exactly what the Septuagint did to Greek: *Dominus regit me* —
or, in Jerome's newer version made from the Hebrew, *Dominus pascit me* —
*the Lord shepherds me*. *In saeculum saeculi.* *Et factum est.* The Latin
translators kept the shape of the original through the Greek, and the
Latin Psalms you grew up with have the same *and-and-and* and the same
chains of genitives.

So when Julia says *this is not good Greek*, the answer is that it is not
good Latin either, by Cicero's standard, and that most of the people who
pray in it have never minded — though Augustine, who is alive as you read
this, has written that as a young man he did.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ ποιμήν** — shepherd *(genitive **ποιμένος**; you met **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ
  καλός** in Lesson 10)*
- **ποιμαίνω** — I shepherd, I tend
- **εὐλογέω** — I bless *(**εὖ** well + **λέγω**: to speak well of)*
- **ὁ αἰών** — age; eternity *(**εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** = forever)*
- **τὸ ἔλεος** — mercy, compassion *(neuter, Koine; Attic has **ὁ ἔλεος**,
  masculine)*
- **ἄγω** — I lead, bring *(you saw it inside **παιδαγωγός** in Lesson 3)*
- **ὥσπερ** — just like, as
- **ὁ θάνατος** — death
- **ὁ οἶκος** — house; household, family line *(Lesson 2, now in its
  Hebraic sense)*
- **τὸ ὕδωρ** — water *(third declension; genitive **ὕδατος**)*
- **ἡ τράπεζα** — table *(the cat's, in Lesson 3)*
- **ὁ τόπος** — place
- **ἡ κεφαλή** — head
- **ἐπί** — on, upon *(with the genitive)*; on to, against *(with the
  accusative)*
- **μετά** — with *(with the genitive: **μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ**, with me)*; after
  *(with the accusative)*

::: note
**εὐλογέω** is **εὖ** + **λέγω**, *to speak well of*, and both halves are
words you know. The Latin *benedicere* is the same compound built in Latin:
*bene* + *dicere*. Both are translation words, made to carry the Hebrew
*bless*, and English *bless* is the odd one out.

**ὁ αἰών** gives English *eon*. **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** is *into the age* and
means *forever*; **εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων**, *into the ages of the ages*,
is the Hebrew superlative — *for ever and ever* — built out of the Hebrew
habit of repeating a noun in the genitive to make it bigger.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From Lessons 16 to 19. Answer from memory.

1. **βούλεται μανθάνειν.** Translate; which word is the infinitive, and
   does it tell you anything about time?
2. Give the genitive of **ἡ χάρις** and of **ἡ πίστις**.
3. **πιστεύω** takes which case in Attic? What else does the New Testament
   do with it?
4. Put **οὖν** into **ὁ ἄνθρωπος λέγει** so that it means *so the man
   speaks*.
5. **ἔμενον** — imperfect or aorist? Which two persons could it be?
6. **ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.** Why can it not mean *love is God*?
7. Translate **ὁ κύριος τοῦ οἴκου** twice: once as an Athenian would have
   heard it, once as a reader of the Septuagint might.
8. **ἀπέστειλε** — name the verb, the tense, and the person.
9. **Οὖτις ἐμοί γ᾽ ὄνομα.** Whose line, and where is the trick?
10. **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ** — what is it, and which letter of the article has it
    lost?
:::

::: answers
1. *He wants to learn.* **μανθάνειν**, present infinitive; it says the
   learning is open-ended and says nothing about when. *He wanted* is the
   error of a pupil who has heard an infinitive as past.
2. **χάριτος**, **πίστεως**. Both are in the Lesson 19 box; the second is
   the one that gets guessed as **πίστιδος**.
3. The dative (**πιστεύω τῷ θεῷ**); the New Testament also has **πιστεύω
   εἰς** with the accusative.
4. **ὁ οὖν ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — **οὖν** second, never first. **οὖν ὁ
   ἄνθρωπος** is the Lesson 17 error and it is still the likeliest.
5. Imperfect (present stem, augment). First singular or third plural —
   *I was staying* or *they were staying*.
6. Both nominative; the article is on **θεός** and not on **ἀγάπη**, so
   **ὁ θεός** is the subject (Lesson 18).
7. *The master of the house* — the man legally in charge of it; and *the
   Lord of the house(hold)* — God. Same four words.
8. **ἀποστέλλω**, aorist, third singular: *he sent out*.
9. Odysseus, to the Cyclops (Lesson 16). The trick is a name that is also
   the word *nobody*, so that the Cyclops's cry for help says no one hurt
   him.
10. The relative pronoun, *who, which*; it has lost the **τ** (**τόν →
    ὅν**) and taken a rough breathing in its place.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate, and name the Hebrew"}
1. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.**
2. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ ὁ ποιμὴν ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
3. **τὸ ἔλεος τοῦ κυρίου μέγα ἦν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.**
4. **ὁ ποιμὴν ἄγει τὰ πρόβατα εἰς τὸν οἶκον τοῦ κυρίου.** *(**τὰ πρόβατα**
   = the sheep)*
5. **καὶ εἶπε Κύριος πρὸς τὸν ποιμένα, καὶ ὁ ποιμὴν ἤκουσεν.**
6. **εὐλόγησε Κύριος τὸν οἶκον τοῦ ποιμένος.** *(**εὐλόγησε** is the
   aorist of **εὐλογέω**)*
7. **ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ κυρίου ἡ ψυχή μου μένει πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας.** *(**μου** =
   my)*
8. **ὁ θεὸς ἄγει με ἐπὶ τὸ ὕδωρ, καὶ ἡ ψυχή μου ζωὴν ἔχει.**
9. **υἱὸς θανάτου ὁ ἀνὴρ οὗτος.**
10. **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλὸς ἄγει τὰ πρόβατα εἰς τόπον ὕδατος.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The Lord shepherds me* — or, in English idiom, *the Lord is my
   shepherd*. What shows through: a verb where Greek and English want a
   noun; **Κύριος** without the article, as a name. Accept either English;
   require the note.
2. *And it came to pass, and the shepherd came into the house.* — **καὶ
   ἐγένετο** opening a narrative; **καί** linking clauses. This sentence is
   composed, and it is composed to sound like the Septuagint; ask the class
   how they can tell.
3. *The mercy of the Lord was great forever.* — **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** as an
   idiom kept whole; **τοῦ κυρίου** as a genitive doing possessive work.
4. *The shepherd leads the sheep into the house of the Lord.* — *house of
   the Lord* for *the Lord's house*; the genitive chain.
5. *And the Lord spoke to the shepherd, and the shepherd heard.* — **καί
   … καί**; **Κύριος** as a name; the verb first in its clause, which is
   the Hebrew order. **ἤκουσεν** is the aorist of **ἀκούω**, built by
   Lesson 14's rule with Lesson 13's augment; give it if asked.
6. *The Lord blessed the house of the shepherd.* — verb first; **οἶκος**
   in its sense of *household*; **εὐλογέω** itself, a word made to
   translate. A pupil who writes *praised* has read the Greek parts and
   missed that the compound is a technical term.
7. *In the house of the Lord my soul remains all the days* — that is, *as
   long as I live*. The genitive chain, and the idiom **πάσας τὰς
   ἡμέρας** kept whole.
8. *God leads me to the water, and my soul has life.* — a picture where
   Attic would have an abstraction; **ἐπί** with the accusative, *on to*.
   Composed to sound like verse 2 of the Psalm, which the Reading has.
9. *This man is a son of death* — he deserves to die. No verb; *son of X*
   for an adjective, the Hebrew way of saying *deserving X*. Composed on
   the pattern of 2 Kingdoms 12:5. **οὗτος** after its noun, with the
   article between — the demonstrative's normal place, which they have
   not been taught and can be told.
10. *The good shepherd leads the sheep to a place of water.* — **τόπον
    ὕδατος** with no article, a genitive chain for *a watering place*.
    **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός** is Lesson 10's phrase, and its second article is
    the Attic way of doing what the Hebrew chain does — ask the class to
    find the two devices in one sentence.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Rewrite as Attic"}
Each of these is Septuagint Greek. Write what an Athenian would have said
instead, using the patterns given in the grammar section. Then say, in one
line, what was lost. New words you may need are given in brackets.

1. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.**
2. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ ἦλθεν ὁ ποιμὴν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
3. **τὸ ἔλεος τοῦ κυρίου μέγα ἐστίν.**
4. **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.**
5. **ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ κυρίου.** *(**τὸ ἱερόν** = the temple)*
6. **σκιὰ θανάτου.** *(**θανάσιμος** = deadly)*
7. **υἱὸς θανάτου ὁ ἀνήρ.** *(**ἄξιος** = worthy of, with the genitive)*
8. **καὶ εἶπε Κύριος πρὸς τὸν ποιμένα, καὶ ὁ ποιμὴν ἤκουσεν.**
9. **ἄγει τὰ πρόβατα εἰς τόπον ὕδατος.** *(**ὁ ποταμός** = river)*
10. **ὁ θεὸς τοῦ οὐρανοῦ.** *(**οὐράνιος** = heavenly)*
:::

::: answers
1. Something like **ὁ θεὸς ὥσπερ ποιμὴν ἐπιμελεῖταί μου**, or with
   **ποιμαίνει** kept: **ὁ θεὸς ποιμαίνει με ὥσπερ πρόβατον**. What was
   lost: the metaphor became a simile — the Psalm says God *is* a shepherd
   and the Attic says God is *like* one — and the name became a
   description.
2. **ὁ δὲ ποιμὴν ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.** — the opening **καὶ ἐγένετο** goes,
   and **δέ** takes the place of the first **καί**. Lost: the sound of a
   story beginning the way scripture begins one.
3. **ὁ τοῦ θεοῦ ἔλεος μέγας ἐστίν.** — Attic puts the genitive between
   article and noun, uses **ὁ θεός** for the name, and has **ἔλεος**
   masculine, so **μέγας**. Lost: **Κύριος**, and the neuter the Koine
   reader knows.
4. **ἀεί.** — Lost: the picture of an age one walks into.
5. **ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ**, or **ἐν τῷ τοῦ θεοῦ οἴκῳ**. — Lost: *house*, with the
   household in it; a temple is a building.
6. **θανάσιμος σκιά** or **σκιὰ θανάσιμος**. — One adjective for two nouns.
   Lost: *death* as a thing with a shadow of its own.
7. **ὁ ἀνὴρ ἄξιός ἐστι θανάτου.** — Lost: the Hebrew *son of*, which makes
   the man's deserving a kind of kinship.
8. **ὁ δὲ θεὸς εἶπε τῷ ποιμένι, ὁ δὲ ἤκουσεν.** — **δέ … δέ** for **καί …
   καί**; the dative after **εἶπε**; the second subject reduced to its
   article. Lost: the chain, and the name.
9. **ἄγει τὰ πρόβατα πρὸς τὸ ὕδωρ** or **εἰς τὸν ποταμόν**. — Lost: the
   genitive chain, and with it the slight strangeness that marks the
   sentence as translated.
10. **ὁ οὐράνιος θεός.** — Lost: the chain again; and gained something
    false, because an Athenian who said *the heavenly god* would have had
    to say which one.

Any Attic the class produces will be, in one way or another, more elegant and
less faithful. That is the lesson, and the pupil who notices it unprompted
has done the afternoon's work. Item 7 is the one where most of the meaning
goes; ask the class which item it was before you tell them.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
Write these in Septuagint Greek — that is, keep the Hebrew shape.

1. The Lord is my shepherd.
2. The mercy of the Lord was great.
3. The shepherd leads the sheep into the house.
4. Forever.
5. And the Lord spoke to the shepherd, and the shepherd heard.
6. The Lord leads me to a place of water.
7. Death has no mercy.
8. The Lord blessed the house of the shepherd forever.
9. You are with me. *(**ἐμοῦ** = of me)*
10. All the days of my life.
:::

::: answers
1. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.** — a verb, no article. Accept **ὁ κύριος ποιμήν
   μού ἐστιν** as correct Greek that has missed the instruction.
2. **τὸ ἔλεος τοῦ κυρίου μέγα ἦν.** — **μέγα** is the neuter of **μέγας**,
   agreeing with **ἔλεος**.
3. **ὁ ποιμὴν ἄγει τὰ πρόβατα εἰς τὸν οἶκον.** — **ἄγω** is in today's
   list; the class saw it inside **παιδαγωγός** in Lesson 3.
4. **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.** — the idiom whole. A pupil who writes **ἀεί** has
   written Attic, which is correct and is not what was asked.
5. **καὶ εἶπε Κύριος πρὸς τὸν ποιμένα, καὶ ὁ ποιμὴν ἤκουσεν.** — verb
   first, **καί** twice, no article on **Κύριος**. **ὁ κύριος εἶπε τῷ
   ποιμένι** is good Greek and has lost the Hebrew.
6. **Κύριος ἄγει με εἰς τόπον ὕδατος.** — the genitive chain, no article.
   **πρὸς τὸ ὕδωρ** is the Attic instinct and should be named as such.
7. **ὁ θάνατος οὐκ ἔχει ἔλεος.** — **ἔλεος** neuter, accusative, so it
   looks like the nominative; a pupil who writes **ἔλεον** has given the
   Attic masculine, which is not wrong and is not Koine.
8. **εὐλόγησε Κύριος τὸν οἶκον τοῦ ποιμένος εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.** — verb first;
   the idiom whole at the end.
9. **σὺ μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ εἶ.** — The Ancient Voice has it. **μετά** with the
   genitive; **σύ** written out, as the Psalm writes it. **σὺν ἐμοί** is
   correct Attic and the wrong register.
10. **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου.** — accusative for *how long*; the
    chain of genitives. **πάσας** is the form to check.
:::
:::

::: {.voice source="Psalm 22:1, 4, 6 (LXX)"}
The Psalm every one of you knows in Latin, in the Greek the Latin was made
from.

**Κύριος ποιμαίνει με, καὶ οὐδέν με ὑστερήσει.**

- **Κύριος** — the Lord *(no article: a name)*
- **ποιμαίνει** — shepherds *(a verb; the Latin kept it, and every English
  Bible has put a noun)*
- **με** — me
- **οὐδέν** — nothing
- **ὑστερήσει** — will be lacking *(future; recognise it)*

*The Lord shepherds me, and nothing will be lacking to me.*

**ἐὰν γὰρ καὶ πορευθῶ ἐν μέσῳ σκιᾶς θανάτου, οὐ φοβηθήσομαι κακά, ὅτι σὺ
μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ εἶ.**

- **ἐὰν γὰρ καὶ πορευθῶ** — for even if I walk *(a form you will meet
  later; take it as given)*
- **ἐν μέσῳ** — in the midst
- **σκιᾶς θανάτου** — of the shadow of death *(two genitives: the Hebrew
  chain)*
- **οὐ φοβηθήσομαι** — I shall not fear *(future)*
- **κακά** — evil things *(neuter plural of **κακός**, Lesson 10)*
- **ὅτι** — because
- **σὺ μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ εἶ** — you are with me

*For even if I walk in the midst of the shadow of death, I shall not fear
evil things, because you are with me.*

**καὶ τὸ ἔλεός σου καταδιώξεταί με πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου.**

- **τὸ ἔλεός σου** — your mercy
- **καταδιώξεταί με** — will pursue me *(future; the verb is the one for
  hunting something down)*
- **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας** — all the days *(**ἡμέρα**, Lesson 7)*
- **τῆς ζωῆς μου** — of my life *(**ζωή**, Lesson 5)*

*And your mercy will pursue me all the days of my life.*

**Count what you recognise.** **Κύριος, οὐδέν, ἐν, κακά, ὅτι, καί, τὸ ἔλεος,
τὰς ἡμέρας, τῆς ζωῆς** — most of the words are yours. The Psalm is not hard
Greek. It is strange Greek, and now you know why.

**And look at σκιᾶς θανάτου.** *Of the shadow of death.* An Athenian would
have written one adjective. The translator wrote two nouns in the genitive,
one hanging from the other, because the Hebrew did — and the result is a
phrase that has outlived every elegant alternative in every language it was
ever carried into. Julia was right that Plato would not have written it.
Nobody remembers what Plato would have written instead.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The verses are 22:1, 4 and 6 in the Septuagint's
numbering (Psalm 23 in the Hebrew and in English Bibles) and are quoted from
memory of the standard text. Check each against the edition in use before it
goes on the board; the last clause of verse 6 is omitted here and editions
differ slightly in punctuation.

**On Julia's objection.** Let it stand as a real objection and let the
answer be real too. The aim is not to make the pupils admire the Septuagint
but to make them able to say *why* it reads as it does — which means they
must first be allowed to notice that it reads oddly. A class that is told
the Psalm is beautiful before it is allowed to find it strange learns
nothing about either.
:::

ἔλαιον ἔχω ἐπὶ τῆς κεφαλῆς μου, καὶ τὸ ποτήριόν μου μέγα ἐστίν.

καὶ τὸ ἔλεος τοῦ κυρίου μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ ἐστι πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου, καὶ
ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ κυρίου μένω εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.

**The original** *(Psalm 22, Septuagint, text of Rahlfs)*

Ψαλμὸς τῷ Δαυιδ.

Κύριος ποιμαίνει με, καὶ οὐδέν με ὑστερήσει. εἰς τόπον χλόης, ἐκεῖ με
κατεσκήνωσεν, ἐπὶ ὕδατος ἀναπαύσεως ἐξέθρεψέν με, τὴν ψυχήν μου
ἐπέστρεψεν. ὡδήγησέν με ἐπὶ τρίβους δικαιοσύνης ἕνεκεν τοῦ ὀνόματος
αὐτοῦ.

ἐὰν γὰρ καὶ πορευθῶ ἐν μέσῳ σκιᾶς θανάτου, οὐ φοβηθήσομαι κακά, ὅτι σὺ
μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ εἶ· ἡ ῥάβδος σου καὶ ἡ βακτηρία σου, αὐταί με παρεκάλεσαν.

ἡτοίμασας ἐνώπιόν μου τράπεζαν ἐξ ἐναντίας τῶν θλιβόντων με· ἐλίπανας ἐν
ἐλαίῳ τὴν κεφαλήν μου, καὶ τὸ ποτήριόν σου μεθύσκον ὡς κράτιστον.

καὶ τὸ ἔλεός σου καταδιώξεταί με πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου, καὶ τὸ
κατοικεῖν με ἐν οἴκῳ κυρίου εἰς μακρότητα ἡμερῶν.

::: gloss
- **με, μου, σου, ἐμοῦ** — me, my, your, of me *(the little forms of
  **ἐγώ** and **σύ**)*
- **ἡ σκιά** — shadow
- **ὁ φόβος** — fear *(adapted text only)*
- **ἡ ῥάβδος** — rod, staff
- **τὸ ἔλαιον** — olive oil
- **τὸ ποτήριον** — cup
- **Ψαλμὸς τῷ Δαυιδ** — a psalm; of David *(the heading; the dative stands
  for the Hebrew "belonging to". Rahlfs prints the name without accent,
  as the manuscripts do with Hebrew names)*
- **χλόης** — of fresh grass *(**τόπος χλόης**, a place of grass: a
  pasture)*
- **ἐκεῖ** — there
- **κατεσκήνωσεν** — he settled *(me)*, made *(me)* camp *(aorist; a
  **σκηνή**, tent, is inside it)*
- **ἀναπαύσεως** — of rest *(**ὕδωρ ἀναπαύσεως**: restful water)*
- **ἐξέθρεψέν με** — he reared me, fed me up *(aorist)*
- **ἐπέστρεψεν** — he turned back, restored *(aorist)*
- **ὡδήγησέν με** — he guided me *(aorist; **ὁδός** is inside it)*
- **τρίβους** — paths *(accusative plural)*
- **ἕνεκεν τοῦ ὀνόματος αὐτοῦ** — for the sake of his name
- **ἡ βακτηρία** — staff, walking-stick
- **αὐταί** — they *(feminine; emphatic — Lesson 21)*
- **παρεκάλεσαν** — comforted *(aorist, plural)*
- **ἡτοίμασας** — you prepared *(aorist, second person)*
- **ἐνώπιόν μου** — before me, in my sight
- **ἐξ ἐναντίας τῶν θλιβόντων με** — over against those who oppress me
  *(a participle, Lesson 22)*
- **ἐλίπανας** — you made rich with oil, anointed *(aorist, second person)*
- **ἐν ἐλαίῳ** — with oil *(**ἐν** for the instrument: the Hebrew showing)*
- **μεθύσκον** — making drunk, intoxicating *(a participle agreeing with
  **ποτήριον**)*
- **ὡς κράτιστον** — like the strongest *(wine)*; as the best
- **τὸ κατοικεῖν με** — my dwelling *(an infinitive made a noun by the
  article, with **με** as its subject — Lesson 16's accusative)*
- **εἰς μακρότητα ἡμερῶν** — to length of days
- For **ὑστερήσει**, **πορευθῶ**, **φοβηθήσομαι** and **καταδιώξεται**,
  see The Ancient Voice.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted Psalm aloud, all of it, before you look at the original.

1. In the adapted text, who is the subject of **ποιμαίνει**, and what
   tells you that **Κύριος** is a name here rather than *the master*?
2. Count the **καί** in the first paragraph of the adapted text. How many
   clauses do they join? What would an Athenian have done instead?
3. The adapted text has **ἐπὶ τὸ ὕδωρ**; the original has **ἐπὶ ὕδατος
   ἀναπαύσεως**. What case follows **ἐπί** in each, and what has the
   original's second noun added?
4. In the original, find the four verbs of the second and third sentences
   (**κατεσκήνωσεν** … **ὡδήγησεν**). What tense are they, and what two
   marks tell you?
5. **ἡ ῥάβδος σου καὶ ἡ βακτηρία σου, αὐταί με παρεκάλεσαν.** The verb
   ending already says *they*. Why is **αὐταί** there?
6. **ἡτοίμασας** and **ἐλίπανας** — what person are they? Where in the
   Psalm does the speaker stop saying *he* about God and start saying
   *you*?
7. **ἐν ἐλαίῳ.** What case, and what is **ἐν** doing that an Athenian
   would have done with the bare dative?
8. **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου.** What case is **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας**,
   what question does it answer, and what idiom is being carried whole?
9. The original ends with **τὸ κατοικεῖν με**. What kind of word is
   **κατοικεῖν**, what has the article done to it, and what case is **με**
   and why?
10. Find every genitive chain in the original — a noun hanging from a
    noun where Attic would have an adjective. There are at least five.
    Which of them, if any, could stand in a sentence of Plato?
:::

::: answers
1. **Κύριος**; it has no article, and a nominative with no article at the
   head of a sentence is a name (the grammar note). A pupil who says *the
   article is missing* has seen the evidence and needs to be told what it
   is evidence of.
2. Three, joining five clauses in one paragraph. An Athenian would have
   subordinated — *when he leads me … my soul lives* — or used **δέ**, or
   a participle, which they meet in Lesson 22.
3. Accusative (**τὸ ὕδωρ**: motion *on to*) and genitive (**ὕδατος**:
   position *on, at*). The original's **ἀναπαύσεως** is a genitive doing
   an adjective's work — *water of rest* for *restful water* — the fourth
   feature of the grammar section.
4. Aorist. The augment at the front (**κατ-ε-σκήνωσεν**, **ἐξ-έ-θρεψεν**,
   **ἐπ-έ-στρεψεν**, **ὡ-δήγησεν** — the **ὁ-** of **ὁδηγέω** lengthened
   to **ὡ-**) and the **σ** before the ending. A pupil who calls them
   imperfect has seen the augment and not the **σ**.
5. Emphasis — *they*, those two things, and nothing else comforted me. The
   Hebrew writes its pronoun there, and the translators kept it. (They
   meet **αὐτός** properly in Lesson 21; here they can simply see what it
   does.)
6. Second person singular: *you prepared*, *you anointed*. The turn is at
   **σὺ μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ εἶ** in verse 4 — the Psalm speaks *about* God for
   three verses and *to* God for three, and the moment it turns is the
   shadow of death.
7. Dative. Marking the instrument: Attic says **ἐλαίῳ** alone for *with
   oil*; **ἐν** is there because the Hebrew preposition was, and it is
   one of the places where the dative is being propped up by a
   preposition (Lesson 23 will say more).
8. Accusative; *for how long?*; **πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ζωῆς μου**, *all
   the days of my life*, for *as long as I live* — the fifth feature,
   carried across whole.
9. An infinitive; the article has made it a noun, *the dwelling*; **με**
   is accusative because it is the subject of the infinitive — the rule of
   Lesson 16, turning up in the Psalter. The clause has no main verb: *and
   my dwelling [will be] in the house of the Lord*.
10. **τόπον χλόης**, **ὕδατος ἀναπαύσεως**, **τρίβους δικαιοσύνης**,
    **σκιᾶς θανάτου**, **μακρότητα ἡμερῶν**; accept also **οἴκῳ κυρίου**.
    **τρίβους δικαιοσύνης** is the one Plato could nearly write — *paths
    of justice* is a Greek picture too — and the class should argue about
    it. The point is that they can now hear which chains are Greek and
    which are Hebrew in Greek clothing.
:::

1. The translators could have made the Greek sound natural and chose not
   to. What were they protecting? What did they give up?
2. Julia said *this is not good Greek* and was right. Is *good* the right

3. The Latin you grew up with has the same strangeness, and you never
   noticed. Why not?

::: story
Aelia said that in Antioch, where her mother's family came from, people
talked like the Psalm.

"Like the Psalm?"

"*And* this, *and* that, *and* the other. **καί, καί, καί.** My
grandmother does it in Greek and in Syriac both."

Theodoros considered this.

"Then your grandmother," he said, "has been speaking translation Greek for
seventy years without a Hebrew text in front of her, which tells you that
the Septuagint did not only come out of the streets. It went back into
them."
