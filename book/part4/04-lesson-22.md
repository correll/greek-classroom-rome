# The Good Samaritan

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros read the story through once, in Greek, at speaking pace, and then
asked who had done what.

Marcus started to answer and stopped, because he had lost the thread
somewhere around the second priest.

Julia had the first sentence and was still working on the second.

Sabina had the whole thing.

"One man is done to," she said. "From the moment the bandits get him,
he never does anything again. He is stripped, he is beaten, he is left, he is
seen, he is seen, he is seen, he is bound up, he is lifted, he is carried, he
is paid for. He is the object of every verb in the story and the subject of
none."

"Go on."

"Three men see him. The word is the same each time — **ἰδών**, *having
seen*. Two of them see and go round. One sees and *stops*. The whole story
is three men doing the same first thing and then not the same second
thing."

"And the Samaritan?"

"He does eight things in a row. He comes up, he binds, he pours, he lifts,
he leads, he looks after, he pays, he promises. Eight verbs. The bandits get
four between them; the priest and the Levite two each." She looked up. "It is not a story about
*who* the neighbour is. It is a story about who *does* things. You can see
it in the grammar before you can see it in the sense."

There was a pause.

"Sabina," said Theodoros, "has just told you what a participle is for, and
she has done it without knowing the word. The rest of you have spent
twenty-one lessons being told that parsing is plot and not believing it.
Today it is going to be impossible not to."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The participle

A **participle** is a verb wearing an adjective's clothes. It has a verb's
meaning — *seeing*, *having come*, *having bound* — and an adjective's
endings, so that it can agree with a noun in case, number and gender.

Greek narrative runs on participles. Where English says *he saw him and
passed by*, Greek says **ἰδὼν αὐτὸν ἀντιπαρῆλθεν** — *having seen him, he
passed by*. One main verb; the other action hangs from it as a participle.
A Greek sentence can have one main verb and five participles, each marking a
step, and the whole thing reads as a single movement.

## The forms you need

The nominative singular, masculine, feminine and neuter. The masculine is
the one you will meet most.

| | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Present** | **λέγων** | **λέγουσα** | **λέγον** | saying, while saying |
| **First aorist** | **γράψας** | **γράψασα** | **γράψαν** | having written |
| **Second aorist** | **ἰδών** | **ἰδοῦσα** | **ἰδόν** | having seen |
| | **ἐλθών** | **ἐλθοῦσα** | **ἐλθόν** | having come |
| | **εἰπών** | **εἰποῦσα** | **εἰπόν** | having said |

The masculine genitive is **-οντος** or **-αντος**: **ἰδόντος**,
**γράψαντος**. You will see one in the passage — **τοῦ ἐμπεσόντος**, *of the
one who fell among* — and you can recognise it from the ending.

## Aspect, one more time

The participle carries aspect, not time. This is the third time the course
has said this and it is the most important.

**A present participle** means *while doing*: the action runs alongside the
main verb. **ἐπιχέων ἔλαιον** — *pouring on oil*, as he binds.

**An aorist participle** means *having done*: one complete act, usually the
step before the main verb. **ἰδὼν … ἀντιπαρῆλθεν** — *having seen, he
passed by*. **προσελθὼν κατέδησεν** — *having come up, he bound*.

A chain of aorist participles is a sequence of steps. A present participle
among them is something going on during a step. Read the aspect and you have
the choreography.

## Agreement tells you who

The participle agrees with the person doing it. **ἰδών** is masculine
nominative singular, so it goes with the subject — the priest, the Levite,
the Samaritan, each in turn. If it were **ἰδόντα**, accusative, it would go
with the man on the ground.

This is how you track who is doing what in a long sentence: the participle
ending points at its noun the way an adjective ending did in Lesson 10.

## The article makes a noun of it

You saw this in Lesson 21: **οἱ πενθοῦντες**, *those who mourn*. The
passage ends with two of them. **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος** — *the one who did the
mercy*. **τοῦ ἐμπεσόντος εἰς τοὺς λῃστάς** — *of the one who fell among the
bandits*. Article plus participle equals *the person who*.

::: latinbridge
**Latin has participles and uses them less.** Latin's present participle
(*videns*) and perfect participle (*visus*) exist, and the ablative absolute
is built on them. But Latin's perfect participle is passive — *visus* is
*having been seen*, not *having seen* — so Latin cannot do what Greek does
in **ἰδὼν ἀντιπαρῆλθεν**. Jerome writes *et viso illo praeterivit* — *and
that man having been seen, he passed by* — because that is the shape Latin
has.

Greek's aorist active participle, *having seen*, is the thing Latin lacks,
and it is the thing that lets Greek narrative move the way it does.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ ἱερεύς** — priest *(genitive **ἱερέως**)*
- **ὁ λῃστής** — bandit, robber
- **παρέρχομαι** — I pass by *(aorist **παρῆλθον**; the passage has
  **ἀντιπαρῆλθεν**, "passed by on the other side")*
- **σπλαγχνίζομαι** — I am moved with compassion *(Koine; from **τὰ
  σπλάγχνα**, the inward parts — the place pity is felt)*
- **ὁ πλησίον** — the neighbour *(an adverb, "nearby", with the article)*
- **ἐλεέω** — I show mercy *(the verb of **τὸ ἔλεος**, Lesson 20)*
- **ποιέω** — I make, I do *(aorist **ἐποίησα**; participle **ποιήσας**)*
- **ἰδών** — having seen *(aorist participle of **ὁράω**)*

::: note
**σπλαγχνίζομαι** is the Gospels' word for the pity that grips you
physically — it is built on the word for the guts, and the nearest English
is *his heart went out to him*, which puts the feeling in a different organ.
There is no Attic equivalent. The word belongs to the Septuagint and the
Gospels and, like **ἀγάπη**, it was made for them.

**ὁ πλησίον** is an adverb, *near*, turned into a noun by the article: *the
near one*. It is the word the lawyer used in his question, and the whole
story is an answer to what it means.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros had them mark every participle in the passage with a stroke
above it and every main verb with a stroke below, and then count.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ ἱερεὺς ἰδὼν τὸν ἄνθρωπον παρῆλθεν.**
2. **ὁ Σαμαρίτης ἐλθὼν ἐποίησε τὸ ἔλεος.**
3. **ὁ λέγων τὴν ἀλήθειαν φίλος ἐστίν.**
4. **ἄνθρωπός τις ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The priest, having seen the man, passed by.* — or *saw the man and
   passed by*. **ἰδών** nominative, agreeing with **ὁ ἱερεύς**; **τὸν
   ἄνθρωπον** is what he saw.
2. *The Samaritan, having come, showed mercy* — literally *did the mercy*,
   which is the passage's own phrase.
3. *The one who speaks the truth is a friend.* — article plus present
   participle; *the speaking one*.
4. *A certain man came into the house.* — **τις** without an accent is the
   indefinite, *a certain*, *some*; with an accent, **τίς**, it would be
   *who?*. The difference is the subject of Lesson 25, and this is the first
   sight of it: the passage opens **ἄνθρωπός τις**, *a certain man*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Who did what?"}
**Σαμαρίτης δέ τις ὁδεύων ἦλθεν κατ᾽ αὐτὸν καὶ ἰδὼν ἐσπλαγχνίσθη.**

1. Find the main verbs. How many are there?
2. Find the participles. For each, say whether it is present or aorist, and
   what that tells you about when it happens relative to its main verb.
3. Who is **αὐτόν**, and how do you know he is not the one travelling?
:::

::: answers
1. Two: **ἦλθεν** (*came*) and **ἐσπλαγχνίσθη** (*was moved with
   compassion*), joined by **καί**.
2. **ὁδεύων**, present — *travelling*, going on while he came. **ἰδών**,
   aorist — *having seen*, the step before he was moved. The sequence is:
   on his journey, he came up to him; having seen, he was moved.
3. **αὐτόν** is accusative — the man on the ground. The traveller is
   **Σαμαρίτης τις**, nominative, and both participles are nominative and
   agree with him. Sabina's point exactly: the victim is in the accusative
   for the whole story.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. Having seen the man, the priest passed by.
2. The Samaritan, having come, showed mercy.
3. The one who speaks the truth is a friend.
4. And who is my neighbour?
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ ἱερεὺς ἰδὼν τὸν ἄνθρωπον παρῆλθεν.** — the participle agrees with the
   priest, not with the man. **ἰδόντα** here would make the man do the
   seeing.
2. **ὁ Σαμαρίτης ἐλθὼν ἐποίησε τὸ ἔλεος.** — accept **ἠλέησεν**, the aorist
   of **ἐλεέω**, if they are given it.
3. **ὁ λέγων τὴν ἀλήθειαν φίλος ἐστίν.**
4. **καὶ τίς ἐστίν μου πλησίον;** — this is Luke 10:29, the lawyer's
   question, word for word. **τίς** with the accent: *who?*. If the class
   can produce this unprompted, they have written a sentence of the Gospel
   from nothing, and should be told so.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Luke 10:30–37, abridged"}
The story, with two verses left out to keep it to a page. The participles
are marked in the glosses.

**ἄνθρωπός τις κατέβαινεν ἀπὸ Ἰερουσαλὴμ εἰς Ἰεριχὼ καὶ λῃσταῖς
περιέπεσεν, οἳ καὶ ἐκδύσαντες αὐτὸν καὶ πληγὰς ἐπιθέντες ἀπῆλθον ἀφέντες
ἡμιθανῆ.**

- **ἄνθρωπός τις** — a certain man
- **κατέβαινεν** — was going down *(imperfect)*
- **λῃσταῖς περιέπεσεν** — fell among bandits *(dative)*
- **οἵ** — who *(the relative, Lesson 17)*
- **ἐκδύσαντες** — having stripped *(aorist participle, plural)*
- **πληγὰς ἐπιθέντες** — having laid on blows *(aorist participle)*
- **ἀπῆλθον** — they went off *(the main verb)*
- **ἀφέντες ἡμιθανῆ** — leaving him half dead *(aorist participle)*

*A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho and fell among
bandits, who stripped him and beat him and went off, leaving him half dead.*

Three participles hanging from one verb, **ἀπῆλθον**. The bandits do three
things on the way; the grammar makes them one act of going away.

**κατὰ συγκυρίαν δὲ ἱερεύς τις κατέβαινεν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ ἐκείνῃ, καὶ ἰδὼν αὐτὸν
ἀντιπαρῆλθεν.**

- **κατὰ συγκυρίαν** — by chance
- **ἱερεύς τις** — a certain priest
- **ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ ἐκείνῃ** — on that road *(**ὁδός**, Lesson 2)*
- **ἰδὼν αὐτόν** — having seen him
- **ἀντιπαρῆλθεν** — passed by on the opposite side

*By chance a certain priest was going down on that road, and having seen
him, passed by on the other side.*

*(A Levite does the same, in the same words. Then:)*

**Σαμαρίτης δέ τις ὁδεύων ἦλθεν κατ᾽ αὐτὸν καὶ ἰδὼν ἐσπλαγχνίσθη, καὶ
προσελθὼν κατέδησεν τὰ τραύματα αὐτοῦ ἐπιχέων ἔλαιον καὶ οἶνον, ἐπιβιβάσας
δὲ αὐτὸν ἐπὶ τὸ ἴδιον κτῆνος ἤγαγεν αὐτὸν εἰς πανδοχεῖον καὶ ἐπεμελήθη
αὐτοῦ.**

- **ὁδεύων** — travelling *(present participle)*
- **ἦλθεν κατ᾽ αὐτόν** — came up to him
- **ἰδὼν ἐσπλαγχνίσθη** — having seen, was moved with compassion
- **προσελθὼν κατέδησεν** — having come up, bound *(aorist participle,
  main verb)*
- **τὰ τραύματα αὐτοῦ** — his wounds
- **ἐπιχέων ἔλαιον καὶ οἶνον** — pouring on oil and wine *(present
  participle)*
- **ἐπιβιβάσας** — having set him *(aorist participle)*
- **ἐπὶ τὸ ἴδιον κτῆνος** — on his own animal
- **ἤγαγεν** — led *(aorist of **ἄγω**, Lesson 3)*
- **εἰς πανδοχεῖον** — to an inn
- **ἐπεμελήθη αὐτοῦ** — took care of him

*A certain Samaritan, travelling, came up to him and, having seen, was
moved with compassion; and having come up he bound his wounds, pouring on
oil and wine, and having set him on his own animal led him to an inn and
took care of him.*

Five participles. Five main verbs. One man.

*(He pays the innkeeper and promises to cover the rest. Then the question:)*

**τίς τούτων τῶν τριῶν πλησίον δοκεῖ σοι γεγονέναι τοῦ ἐμπεσόντος εἰς τοὺς
λῃστάς; ὁ δὲ εἶπεν· ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ. εἶπεν δὲ αὐτῷ ὁ Ἰησοῦς·
πορεύου καὶ σὺ ποίει ὁμοίως.**

- **τίς τούτων τῶν τριῶν** — which of these three *(**τίς** with the
  accent: a question)*
- **πλησίον … γεγονέναι** — to have become neighbour *(an infinitive, and
  the construction of Lesson 16)*
- **δοκεῖ σοι** — seems to you
- **τοῦ ἐμπεσόντος** — of the one who fell among *(article + aorist
  participle, genitive)*
- **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ** — the one who did the mercy with him
- **πορεύου καὶ σὺ ποίει ὁμοίως** — go, and you do likewise *(two
  imperatives; Lesson 26)*

*"Which of these three seems to you to have become neighbour to the one who
fell among the bandits?" He said, "The one who did the mercy with him." And
Jesus said to him, "Go, and you do likewise."*

**Notice the lawyer's answer.** He does not say *the Samaritan*. He says
**ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος** — *the one who did the mercy* — a participle with
the article, naming the man by his act and not by his nation. Whether he
could not bring himself to say the word, or whether the story has taught
him to name people by what they do, the grammar does not say. It only gives
you the participle, and lets you decide.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text is quoted from memory of the standard
Greek New Testament, with verses 32 and 35 summarised rather than quoted.
Check the passage against the edition in use; **Σαμαρίτης** is also spelt
**Σαμαρείτης** in some editions, and the word order in verse 34 varies.

**On Sabina.** This is her lesson, and the point of giving it to her is that
the pupil who has been impatient with parsing all year turns out to parse
fastest when the parsing is a story — because for her it always was one.
Let her account of the passage stand as the model and build the grammar
underneath it, rather than the other way round.

**On the lawyer's answer.** The reading offered in the last paragraph is one
reading, and the text does not settle it. Say so if a pupil asks.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. The parable answers a question the lawyer did not ask — *who acted as a
   neighbour?* — and refuses the one he did — *who is my neighbour?* Does
   that count as an answer?
2. The victim is the object of every verb and the subject of none. What
   would the story be like if he were given one verb of his own?
3. The lawyer names the Samaritan by what he did, not who he was. Is that a
   generous way to name someone, or an evasive one?
:::

Marcus said that he had lost the thread at the second priest because the
sentence had too many verbs in it.

"It has one verb," said Sabina. "**ἀντιπαρῆλθεν.** The rest are
participles. If you had been reading the endings you would have known which
one was the verb and which ones were hanging from it."

"I was reading the *story*."

"So was I," said Sabina. "That is what the endings are."
