# Two Worlds, One Language Family

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"One rule," said Theodoros. "You may not say that two texts agree when they
do not."

Each pupil had been given a week, two passages — one from the classical
authors, one from scripture — and the instruction to read both, explain the
grammar of both, and say what each one meant. Now they were to present.

"That is a strange rule," said Quintus.

"It is the only rule that matters. You are going to stand up with Homer in
one hand and the Gospel in the other, and you will feel a pull to make them
say the same thing, because they are both old and both beautiful and both in
this book. Resist it. If they disagree, say so. If you cannot tell, say
that. The one thing I will not accept is a pupil smoothing two texts into
one because it felt tidier."

Julia went first, with the two first lines — Homer's **ἄνδρα** and John's
**ὁ λόγος** — and showed that both poems open by naming their subject in
the first words and then do opposite things with it: Homer asks a goddess
to tell him about his man, John asserts about his Word and asks nobody.

Quintus took Socrates and John on truth, and reported — with the air of a
man who had argued with himself for a week and lost — that they did not say
the same thing, and that he had wanted them to.

Theodosius had Protagoras's *man the measure* against *blessed are the
beggars*, and found, to his evident surprise, that it was *easier* to
present two texts that flatly disagreed than two that nearly agreed. "They
are not even arguing," he said. "They are in different rooms."

Sabina had Odysseus and the Samaritan, and said that both were stories
about a stranger whose name was the point — one who hid it, one who was
never given one — and that she did not think the two authors would have
liked each other.

Theodoros listened to all of it without a word, which was unusual, and at
the end said only: "Nobody broke the rule. I was not sure anybody would
manage that. Sit down, all of you, and we will see what you have found."
:::

## No new grammar

This is the last lesson of Part IV and it consolidates. You now have, in
your hands, four kinds of Greek:

| | When | Who wrote it | What it is like |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Homeric** | 8th c. BC and earlier | oral poets | no article; long old endings; the metre in charge |
| **Attic** | 5th–4th c. BC | Athenians writing for Athenians | the article load-bearing; subordinate clauses; **μέν … δέ** |
| **Septuagint** | 3rd c. BC onward | translators in Alexandria | grammatical and unidiomatic; Hebrew showing through |
| **New Testament** | 1st c. AD | Koine speakers writing for crowds | short clauses on **καί**; **ὅτι** and **ἵνα**; pronouns said aloud |

You can tell them apart. That is the achievement of Parts III and IV, and
it is a larger one than it looks.

## Five questions for a passage

In Lesson 18 you had four questions for a *sentence*. For a *passage* — a
whole text, with an author and an audience — add five more, and put them
first.

**One. Who wrote this, and when?** Not the name; the kind of writer. A poet
composing aloud? A philosopher? A translator with a sacred text in front of
him? A preacher writing for people who would hear it read?

**Two. For whom?** Readers or hearers? Natives or learners? People who
already agreed, or people who had to be won?

**Three. In which Greek?** Homeric, Attic, Septuagint, Koine — and what
features tell you.

**Four. What is the sentence doing?** Asserting, asking, commanding,
blessing, arguing, telling a story. The Beatitudes pronounce. The *Apology*
argues. Homer invokes.

**Five. What does it claim?** Only now. Only once you know who is speaking,
to whom, in what Greek, doing what — only then do you say what the text is
*saying*, because everything before this changes what the words mean.

## The rule, and why it is a grammar rule

*You may not say two texts agree when they do not* sounds like a rule about
honesty. It is also a rule about reading.

Two texts that *seem* to agree usually seem so because the reader has
already translated both into a third thing — a summary, a moral, a familiar
idea — and is comparing the summaries. The Greek has been left behind. A
pupil who says *Socrates and John both think truth matters* has not read
either; they have read their own sentence about both.

The cure is the Greek. Put the two sentences on the board. Ask what each
verb is doing, what each noun has for an article, what each connector joins.
Agreement that survives *that* is real. Agreement that does not was never
there.

::: latinbridge
**You have done this before, in Latin, without the rule.** Every educated
Roman child is taught Virgil and the Psalms side by side and encouraged to
hear the one in the other — *Arma virumque cano* and *Cantate Domino* — as
if they belonged to one tradition. They do not. Virgil would not have
understood a word of the Psalms' purpose, and the Psalmist had never heard
of Troy.

What Rome has done is build a culture in which both can be held at once by
one person. That is a real achievement and it is not the same as the two
texts agreeing. Learn to hold both without pretending they are one.
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros set the exercises after the presentations, so that they would be
answered with the presentations still in the room.

::: {.exercise title="Which Greek?"}
Name the variety, and the feature that gives it away.

1. **μῆνιν ἄειδε, θεά.**
2. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ ἦλθεν ὁ ποιμὴν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
3. **ὁ μὲν δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ δὲ ἄδικος ψεύδεται.**
4. **λέγει ὅτι ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.**
:::

::: answers
1. Homeric: no article, uncontracted **ἄειδε**, a goddess invoked in the
   vocative.
2. Septuagint style: **καὶ ἐγένετο** opening a narrative, **καί** linking.
   (Composed; the class should say so if they can.)
3. Attic: **μέν … δέ**, balanced clauses, no pronouns.
4. Koine: **ὅτι** + verb for reported speech, and the word **ἀγάπη**, which
   barely exists outside the Greek Bible.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Do they agree?"}
For each pair, say whether the two texts make the same claim, different
claims, or claims that do not meet. Give one reason from the Greek.

1. **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.** / **γνώσεσθε τὴν ἀλήθειαν,
   καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια ἐλευθερώσει ὑμᾶς.**
2. **πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος.** / **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ
   πνεύματι.**
3. **ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα.** / **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**
:::

::: answers
1. Different claims that *sound* alike. Socrates's noun is **βίος**, a life
   as lived, and his good is the examining; John's noun is **ἀλήθεια** and
   his verb is a future, **γνώσεσθε** — a knowing that arrives and then acts.
   One is a process, the other a result. Credit a pupil who says *both
   value truth* only if they go on to say that this is not the same as
   agreeing.
2. Claims that do not meet. Protagoras makes **ἄνθρωπος** the measure of
   everything; the Beatitude blesses **οἱ πτωχοί**, whom nobody would
   choose as a measure of anything. They are not contradicting one another;
   they are not in the same conversation. Theodosius's "different rooms" is
   exactly right.
3. Neither agreement nor disagreement — they are not claims of the same
   kind. Homer asks (**ἔννεπε**, an imperative, to a goddess); John states
   (**ἦν**, indicative). The comparison is about *shape*: both put the
   subject of the whole work in the first words. A pupil who wants to say
   they agree should be asked what claim Homer's line makes that John could
   agree with.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The man of many turns came home. *(Attic)*
2. Blessed is the one who speaks the truth. *(Gospel style)*
3. And it came to pass, and the Lord shepherded the people. *(Septuagint
   style)*
4. We know that the two books do not say the same thing. *(Attic; use
   **τὰ δύο βιβλία** and **τὸ αὐτό**, "the same thing")*
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνὴρ ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.** — Attic, with the article.
   Not Homeric; the class cannot yet write Homer and should not try.
2. **μακάριος ὁ λέγων τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — article + participle, no verb.
3. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ Κύριος ἐποίμανεν τὸν λαόν.** — accept **ποιμαίνει**;
   **ἐποίμανεν** is the aorist. **ὁ λαός**, *the people*, may need to be
   given.
4. **γιγνώσκομεν τὰ δύο βιβλία οὐ τὸ αὐτὸ λέγειν.** — accusative and
   infinitive. The sentence is this lesson's rule, written in the Greek of
   Lesson 16, and a class that can produce it has consolidated Part III and
   Part IV at once.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="The pupils' own pairs"}
There is no single text for this lesson. There are four pairs, and the
Greek of every one of them is already in this book.

**Julia.** *Odyssey* 1.1 and John 1.1.
**ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον** — **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**.
Both open by naming the subject of the whole work in the first words:
**ἄνδρα**, **ὁ λόγος**. Then they diverge. Homer's subject is an accusative
— the object of a verb of telling, with a goddess asked to do the telling.
John's is a nominative with the article — the subject of **ἦν**, asserted,
with nobody asked. One poem begins by admitting it does not know; one gospel
begins by stating what is. The grammar of the first word tells you which.

**Quintus.** *Apology* 38a and John 8:32. The pair from Lesson 23, and he
reported what the class found there: that the two sentences sound alike and
make different claims, and that he had spent a week wanting them to agree
before conceding that **βίος** and **ἀλήθεια** are not the same noun and
*examining* and *knowing* are not the same act.

**Theodosius.** Protagoras and the first Beatitude.
**πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος** — **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ
πνεύματι**. Man as the measure of all things; the destitute as the blessed.
He had expected to find it hard to set a pagan philosopher against the
Gospel. He found it easy, because the two are not arguing — they do not
share a question. The hard pair, he said, would have been two that nearly
agreed.

**Sabina.** *Odyssey* 9.366 and Luke 10:33.
**Οὖτις ἐμοί γ᾽ ὄνομα** — **Σαμαρίτης δέ τις**. A man who gives a false name
and a man who is given none. Odysseus hides behind **Οὖτις** and the trick
is in the grammar; the Samaritan is **τις**, *a certain one*, and never
more, and the story names him at the end only by what he did. Both authors,
she said, knew exactly what a name was worth and used its absence on
purpose — and she did not think they would have agreed about anything else.

**What the four have in common.** Nothing in their claims. Everything in
their method. Each pupil read the Greek first and the meaning second, said
what the endings and the articles and the connectors were doing, and only
then said what the text meant — and when the two texts disagreed, said so.

That is what Part IV was for.

::: teacheronly
**On running this lesson.** It works best as a genuine presentation lesson,
with the pupils' own pairs replacing or supplementing the four given here.
The four are models of what a good pairing looks like, not a syllabus. Any
two passages from the book may be paired; the rule is the only constraint.

**On the rule.** Hold it strictly. The failure mode is not a pupil who
says two texts disagree when they agree — that almost never happens. It is
the pupil who finds a comfortable generality that covers both and stops
there. The remedy, every time, is to send them back to the Greek: *which
word in each text is making that claim?*

**Around this lesson**, as the scope-and-sequence notes, is the time to say
to the class what the teacher's notes say about the characters: that every
one of them has got better at something they began badly at. They have
watched Felix's ear be right, Sabina's impatience with parsing turn into the
fastest parsing in the room, Julia learn to be heard, Marcus learn to stop.
Say it now, briefly, and do not say it again.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Homer, Plato, the Psalms and the Gospels are all in this book. They do
   not agree with each other about very much. What is gained by reading
   them together rather than separately?
2. Theodosius found it easier to present two texts that disagreed than two
   that nearly agreed. Why might that be true of reading in general?
3. Rome holds Virgil and the Psalms in one culture without pretending they
   are one tradition. Can a person do the same thing inside one mind?

"No," said Theodoros. "It was wrong of you not to check. You checked. That
