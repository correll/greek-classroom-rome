# Two Worlds, One Language Family

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"One rule," said Theodoros. "You may not say that two texts agree when they
do not."

Each pupil had been given a week, two passages — one from the classical
authors, one from scripture — and the instruction to read both, explain the
grammar of both, and say what each one meant. Now they were to present.

"That is a strange rule," said Quintus.

"It is the only rule that matters. You are going to stand up with Homer in
one hand and the Gospel in the other, and you will feel a pull to make them
say the same thing, because they are both old and both beautiful and both in
this book. Resist it. If they disagree, say so. If you cannot tell, say
that. The one thing I will not accept is a pupil smoothing two texts into
one because it felt tidier."

Julia went first, with the two first lines — Homer's **ἄνδρα** and John's
**ὁ λόγος** — and showed that both poems open by naming their subject in
the first words and then do opposite things with it: Homer asks a goddess
to tell him about his man, John asserts about his Word and asks nobody.

Quintus took Socrates and John on truth, and reported — with the air of a
man who had argued with himself for a week and lost — that they did not say
the same thing, and that he had wanted them to.

Theodosius had Protagoras's *man the measure* against *blessed are the
beggars*, and found, to his evident surprise, that it was *easier* to
present two texts that flatly disagreed than two that nearly agreed. "They
are not even arguing," he said. "They are in different rooms."

Sabina had Odysseus and the Samaritan, and said that both were stories
about a stranger whose name was the point — one who hid it, one who was
never given one — and that she did not think the two authors would have
liked each other.

Theodoros listened to all of it without a word, which was unusual, and at
the end said only: "Nobody broke the rule. I was not sure anybody would
manage that. Sit down, all of you, and we will see what you have found."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## No new grammar

This is the last lesson of Part IV and it consolidates. You now have, in
your hands, four kinds of Greek:

| | When | Who wrote it | What it is like |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Homeric** | 8th c. BC and earlier | oral poets | no article; long old endings; the metre in charge |
| **Attic** | 5th–4th c. BC | Athenians writing for Athenians | the article load-bearing; subordinate clauses; **μέν … δέ** |
| **Septuagint** | 3rd c. BC onward | translators in Alexandria | grammatical and unidiomatic; Hebrew showing through |
| **New Testament** | 1st c. AD | Koine speakers writing for crowds | short clauses on **καί**; **ὅτι** and **ἵνα**; pronouns said aloud |

You can tell them apart. That is the achievement of Parts III and IV, and
it is a larger one than it looks.

## Five questions for a passage

In Lesson 18 you had four questions for a *sentence*. For a *passage* — a
whole text, with an author and an audience — add five more, and put them
first.

**One. Who wrote this, and when?** Not the name; the kind of writer. A poet
composing aloud? A philosopher? A translator with a sacred text in front of
him? A preacher writing for people who would hear it read?

**Two. For whom?** Readers or hearers? Natives or learners? People who
already agreed, or people who had to be won?

**Three. In which Greek?** Homeric, Attic, Septuagint, Koine — and what
features tell you.

**Four. What is the sentence doing?** Asserting, asking, commanding,
blessing, arguing, telling a story. The Beatitudes pronounce. The *Apology*
argues. Homer invokes.

**Five. What does it claim?** Only now. Only once you know who is speaking,
to whom, in what Greek, doing what — only then do you say what the text is
*saying*, because everything before this changes what the words mean.

## The rule, and why it is a grammar rule

*You may not say two texts agree when they do not* sounds like a rule about
honesty. It is also a rule about reading.

Two texts that *seem* to agree usually seem so because the reader has
already translated both into a third thing — a summary, a moral, a familiar
idea — and is comparing the summaries. The Greek has been left behind. A
pupil who says *Socrates and John both think truth matters* has not read
either; they have read their own sentence about both.

The cure is the Greek. Put the two sentences on the board. Ask what each
verb is doing, what each noun has for an article, what each connector joins.
Agreement that survives *that* is real. Agreement that does not was never
there.

::: latinbridge
**You have done this before, in Latin, without the rule.** Every educated
Roman child is taught Virgil and the Psalms side by side and encouraged to
hear the one in the other — *Arma virumque cano* and *Cantate Domino* — as
if they belonged to one tradition. They do not. Virgil would not have
understood a word of the Psalms' purpose, and the Psalmist had never heard
of Troy.

What Rome has done is build a culture in which both can be held at once by
one person. That is a real achievement and it is not the same as the two
texts agreeing. Learn to hold both without pretending they are one.
:::
:::

::: vocab
No new words. Twelve old ones, each of which has turned up in more than one
of the four Greeks, with the lesson that first gave it to you. These are
the words the pairs are built from, and they are the ones to be sure of.

- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — human being, person *(Lesson 1; Protagoras's measure,
  Luke's **ἄνθρωπός τις**)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — word; account; reason *(Lesson 1; John's first subject;
  the fable's **ὁ λόγος δηλοῖ**)*
- **ὁ θεός** — god *(Lesson 2; Homer's **θεά**, John's **ὁ θεός**)*
- **ὁ οὐρανός** — sky, heaven *(Lesson 2; plural in the Gospel)*
- **ἡ ἀλήθεια** — truth *(Lesson 4; the noun Socrates and John do not
  share)*
- **ἡ ἀρχή** — beginning; rule *(Lesson 6; **ἐν ἀρχῇ**, and Cyrus's
  province)*
- **τὸ ὄνομα** — name *(Lesson 8; Odysseus's false one, the Samaritan's
  missing one)*
- **ὁ ἀνήρ** — man, as against a woman *(Lesson 15; Homer's **ἄνδρα**)*
- **ὁ βίος** — life as it is lived *(Lesson 17; Socrates's noun)*
- **ἡ ἀγάπη** — love *(Lesson 19; Koine — the word that gives a Gospel
  away)*
- **μακάριος** — blessed, beyond the reach of trouble *(Lesson 21; Homer's
  gods, Matthew's beggars)*
- **θέλω** — I want *(Lesson 23; Koine — Attic **ἐθέλω**, Lesson 11)*

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From Lessons 20 to 23. Answer from memory.

1. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.** Which Greek, and what two things tell you?
2. **ἐδίδασκε** and **ἐδίδαξε** — which is imperfect and which aorist?
   Translate both.
3. **θέλω** and **ἐθέλω** — which is Attic and which Koine? What happened
   to the word?
4. Spell **ἡ θάλαττα** the Koine way. What other Attic spelling changes
   in the same way?
5. **μακάριος ὁ λέγων τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** Translate. What is **λέγων**, and
   what has the article made of it?
6. **ἰδὼν αὐτὸν ἀντιπαρῆλθεν.** Who saw? How does the ending of **ἰδών**
   tell you?
7. **λέγει τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἀγαθὸν εἶναι.** Say it the Koine way.
8. **ὡς φίλον** and **ὥσπερ ποιμήν** — translate both. Which lesson gave
   you each word?
9. Give the genitive of **τὸ πνεῦμα**, **ὁ ἱερεύς**, **ὁ ἄρχων**.
10. **ἄνθρωπός τις κατέβαινεν ἀπὸ Ἰερουσαλήμ.** Why is the verb imperfect?
    What does the tense do for the bandits who are about to arrive?
:::

::: answers
1. The Septuagint's: **Κύριος** without the article, as a name, and a verb
   of herding used of a god — a metaphor where Attic would have a simile
   (Lesson 20).
2. **ἐδίδασκε** imperfect (*he was teaching*, *he went on teaching*);
   **ἐδίδαξε** aorist (*he taught*, once — the **σ** after the stem,
   with **κ** + **σ** written **ξ**).
3. **ἐθέλω** Attic, **θέλω** Koine. The first vowel wore off in speech;
   the past tense **ἤθελον** never changed.
4. **θάλασσα**; **πράττω** becomes **πράσσω** — Attic **-ττ-** is Koine
   **-σσ-** throughout.
5. *Blessed is the one who speaks the truth.* A present participle; the
   article has made it a noun, *the speaking one*, and the formula needs
   no verb (Lesson 21, Lesson 22).
6. The priest — or the Levite, or the Samaritan; in each sentence the one
   in the nominative. **ἰδών** is nominative singular masculine, so it
   agrees with the subject, not with **αὐτόν**; **ἰδόντα** would make the
   man on the ground do the seeing.
7. **λέγει ὅτι ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἀγαθός ἐστιν.** — the accusative becomes a
   nominative with a verb of its own.
8. *As a friend*; *just like a shepherd*. **ὡς** is Lesson 23's, **ὥσπερ**
   Lesson 20's — and **ὥσπερ** is **ὡς** with **περ** on the end to make
   it emphatic.
9. **πνεύματος**, **ἱερέως**, **ἄρχοντος**.
10. Because the going-down is the background: it was in progress, and
    something happened in the middle of it. The imperfect leaves the man
    on the road so that the aorist **περιέπεσεν** — the bandits — can
    interrupt him. Aorist for the event, imperfect for what it interrupts
    (Lesson 14, still working).
:::

::: {.exercise title="Which Greek?"}
Name the variety, and the feature that gives it away.

1. **μῆνιν ἄειδε, θεά.**
2. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ ἦλθεν ὁ ποιμὴν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
3. **ὁ μὲν δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ δὲ ἄδικος ψεύδεται.**
4. **λέγει ὅτι ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.**
5. **ἐγὼ θέλω ὑπάγειν εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν.**
6. **ὁ δὲ στρατηγὸς τοὺς Ἕλληνας ἄγει ἐπὶ τὴν θάλατταν.**
7. **καὶ εὐλόγησεν Κύριος τὸν οἶκον αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.**
8. **Οὖτις ἐμοί γ᾽ ὄνομα· Οὖτιν δέ με κικλήσκουσι.**
9. **οἱ μὲν οὖν Ἕλληνες ἀνέβαινον, ὁ δὲ Κῦρος αὐτοὺς ἦγεν.**
10. **μακάριος ὁ ἀνὴρ ὃς οὐκ ἦλθεν εἰς τὴν ὁδὸν τῶν κακῶν.**
:::

::: answers
1. Homeric: no article, uncontracted **ἄειδε**, a goddess invoked in the
   vocative.
2. Septuagint style: **καὶ ἐγένετο** opening a narrative, **καί** linking.
   (Composed; the class should say so if they can.)
3. Attic: **μέν … δέ**, balanced clauses, no pronouns.
4. Koine: **ὅτι** + verb for reported speech, and the word **ἀγάπη**, which
   barely exists outside the Greek Bible.
5. Koine, three times over: **θέλω**, **ὑπάγω**, **θάλασσα** — and **ἐγώ**
   said aloud. (Composed.)
6. Attic: **θάλατταν**. Nothing else in the sentence decides it, and the
   pupil should say so; one letter is enough. (Composed.)
7. Septuagint style: **καί** opening, **Κύριος** as a name without the
   article, **αὐτοῦ** after its noun where Attic would not bother, **εἰς
   τὸν αἰῶνα**. (Composed, in the Psalm's manner; the class should say
   so.)
8. Homeric: no article, the old verb **κικλήσκουσι** for *they call*,
   **ἐμοί γ᾽** with its particle, the verb *is* left out. Lesson 16; a
   pupil who names the speaker has done more than was asked.
9. Attic: **μὲν οὖν … δέ**, two imperfects laid side by side for a scene
   in progress, Xenophon's names. **αὐτούς** is an object, which Attic
   needs; it is the *subject* pronoun that Attic drops. (Composed.)
10. Septuagint or Gospel — the class may say either, and should be asked
    what would decide. The **μακάριος** formula with no verb and the
    relative clause after it are the Psalms' opening shape (Psalm 1 begins
    almost exactly so); **ὁ ἀνήρ** with the article is the Septuagint's
    Hebrew showing through. (Composed, after Psalm 1:1.)
:::

::: {.exercise title="Do they agree?"}
For each pair, say whether the two texts make the same claim, different
claims, or claims that do not meet. Give one reason from the Greek.

1. **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.** / **γνώσεσθε τὴν ἀλήθειαν,
   καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια ἐλευθερώσει ὑμᾶς.**
2. **πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος.** / **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ
   πνεύματι.**
3. **ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα.** / **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**
4. **Κύριος ποιμαίνει με.** / **ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός.**
5. **πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος.** / **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ
   βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.**
6. **ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.** / **μῆνιν ἄειδε, θεά.**
7. **ἰδὼν αὐτὸν ἀντιπαρῆλθεν.** / **ἰδὼν ἐσπλαγχνίσθη.**
8. **Οὖτις ἐμοί γ᾽ ὄνομα.** / **Σαμαρίτης δέ τις.**
9. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.** / **πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος.**
10. **σὺ δέ μοι δὸς ξείνιον, ὥς περ ὑπέστης.** / **ἔδωκεν δύο δηνάρια τῷ
    πανδοχεῖ.**
:::

::: answers
1. Different claims that *sound* alike. Socrates's noun is **βίος**, a life
   as lived, and his good is the examining; John's noun is **ἀλήθεια** and
   his verb is a future, **γνώσεσθε** — a knowing that arrives and then acts.
   One is a process, the other a result. Credit a pupil who says *both
   value truth* only if they go on to say that this is not the same as
   agreeing.
2. Claims that do not meet. Protagoras makes **ἄνθρωπος** the measure of
   everything; the Beatitude blesses **οἱ πτωχοί**, whom nobody would
   choose as a measure of anything. They are not contradicting one another;
   they are not in the same conversation. Theodosius's "different rooms" is
   exactly right.
3. Neither agreement nor disagreement — they are not claims of the same
   kind. Homer asks (**ἔννεπε**, an imperative, to a goddess); John states
   (**ἦν**, indicative). The comparison is about *shape*: both put the
   subject of the whole work in the first words. A pupil who wants to say
   they agree should be asked what claim Homer's line makes that John could
   agree with.
4. Claims that meet, and are meant to. The Psalm says who the shepherd is
   — **Κύριος**, a name, no article; John has the speaker claim the title
   — **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός**, two articles, *the* shepherd and no other. The
   second sentence is written to be heard against the first. Whether that
   makes them *agree* is a question the Greek cannot settle; the class
   should see that the second text knows the first, which is a different
   thing from saying the same.
5. Different claims, in the same room — the pair Theodosius said would be
   harder. Both are Attic, both are about **ἄνθρωπος**, and they
   disagree: Protagoras makes man the measure of everything, so that
   nothing stands above him to examine him by; Socrates makes a human life
   answerable to examination, which means there is a measure a life can
   fail. The dative **ἀνθρώπῳ** — *for a man* — puts the human being on
   the receiving end of the judgement, where Protagoras's nominative had
   him giving it.
6. Claims that do not meet. **ὁ θεός**, singular with the article, *the
   God*, is said to *be* something; **θεά**, a goddess among many, is
   *asked* to sing of anger. One text defines its god; the other gives
   hers a job. The pupil who says *both are about gods* has summarised,
   and the rule says to send them back to the article.
7. Different claims — from the same story, four words apart. The
   participle is identical, **ἰδών**; the main verb is the opposite. The
   whole parable turns on two men doing the same first thing and not the
   same second thing, and the exercise is here to show that the rule
   works inside one text as well as between two.
8. Claims of the same kind, pointing opposite ways — Sabina's pair. Both
   authors withhold a name on purpose, and the Greek shows it: Homer's
   **Οὖτις** is a name that is also the word *nobody*; Luke's **τις** is
   the indefinite, *a certain one*, and the man never gets more. One
   hides a name to escape; one is given none and is named at the end by
   his act. Agreement about what a name is worth; nothing else.
9. Claims that meet and collide. Both say what stands first: John puts
   **ὁ λόγος** at **ἀρχή**, with the article and the imperfect **ἦν** —
   it *was*, already, before anything; Protagoras puts **ἄνθρωπος**
   without the article, *a human being*, any one, as the **μέτρον**, in
   the present, **ἐστίν**. Each answers the other's question with a
   different noun. This is the pair that *nearly* agrees in shape and
   does not in substance.
10. Claims that meet — both are about what one owes a stranger — and go
    opposite ways. Odysseus *demands* the gift: **δός**, an imperative,
    and **ὥς περ ὑπέστης**, *as you promised*: the guest-gift is a debt
    the host owes by custom. The Samaritan *gives*: **ἔδωκεν**, a plain
    aorist, with no request before it and no custom behind it. Homer's
    world has a rule about strangers; Luke's story has a man who did not
    need one.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The man of many turns came home. *(Attic)*
2. Blessed is the one who speaks the truth. *(Gospel style)*
3. And it came to pass, and the Lord shepherded the people. *(Septuagint
   style)*
4. We know that the two books do not say the same thing. *(Attic; use
   **τὰ δύο βιβλία** and **τὸ αὐτό**, "the same thing")*
5. The child wants to go up to the sea. *(Koine)*
6. The general led the Greeks to the sea. *(Attic)*
7. Blessed are the pure in heart. *(Gospel style)*
8. And the Lord blessed the house from heaven forever. *(Septuagint
   style)*
9. The priest, having seen the man, passed by; but the Samaritan, having
   seen him, showed mercy. *(Gospel style; use participles)*
10. The teacher says that the man of many turns is wise. *(Attic, then
    Koine)*
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνὴρ οἴκαδε ἦλθεν.** — Attic, with the article;
   **οἴκαδε**, *homeward*, is the Attic for Homer's **οἶκόνδε** (Lesson
   15). Accept **εἰς τὸν οἶκον**, *into the house*. Not Homeric; the class cannot yet write Homer and should not try.
2. **μακάριος ὁ λέγων τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — article + participle, no verb.
3. **καὶ ἐγένετο, καὶ Κύριος ἐποίμανεν τὸν λαόν.** — accept **ποιμαίνει**;
   **ἐποίμανεν** is the aorist. **ὁ λαός**, *the people*, may need to be
   given.
4. **γιγνώσκομεν τὰ δύο βιβλία οὐ τὸ αὐτὸ λέγειν.** — accusative and
   infinitive. The sentence is this lesson's rule, written in the Greek of
   Lesson 16, and a class that can produce it has consolidated Part III and
   Part IV at once.
5. **τὸ παιδίον θέλει ἀναβαίνειν εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν.** — three Koine marks
   in five words: **παιδίον**, **θέλει**, **θάλασσαν**. **βούλεται** or
   **θάλατταν** would each pull the sentence back into the other column.
6. **ὁ στρατηγὸς τοὺς Ἕλληνας ἤγαγεν ἐπὶ τὴν θάλατταν.** — **ἤγαγεν**,
   the aorist of **ἄγω**; **θάλατταν**. Accept **ἦγεν** if the pupil
   wants the march still in progress, and ask why.
7. **μακάριοι οἱ καθαροὶ τῇ καρδίᾳ.** — Matthew 5:8, word for word. No
   verb; dative of respect. The pupil who adds **εἰσίν** has written
   Attic grammar over a Gospel formula.
8. **καὶ εὐλόγησεν Κύριος τὸν οἶκον ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα.** —
   **καί** first, **Κύριος** bare, **εὐλόγησεν** the aorist of **εὐλογέω**
   (Lesson 20), **ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ** singular (the Septuagint has both
   numbers; the plural is the Gospel's habit), **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** for
   *forever*. **ὁ Κύριος** with the article is the
   mark of a pupil writing Attic by habit.
9. **ὁ ἱερεὺς ἰδὼν τὸν ἄνθρωπον παρῆλθεν, ὁ δὲ Σαμαρίτης ἰδὼν αὐτὸν
   ἠλέησεν.** — or **ἐποίησε τὸ ἔλεος**, the parable's own phrase. Both
   participles nominative, each agreeing with its own man; **ὁ δέ** for
   the turn. Accept **ἀντιπαρῆλθεν**. The sentence is Lesson 22's whole
   plot in one line.
10. Attic **ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγει τὸν πολύτροπον ἄνδρα σοφὸν εἶναι.** Koine
    **ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγει ὅτι ὁ πολύτροπος ἄνθρωπος σοφός ἐστιν.** — the
    construction changes, and so, if the pupil is listening, does the
    noun: Koine reaches for **ἄνθρωπος** where Attic has **ἀνήρ**. Accept
    **ἀνήρ** in the Koine; **πολύτροπος** itself is Homer's word and sits
    oddly in both, which is worth a remark.
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="The pupils' own pairs"}
There is no single text for this lesson. There are four pairs, and the
Greek of every one of them is already in this book.

**Julia.** *Odyssey* 1.1 and John 1:1.
**ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον** — **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**.
Both open by naming the subject of the whole work in the first words:
**ἄνδρα**, **ὁ λόγος**. Then they diverge. Homer's subject is an accusative
— the object of a verb of telling, with a goddess asked to do the telling.
John's is a nominative with the article — the subject of **ἦν**, asserted,
with nobody asked. One poem begins by admitting it does not know; one gospel
begins by stating what is. The grammar of the first word tells you which.

**Quintus.** *Apology* 38a and John 8:32. The pair from Lesson 23, and he
reported what the class found there: that the two sentences sound alike and
make different claims, and that he had spent a week wanting them to agree
before conceding that **βίος** and **ἀλήθεια** are not the same noun and
*examining* and *knowing* are not the same act.

**Theodosius.** Protagoras and the first Beatitude.
**πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος** — **μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ
πνεύματι**. Man as the measure of all things; the destitute as the blessed.
He had expected to find it hard to set a pagan philosopher against the
Gospel. He found it easy, because the two are not arguing — they do not
share a question. The hard pair, he said, would have been two that nearly
agreed.

**Sabina.** *Odyssey* 9.366 and Luke 10:33.
**Οὖτις ἐμοί γ᾽ ὄνομα** — **Σαμαρίτης δέ τις**. A man who gives a false name
and a man who is given none. Odysseus hides behind **Οὖτις** and the trick
is in the grammar; the Samaritan is **τις**, *a certain one*, and never
more, and the story names him at the end only by what he did. Both authors,
she said, knew exactly what a name was worth and used its absence on
purpose — and she did not think they would have agreed about anything else.

**What the four have in common.** Nothing in their claims. Everything in
their method. Each pupil read the Greek first and the meaning second, said
what the endings and the articles and the connectors were doing, and only
then said what the text meant — and when the two texts disagreed, said so.

That is what Part IV was for.

::: teacheronly
**On running this lesson.** It works best as a genuine presentation lesson,
with the pupils' own pairs replacing or supplementing the four given here.
The four are models of what a good pairing looks like, not a syllabus. Any
two passages from the book may be paired; the rule is the only constraint.

**On the rule.** Hold it strictly. The failure mode is not a pupil who
says two texts disagree when they agree — that almost never happens. It is
the pupil who finds a comfortable generality that covers both and stops
there. The remedy, every time, is to send them back to the Greek: *which
word in each text is making that claim?*

**If it was not said at Lesson 18**, where the scope-and-sequence suggests
it, this is the time to say to the class what the teacher's notes say about
the characters: that every
one of them has got better at something they began badly at. They have
watched Felix's ear be right, Sabina's impatience with parsing turn into the
fastest parsing in the room, Julia learn to be heard, Marcus learn to stop.
Say it now, briefly, and do not say it again.
:::
:::

::: {.reading time="20 min" title="The wind and the sun" source="Aesop, *The North Wind and the Sun* (Perry 46), in the Attic-ising Greek of the late collections (Chambry 73), with an adapted tier"}
A fifth Greek, to end the Part with — and a test of the Five Questions.
Everyone in the room knows this fable; nobody has read it in Greek.
Aesop, if he lived, was a slave of the sixth century BC and wrote nothing
down. The text below is from a collection put together perhaps a thousand
years later, by someone writing a careful, bookish Greek that *tries* to
be Attic and does not quite manage it. Read the adapted version first;
then the collection's text; then say which Greek it is, and why that is a
hard question.

**Adapted** *(composed for this lesson from the fable, in the words and
forms you have)*

ὁ Βορέας καὶ ὁ Ἥλιος περὶ τῆς δυνάμεως ἔλεγον· ὁ μὲν γὰρ Βορέας ἔλεγεν
ἑαυτὸν μέγαν εἶναι, ὁ δὲ Ἥλιος ἑαυτόν. ἔπειτα δὲ ἄνθρωπον ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ εἶδον,
καὶ εἶπεν ὁ Ἥλιος· ὁ τὸ ἱμάτιον ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου λαβὼν μέγας ἐστίν.

πρῶτον οὖν ὁ Βορέας ἔπνει μέγα· ὁ δὲ ἄνθρωπος, ὅτι ψυχρὸς ἦν, τὸ ἱμάτιον
ἔτι μᾶλλον εἶχεν. ὁ δὲ Βορέας ἔτι μᾶλλον ἔπνει· ὁ δὲ ἄνθρωπος καὶ ἄλλο
ἱμάτιον ἔλαβεν. τέλος δὲ ὁ Βορέας οὐκέτι ἐδύνατο.

ἔπειτα δὲ ὁ Ἥλιος πρῶτον μὲν μικρὸν ἔλαμπεν, καὶ ὁ ἄνθρωπος τὸ ἄλλο

**The collection's text** *(Chambry 73 — not Aesop's words, which nobody
has, but the Greek of a late collector writing in the Attic manner)*

ἀπονεῖμαι, ὃς ἂν αὐτῶν ἄνθρωπον ὁδοιπόρον ἀποδύσῃ. Καὶ ὁ Βορέας ἀρξάμενος
σφοδρὸς ἦν· τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου ἀντεχομένου τῆς ἐσθῆτος μᾶλλον ἐπέκειτο. Ὁ δὲ
ὑπὸ τοῦ ψύχους καταπονούμενος ἔτι μᾶλλον καὶ περιττοτέραν ἐσθῆτα
προσελάμβανεν, ἕως ἀποκαμὼν ὁ Βορέας τῷ Ἡλίῳ μεταπαρέδωκε. Κἀκεῖνος τὸ
μὲν πρῶτον μετρίως προσέλαμψε· τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου τὰ περισσὰ τῶν ἱματίων
ἀποτιθεμένου σφοδρότερον τὸ καῦμα ἐπέτεινε, μέχρις οὗ πρὸς τὴν ἀλέαν
ἀντέχειν μὴ δυνάμενος ἀποδυσάμενος ποταμοῦ παραρρέοντος ἐπὶ λουτρὸν
ἀπῄει.

Ὁ λόγος δηλοῖ ὅτι πολλάκις τὸ πείθειν τοῦ βιάζεσθαι ἀνυστικώτερόν ἐστι.

- **μᾶλλον** — more

  whole)*

- **ἔλιπεν** — left off, took off *(aorist of **λείπω**, Lesson 6; the
  aorist drops the **ε**)*
- **θερμός** — hot *(English *thermal*)*
- **δηλοῖ** — shows, makes plain *(a contract verb, Lesson 34; take it

- **ὁ πείθων** — the one who persuades *(present participle of **πείθω**,

- **πολλάκις** — often *(**πολλοί** inside it)*
- **ἡ βία** — force
- **περὶ δυνάμεως** — about strength *(**ἡ δύναμις**, power; **δύναμαι**

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted text aloud. Then read the collection's text and find the
places where it is doing something the adaptation could not.

1. The label calls the text *Attic-ising*. Find **περιττοτέραν** and
   **περισσά**. What is the difference between them, and what does it tell
   you about the writer?
2. In the adapted text, which verbs are imperfect and which aorist? Why is
   **ἔπνει** imperfect and **ἔλαβεν** aorist?
3. **ὁ τὸ ἱμάτιον ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου λαβὼν μέγας ἐστίν.** Where is the
   subject of this sentence? What has the first word done?
4. The original has **ὃς ἂν αὐτῶν ἄνθρωπον ὁδοιπόρον ἀποδύσῃ** where the
   adaptation has the sentence of question 3. Which is a rule for any
   case and which a statement about one? What word in the original marks
   the difference?
5. **τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου ἀντεχομένου τῆς ἐσθῆτος.** Two genitives making a
   clause, as in Matthew's **καθίσαντος αὐτοῦ**. Find the two other places
   in the fable where the writer does this. What does the construction
   let him do that the adaptation had to do with **ὅτι … ἦν**?
6. **Κἀκεῖνος.** What two words are inside it, and who is **ἐκεῖνος**?
7. **ὁ λόγος δηλοῖ ὅτι …** Is this **ὅτι** *that* or *because*? Which
   column of Lesson 23's table does the construction come from — and does
   that settle which Greek the writer is writing?
8. Put the Five Questions to the fable: who wrote it, for whom, in which
   Greek, doing what, claiming what? One line each.
9. The rule, one last time. **τὸ πείθειν τοῦ βιάζεσθαι ἀνυστικώτερόν
   ἐστι** beside **μακάριοι οἱ πραεῖς, ὅτι αὐτοὶ κληρονομήσουσιν τὴν γῆν.**
   Do they agree? One reason from the Greek.
10. Is this Aesop's Greek? Which of the four Greeks of the table is the
    text closest to, and why does it fit none of them exactly?
:::

::: answers
1. The same word for *surplus, excessive* spelt twice: once with Attic
   **-ττ-**, once with Koine **-σσ-** (Lesson 23's row on spelling). The
   writer is aiming at Attic and his own Greek is showing through — he
   remembered the Attic spelling in one sentence and forgot it in the
   next. That is what *Attic-ising* means: not Attic, but trying.
2. Imperfect: **ἔλεγον**, **ἔλεγεν**, **ἔπνει**, **εἶχεν**, **ἐδύνατο**,
   **ἔλαμπεν**, **ἦν**. Aorist: **εἶδον**, **εἶπεν**, **ἔλαβεν**,
   **ἔλιπεν**, **ἔδραμεν**. The blowing goes on, and the man's holding on
   goes on through it; taking a second cloak is one act, done and
   finished. The fable is built like Lesson 14's tablet: a background of
   imperfects with aorists happening against it.
3. The subject is the whole phrase **ὁ … λαβών**, *the one who has taken
   the cloak from the man*, and it is in the nominative — **ὁ** at the
   start and **λαβών** at the end are its two ends, with the object
   **τὸ ἱμάτιον** and its **ἀπό**-phrase packed between them. The article
   has done what it did in Lesson 22: turned a participle into *the one
   who*. A pupil who makes **τὸ ἱμάτιον** the subject has read the first
   accusative as a nominative — the error of Lesson 3, still available.
4. The original is the rule: *whichever of them should strip* — any wind,
   any sun, any traveller. The adaptation is a statement: *the one who
   has taken it is great*, said of this traveller, after the fact. The
   mark is **ἄν**, the little word that makes a clause general and
   conditional, and which drags the verb into a form (Lesson 35) the
   class cannot yet use. The adaptation lost the *if*.
5. **τοῦ δὲ ἀνθρώπου … ἀποτιθεμένου** (*as the man was putting off*) and
   **ποταμοῦ παραρρέοντος** (*a river flowing past*). The construction
   sets a scene in two words and lets the main clause get on with the
   story: *the man holding on, the wind pressed harder*. The adaptation
   had to stop and give the man a sentence of his own — **ὅτι ψυχρὸς
   ἦν** — each time. Three genitive clauses in nine lines is a writer who
   has read his Attic prose and is showing it.
6. **καὶ ἐκεῖνος** — *and that one*, run together with the mark over the
   **ἀ** to show where the join is. **ἐκεῖνος** is the Sun: *that one*
   as against the Wind who has just given up. The pupil should notice
   that this is the first time the Sun has had a word of his own.
7. *That* — it follows a verb of showing, so it reports what the fable
   shows. The construction is the Koine column: **ὅτι** + verb where
   strict Attic would prefer the accusative and infinitive. But Attic
   *can* do this (Lesson 23 said so), and a formula repeated at the end
   of two hundred fables is not evidence of much. It does not settle the
   question; it leans.
8. *Who:* an anonymous compiler, late, educated, writing down stories that
   had been told aloud for a thousand years. *For whom:* readers — people
   learning Greek, probably, since fables were a school text; perhaps
   children like these. *Which Greek:* a literary Koine dressed as Attic
   — see question 10. *Doing what:* telling a story and then stating its
   moral; narrative, then a flat assertion with **δηλοῖ**. *Claiming:*
   that persuasion gets more done than force — a claim about
   effectiveness, note, not about right.
9. They nearly agree, and the rule says to look harder. Aesop's claim is
   a comparative of *usefulness* — **ἀνυστικώτερον**, *more effective*:
   gentleness is recommended because it works. The Beatitude is a
   blessing with a future promise — **μακάριοι … κληρονομήσουσιν** — and
   says nothing about whether the gentle get anything done; it says they
   are blessed, and that the earth will be theirs. One is advice to
   winds; the other is a pronouncement over people who are not winning.
   A pupil who says *both prefer gentleness* has found the comfortable
   generality the lesson warned about, and should be sent back to
   **ἀνυστικώτερον**.
10. No — Aesop's Greek, if he had any, is lost; this is the Greek of a
    collector writing centuries after the New Testament. It is closest to
    Attic in its ambitions — participles, genitive clauses, the
    infinitive with the article, **μέν … δέ**, **-ττ-** — and to Koine in
    its slips — **-σσ-**, **ὅτι** after **δηλοῖ**, and a word,
    **ἀνυστικός**, that no classical author uses. It fits none of the four rows
    because it is not a living variety at all: it is a learned imitation
    of one by a speaker of another, which is a fifth thing, and the
    thing most Greek written after AD 100 actually is. The class has
    just read a text whose author was doing what they are doing — writing
    Attic in a Koine world — and did it imperfectly, and that is the
    kindest possible end to Part IV.
:::
