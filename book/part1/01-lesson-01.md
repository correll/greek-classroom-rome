# The Unwelcome Tablet

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Marcus arrived late. He did not hurry, because hurrying would have
suggested that he thought arriving on time mattered.

Felix had already drawn a horse on his wax tablet. It was a good horse.
Julia had finished copying the day's Latin exercise, twice, and was now
sitting with her hands folded in the manner of someone who wishes it to be
noticed that she has finished. Sabina was telling Aelia about a man who had
tricked a king, and Quintus was explaining to nobody in particular that
tardiness was a moral question rather than a practical one. Livia was
watching the door.

Theodoros came in carrying a manuscript. He set it down open on the table,
where all of them could see it.

The page was covered in small, even writing. Not one of them could read a
word of it.

"Today," he said, "we begin Greek. Which means we begin where every
language begins. With the letters."

Marcus did not look at the page. "But we already know letters."

"Excellent," said Theodoros. "Then you should have no difficulty learning
new ones."

Felix whispered, not quite quietly enough, that perhaps Greek letters were
Latin letters that had gone wrong on a long journey.

Theodoros heard him. He did not say anything. He walked to the board, and
in large, careful strokes he wrote:

**ΑΘΗΝΑ**

"Tell me," he said, "what you already know."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Twenty-four letters

The Greek alphabet has twenty-four letters. You know more of them than you
expect to, because the Latin alphabet is descended from the Greek one — the
Romans borrowed it, by way of the Etruscans, and changed it as they went.

Each letter has a **name** and a **sound**, and the two are not the same
thing. The letter whose name is **βῆτα** makes the sound *b*; its name is
not its sound any more than the English letter named *double-u* makes the
sound *double-u*.

| | Name | Sound | | | Name | Sound |
|:--|:--|:--|:-:|:--|:--|:--|
| Α α | **ἄλφα** | *a* | | Ν ν | **νῦ** | *n* |
| Β β | **βῆτα** | *b* | | Ξ ξ | **ξῖ** | *ks* |
| Γ γ | **γάμμα** | *g* (hard) | | Ο ο | **ὂ μικρόν** | short *o* |
| Δ δ | **δέλτα** | *d* | | Π π | **πῖ** | *p* |
| Ε ε | **ἒ ψιλόν** | short *e* | | Ρ ρ | **ῥῶ** | *r* |
| Ζ ζ | **ζῆτα** | *zd* | | Σ σ ς | **σίγμα** | *s* |
| Η η | **ἦτα** | long *e* | | Τ τ | **ταῦ** | *t* |
| Θ θ | **θῆτα** | *t* + puff | | Υ υ | **ὖ ψιλόν** | French *u* |
| Ι ι | **ἰῶτα** | *i* | | Φ φ | **φῖ** | *p* + puff |
| Κ κ | **κάππα** | *k* | | Χ χ | **χῖ** | *k* + puff |
| Λ λ | **λάμβδα** | *l* | | Ψ ψ | **ψῖ** | *ps* |
| Μ μ | **μῦ** | *m* | | Ω ω | **ὦ μέγα** | long *o* |

Learn them in this order. It is the order the Greeks used, the order the
Romans borrowed, and the order every dictionary you will ever open is
arranged in.

## The ones that will catch you

Six letters look like Latin letters and are not.

| Looks like | Actually is | Example |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Η η** — an *h* | long **e** | **ἦτα** is *ēta*, not *hēta* |
| **Ρ ρ** — a *p* | **r** | **ῥῶ** is *rhō* |
| **Χ χ** — an *x* | **kh** | **χῖ** is *khī* |
| **Υ υ** — a *y* | **u**, French-style | **ὖ ψιλόν** is *ü psilon* |
| **Ω ω** — a *w* | long **o** | **ὦ μέγα** is *ō mega* |
| **Ν ν** — a *v* | **n** | **νῦ** is *nü* |

Marcus will get three of these wrong before the end of the week. So will
you. Then you will stop.

## σ and ς

One letter, two shapes. **ς** goes at the end of a word and **σ**
everywhere else:

**σοφός** — *wise*. Never *σοφοσ*, never *ςοφός*. <!--nocheck-->

## The sound of two names

Theodoros wrote **ΑΘΗΝΑ**. Letter by letter: **Α Θ Η Ν Α** — *a*, *th*
(with a puff), long *e*, *n*, *a*. Sound it out: *a-thē-na*.

In the lower-case letters you will use in this book, that word is written
two different ways depending on what it means:

- **ἡ Ἀθηνᾶ** — the goddess Athena.
- **αἱ Ἀθῆναι** — the city of Athens. The Greeks called it a plural, as
  though it were a cluster of places rather than one.

::: note
**The Greek in this book is not the Greek on Theodoros's board.**

In AD 400 all Greek was written in capitals, run together without spaces
between words, and with no accents or breathing marks at all. The board
would have read:

**ΑΘΗΝΑ** — and a line of Homer would have read **ΜΗΝΙΝΑΕΙΔΕΘΕΑ**.

The small letters, the accents, the breathings, and the spaces between
words are all later inventions — some from Alexandrian scholars around
200 BC, the small letters not until about four hundred years *after*
Theodoros. Modern printed editions use them because they make reading
enormously easier. This book uses them for the same reason.

But remember what has been added. Every accent you see is a scholar's
judgement about how a word was said, made centuries after the last person
said it.
:::
:::

::: vocab
Five words. Learn each one *with the little word in front of it* — that
word tells you the noun's gender, which you cannot always guess from the
ending. Lesson 3 explains why this matters.

- **ὁ λόγος** — word, speech, account, reason
- **ὁ φίλος** — friend
- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — human being, person
- **ἡ ψυχή** — soul, life, self
- **τὸ δῶρον** — gift
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros did not explain the alphabet. He wrote **ΑΘΗΝΑ** and asked the
class what they already knew, which is a different kind of lesson.

Aelia said she knew the word meant Athens, because her uncle had been
there.

"That is not what I asked," said Theodoros. "I asked what you know. You
have told me what you remember."

::: {.exercise title="What do you recognise?"}
Look at **ΑΘΗΝΑ** without being told what it says.

1. How many letters are there? How many *different* letters?
2. Which of them could you have found in a Latin inscription?
3. Which one is not a Latin letter at all?
4. The letter **Η** appears once. If you assumed it was the Latin *H*, what
   would you think the word sounded like? Why would you be wrong?
:::

::: answers
1. Five letters; four different ones (**Α** appears twice).
2. **Α**, **Η**, **Ν** all have identical Latin shapes — though only
   **Α** and **Ν** also have the same sound.
3. **Θ** has no Latin equivalent in shape. (Latin acquired *Y* and *Z*
   from Greek later, but never took theta.)
4. You would read *a-h-na* or *ath-na*. You would be wrong because Greek
   **Η** is a long *e* — the shape was borrowed into Latin for a sound
   Greek had stopped using. The word is *a-thē-na*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Sound them out"}
Write each word in Latin letters, then say it aloud. Do not try to work out
what it means.

**φίλος** · **λόγος** · **ψυχή** · **ἄνθρωπος** · **δῶρον** · **θεός** ·
**ῥήτωρ** · **ξένος**
:::

::: answers
*philos* · *logos* · *psūchē* · *anthrōpos* · *dōron* · *theos* ·
*rhētōr* · *xenos*

Watch for: **φ** is *ph* (a puffed *p*, not an *f*); **χ** in **ψυχή** is
*ch* as a puffed *k*; **ρ** at the start of a word takes the rough
breathing and so is *rh*; **ξ** is a single letter spelling two sounds.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Copy the letters"}
Copy out the twenty-four letters in order, capital and small, with the name
of each beside it. Do this by hand. Do it twice.

Then cover the names and write them from memory.
:::

::: answers
Check against the table above. The most commonly mis-ordered pair is
**ξ** and **ο** — **ξῖ** comes before **ὂ μικρόν**, not after, because
Greek has no letter between them where Latin has *n*–*o*–*p*.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Carved at Delphi; quoted by Plato, *Charmides* 164e"}
This is the first real Greek you will read. You will not understand it yet.
You can still read it, which is a different skill and worth having.

It was carved on the temple of Apollo at Delphi, where Greeks went to ask
the god questions about their future. Of all the things that could have
been written at the entrance to a place where people come for answers, this
is what was written.

As a stonecutter would have carved it:

**ΓΝΩΘΙΣΑΥΤΟΝ**

With the word division a modern editor supplies:

**ΓΝΩΘΙ ΣΑΥΤΟΝ**

And as it appears in the printed editions you will use:

**γνῶθι σαυτόν**

Sound it out, letter by letter: *gnō-thi sau-ton*.

It means **know yourself**.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. You just read a sentence in a language you do not speak. What exactly
   did you do? Is that *reading*?
2. The stonecutter left no spaces between the words. Ancient readers
   managed. What does that tell you about how they read — silently, or
   aloud?
3. Pilgrims came to Delphi to be told about the future. The inscription
   told them to look at themselves instead. Was that an answer or a
   refusal?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
At the end of the lesson Theodoros asked each pupil to write one Greek word
before leaving.

Marcus wrote **ΒΙΡΓΙΛΙΟΣ**.

Julia looked at it. "That is Virgil," she said. "In Greek letters."

"It is a word," said Marcus, "and it is in Greek letters. Those were the
instructions."

Theodoros looked at the tablet for a long moment. The class had already
learned to find this more alarming than shouting.

"Excellent," he said at last. "You have discovered our first rule. A word
does not become Greek merely because you disguise it."

Felix asked immediately whether the same principle applied to homework.

"It does," said Theodoros. "Five words. Tomorrow."
:::

::: latinbridge
Marcus's joke has a serious point behind it, and the Romans made it
constantly. **ΒΙΡΓΙΛΙΟΣ** really is how a Greek writer would have spelled
*Vergilius* — Greek had no *v*, so it used **β**, and it added **-ος**
because a Greek noun needs a Greek ending to be declined at all.

That last part is the real lesson. A word enters a language properly only
when the language can bend it. Marcus gave Virgil a Greek ending without
meaning to, and in doing so did something more nearly right than he
intended.
:::
