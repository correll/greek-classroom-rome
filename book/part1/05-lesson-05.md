# The Verb Appears

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Each of you will say one thing about yourself," said Theodoros. "In Greek.
Today."

There was the particular silence of a room in which eight people have
simultaneously decided to be somebody else's problem.

"I shall go first," said Aelia.

This surprised nobody. Aelia's mother kept a Greek cook, and Aelia had been
telling the class for a fortnight that she had been hearing Greek spoken at
home since she was four, which was true, and that she therefore had an
advantage, which was about to be tested.

She stood up and said, with considerable confidence:

> **ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός.**

Theodoros's face did something complicated.

"What did you intend to say?"

"That I know the way. That I can show them the way — the others, when they
get lost in it." She hesitated. "It is what my mother says."

"What you have said," said Theodoros, "is *I am the road.*"

Felix made a small sound.

"Not *I know the road*. Not *I show the road*. **I am the road.** You have
told this room that you are a paved surface running from here to Ostia."

Felix had put his head on his knees.

"It is a perfectly good sentence," Theodoros went on. "Every word is
correct. The endings agree. It is, as Greek, faultless. It simply does not
mean what you wanted, and no amount of being right about the grammar will
rescue you from that."

Aelia sat down with great dignity.

Felix said **ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός** at least once a week for the rest of the
term, usually when asked where something was.
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The ending tells you who

Until now you have borrowed verbs whole: **βλέπει**, **γράφει**, **ἔχει**,
**λέγει** — all of them *he, she, or it does this*. Today you get the rest
of them, and the rule behind them.

A Greek verb changes its ending according to **who is doing it**. Here is
**λέγω**, *I say*, in all six:

| | ending | form | meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1st singular | **-ω** | **λέγω** | I say |
| 2nd singular | **-εις** | **λέγεις** | you say |
| 3rd singular | **-ει** | **λέγει** | he, she, or it says |
| 1st plural | **-ομεν** | **λέγομεν** | we say |
| 2nd plural | **-ετε** | **λέγετε** | you (more than one) say |
| 3rd plural | **-ουσι(ν)** | **λέγουσι(ν)** | they say |

Every verb you have met so far behaves this way: **γράφω, γράφεις,
γράφει…**; **ἔχω, ἔχεις, ἔχει…**; **βλέπω, βλέπεις, βλέπει…**

Three things follow, and the third is the one that matters.

**One. The dictionary form is *I*, not *he*.** Greek verbs are listed under
the first person singular — **λέγω**, not **λέγει**. English dictionaries
use *to say*; Greek uses *I say*. This is why your vocabulary list has
changed shape today.

**Two. Greek does not need a separate word for the subject.** **λέγομεν**
is a complete sentence: *we say*. There is no **we** standing in front of
it, because the **-ομεν** already is the *we*. English cannot do this and
must supply a pronoun every time.

**Three. So when a Greek writer *does* supply the pronoun, it means
something.** **λέγω** is *I say*. **ἐγὼ λέγω** is *I* say — as opposed to
you, or to him, or to whoever said otherwise. The pronoun is not needed for
the sense, so it has been added for emphasis, and you should ask why.

Aelia's **ἐγώ** was doing exactly this. She did not say *I am the road*.
She said ***I*** *am the road.*

::: latinbridge
Latin works the same way and for the same reason: *dico, dicis, dicit,
dicimus, dicitis, dicunt* — one word, subject included, and *ego dico* adds
emphasis rather than information.

If you have ever been told off for writing *ego* where it was not needed,
you already understand the third point above. Greek will be stricter about
it than your Latin master was, because Greek authors use the extra pronoun
deliberately and you will be asked why.
:::

## The verb that refuses to behave

**εἰμί** — *I am* — is the commonest verb in Greek and the least regular.
Learn it as a set of six noises; there is no pattern to deduce.

| | form | meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1st singular | **εἰμί** | I am |
| 2nd singular | **εἶ** | you are |
| 3rd singular | **ἐστί(ν)** | he, she, or it is |
| 1st plural | **ἐσμέν** | we are |
| 2nd plural | **ἐστέ** | you (more than one) are |
| 3rd plural | **εἰσί(ν)** | they are |

**ἐστί** is the **ἐστιν** you met in Pilate's question last lesson. You
have been reading this verb for a week without being able to name it.

::: note
**Why some forms end in (ν).** **ἐστί(ν)** and **εἰσί(ν)** add a **-ν**
when the next word begins with a vowel, and usually at the end of a
sentence. It exists to stop two vowels colliding — the same instinct that
makes English choose *an apple* over *a apple*. Greek does this constantly
and you will stop noticing it within a month.
:::

## What εἰμί does not do

**εἰμί** does not take an object. It links two things and says they are the
same thing, so what follows it is **nominative**, not accusative:

> **ὁ φίλος ἐστὶ κριτής.** — The friend is a judge.

Look hard at that sentence, because it contains the trick from Lesson 3.
**ὁ φίλος** has the article; **κριτής** has none. That is how you know which
is the subject and which is being said about it. *The friend is a judge* —
not *the judge is a friend*.

Greek marks the difference with one small word, and it will do this to you
for the rest of your life.
:::

::: vocab
- **λέγω** — I say, speak, tell
- **γράφω** — I write
- **ἔχω** — I have, hold
- **βλέπω** — I see, look at
- **γιγνώσκω** — I know, come to know, recognise
- **εἰμί** — I am
- **ἐγώ** — I
- **ἡ ζωή** — life
- **λαμβάνω** — I take, receive
- **φεύγω** — I flee, run away
- **μένω** — I stay, remain; I wait
- **εὑρίσκω** — I find
- **καθεύδω** — I sleep

::: note
**γιγνώσκω** is *come to know*, not *know a fact*. It is the knowing you do
by meeting something — recognising a face, working out a person, finding out
how a thing behaves. English has lost the distinction and keeps one word for
both; Greek keeps two, and will expect you to choose.

The **γνω-** in it is the same root as in **γνῶθι σαυτόν**, *know yourself*,
the maxim carved at Delphi. That is a **command**, and commands have endings
of their own which you do not meet until Lesson 26. Theodoros points this
out every year and every year somebody tries to issue one anyway.

The five verbs from **λαμβάνω** down all take the endings in the table
exactly as **λέγω** does, and are here so that you have something to
conjugate besides saying and seeing. **καθεύδω** is the cat's verb. You
will meet it in that connection often.
:::

4. 2nd plural — *you (all) have*. **-ετε**.

9. 1st plural — *we stay*. **-ομεν**.

    end.

is worth slowing down on, because the plural endings are where the first
term's marks are lost.

5. **ὁ διδάσκαλός ἐστι φίλος.**
6. **ὁ αἴλουρος καθεύδει, ἀλλὰ ὁ ἵππος φεύγει.**
7. **λαμβάνομεν τὴν δέλτον καὶ γράφομεν.**
8. **εὑρίσκεις τὴν ὁδόν;**
9. **μένω, ἀλλὰ ὁ δοῦλος φεύγει.**
10. **ὁ ξένος ἐστὶ κριτής.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*

::: answers
1. *I know the road.* — Note that this is what Aelia meant to say.
2. *The witness tells the truth.*
3. *We see the horse and the house.* One verb, two objects, joined by
   **καί**.
4. *You have the book, but I write the word.* Two clauses, two different
   subjects, neither of them stated as a separate word.
5. *The teacher is a friend.* — **ὁ διδάσκαλος** has the article and is the
   subject; **φίλος** has none and is what is said about him. A student who
   offered *the friend is a teacher* has read English word order and ignored
   the article.
6. *The cat is asleep, but the horse runs away.* — Accept *sleeps*,
   *flees*.
7. *We take the tablet and write.* — One subject, carried in two endings.
8. *Are you finding the road?* — or *Do you find the road?* The **;** is
   the Greek question mark (Lesson 2). A pupil who read it as a semicolon
   has stopped where they should have asked.
9. *I stay, but the slave runs away.* — The first subject is the ending
   alone; the second is a noun. Both are nominative.
10. *The stranger is a judge.* — The article test again, the other way
    round from 5. A pupil who wrote *the judge is a stranger* has read the
    English order into the Greek.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Aelia's sentence"}
Aelia said **ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός** and meant *I know the way*.

1. Was her sentence grammatically wrong? Say precisely what was and was not
   correct about it.
2. Write what she should have said.
3. **ἐγώ** was not necessary. What did adding it do?
4. Felix says her sentence whenever he is asked where something is. Write
   what *he* should say for *I know the road*, and for *I am finding the
   road*.
5. Change her sentence so that it says *you (one person) are the road*.
   What changes, and what does not?
6. Put it in the third person: *the friend is the road*. What is awkward
   about the result?
7. Theodoros said her sentence was faultless Greek that did not mean what
   she wanted. Find one more sentence in this lesson's exercises that is
   correct Greek and could easily be the wrong claim.
8. Aelia argues that since **ἐγώ** was unnecessary, leaving it out would
   have made the sentence *less* wrong. Would it?
9. Write *I do not know the road*, and then *we do not know the road*.
10. Write *Aelia knows the road* and then *Aelia is the road*. Besides the
    verb, what changes?
:::

::: answers
1. Nothing in it is wrong. **ἐγώ** is nominative, **εἰμί** is first person
   singular and agrees with it, **ἡ ὁδός** is nominative because **εἰμί**
   links rather than acts upon. It is faultless Greek. It is simply a
   different claim from the one she wanted to make. This distinction —
   between *correct* and *what you meant* — is the whole of the course's
   argument about precision, and it is worth naming when it happens.
2. **γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.** *I know the road.* Note the accusative: here the
   road is being acted upon, not identified with the speaker.
3. It threw emphasis onto the speaker: ***I*** *am the road*, as against
   anybody else. Which is what made it funny, and, as you are about to see,
   what makes the same four words enormous in another mouth.
4. **γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν** and **εὑρίσκω τὴν ὁδόν.** Accusative in both: the
   road is being known or found, not been.
5. **εἶ ἡ ὁδός.** The verb changes to the second person, and **ἐγώ** has to
   go — the word for *you* arrives in Lesson 11, and until then the ending
   carries it. **ἡ ὁδός** does not change: still nominative, because
   **εἰμί** still links.
6. **ὁ φίλος ἐστὶν ἡ ὁδός.** Both nouns now carry the article, so the knife
   from Lesson 3 cannot cut: a reader takes the first as the subject, but
   nothing in the endings forces it. Aelia's sentence escaped this because
   **ἐγώ** cannot be anything but a subject.
7. **ὁ διδάσκαλός ἐστι φίλος** against **ὁ φίλος ἐστὶ διδάσκαλος**; or
   **ὁ ξένος ἐστὶ κριτής** against **ὁ κριτής ἐστι ξένος**. Any pair in
   which moving the article moves the claim.
8. No. **εἰμι ἡ ὁδός** is still *I am the road*. Dropping **ἐγώ** removes
   the emphasis and leaves the claim exactly as it was. The pronoun was
   not where the mistake lived.
9. **οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.** **οὐ γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ὁδόν.** — **οὐ** in
   its plain shape, since **γ** is a consonant.
10. **ἡ Αἰλία γιγνώσκει τὴν ὁδόν.** **ἡ Αἰλία ἐστὶν ἡ ὁδός.** Besides the
    verb, the *case of the road* changes: accusative after **γιγνώσκει**,
    nominative after **ἐστίν**. A pupil who kept **τὴν ὁδόν** in the second
    sentence has not yet believed that **εἰμί** takes no object.
:::

   said about him. A pupil who writes **ὁ φίλος ἐστὶ διδάσκαλος** has said
   something different and should be asked which of the two the English
   meant.

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 14:6"}
Aelia's sentence was not original.

About three hundred years before that afternoon, somebody had written down
very nearly the same words, and meant them.

**ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδὸς καὶ ἡ ἀλήθεια καὶ ἡ ζωή.**

- **ἐγώ** — I
- **εἰμι** — am
- **ἡ ὁδός** — the way, the road *(Lesson 2)*
- **καί** — and *(Lesson 2)*
- **ἡ ἀλήθεια** — the truth *(Lesson 4)*
- **ἡ ζωή** — the life

*I am the way, and the truth, and the life.*

You can account for every word in that sentence. There is nothing in it you
have not been taught, and it is the first authentic sentence in this book
that you can read entire.

Three things are worth noticing, and you now have the equipment for all
three.

**The pronoun is there and did not need to be.** **εἰμι** already means
*I am*. **ἐγώ** has been added, exactly as Aelia added it, and for exactly
the same reason: to put the weight on the speaker. *I* am these things.

**All three nouns carry the article.** Not *a way*, not *a kind of truth* —
**ἡ ὁδός**, **ἡ ἀλήθεια**, **ἡ ζωή**, each one definite, each one the only
one. Drop the three articles and the sentence becomes modest. They are the
most important three letters in it.

**It answers the question from last lesson.** Pilate asked
**τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια;** — *what is truth?* This sentence, in the same book,
spoken by the man standing in front of him, is an answer given before the
question was asked. Whether it is a good answer is not a question Greek can
settle for you. But you can now see that it *is* one, which is more than a
translation would have told you.

::: note
Koine again, like Pilate's question, and from the same gospel. You will meet
the differences between this Greek and Plato's in Part IV, where they are
the point rather than a distraction.
:::
:::

Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything. There are eight

1. Who speaks first, and what does he say he sees and does not see?
2. Julia's sentence has **ἐγώ** and Livia's does not. What is Julia
   emphasising, and against whom?
3. Aelia says **γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν**. What case is **τὴν ὁδόν**, and why is
   it that case here when the road was nominative in her sentence last
   week?
4. Felix says Aelia's old sentence. Theodoros answers him. Translate the
   answer, and say which form of **εἰμί** it uses.
5. Sabina says **ἐγώ εἰμι κριτής**, with no article on **κριτής**. What
   difference would **ὁ κριτής** have made?
6. Find every first-person singular verb in the tablet and translate each.
   How many are there?
7. Find the one second-person verb apart from **εἶ**. Who is it addressed
   to, and what shows that?
8. Which pupil's sentence is the shortest? Is it a complete sentence, and
   what tells you?
9. The cat says nothing. What does the tablet say the cat does instead, and
   what person and number is that verb?
10. Theodoros wrote **ἐγώ** into five mouths and left it out of three. Name
    the five, and suggest, for one of them, why.

::: answers
1. Marcus. He sees the road and does not see the book — **τὸ βιβλίον οὐ
   βλέπω**, with the book thrown forward.
2. Herself as the one who writes — against Marcus, who does not, as her
   next sentence says.
3. Accusative: **τήν** and **-όν**. Because **γιγνώσκω** acts upon the
   road, while **εἰμί** links and takes a nominative. The same noun, two
   jobs, two endings.
4. *Felix, you are not the road.* **εἶ**, second person singular, the
   irregular one; and **οὐκ**, because **εἶ** begins with a smooth
   breathing.
5. *I am* the *judge* rather than *I am a judge*: the article would have
   made her the one judge there is, which in that room she was. Theodoros
   left it off. Sabina noticed.
6. Ten: **εἰμι** three times (Marcus, Felix, Sabina) — *I am*; **βλέπω**
   three times (Marcus twice, Livia) — *I see*; **γράφω** — *I write*;
   **γιγνώσκω** twice (Aelia, Quintus) — *I know*; **ἔχω** — *I have*. A
   pupil who found eight has probably missed Marcus's second **βλέπω** or
   Felix's **εἰμι**.
7. **βλέπεις** — *you see*, or here *you are not looking*. Addressed to
   Marcus: the vocative **ὦ Μᾶρκε** in front of it says so.
8. Livia's: **βλέπω**, one word. It is complete, because the **-ω** is
   the subject. She says only that she is watching, which is accurate.
9. **καθεύδει** — *it sleeps*. Third person singular, like every verb
   that has ever been said about the cat.
10. Marcus, Julia, Felix, Sabina, Quintus. Reasons, any one: Marcus is
    introducing himself; Julia is setting herself against Marcus; Felix is
    quoting Aelia word for word; Sabina is claiming the office against
    the room; Quintus is insisting that it is *he* who knows the soul.
    Accept any answer that treats **ἐγώ** as emphasis and says who it is
    emphasised against.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. The Greek verb already says who is acting. English needs a separate word.
   What does a language gain, and what does it lose, by folding the subject
   into the verb?
2. Aelia and the speaker in John 14:6 said almost the same sentence. One was
   funny and one has been argued about for two thousand years. What is the
   difference, given that the grammar is identical?
3. **ἡ ὁδός** can mean a road you walk on and a way of living. English keeps
   *road* and *way* mostly apart. Is a language better for having one word
   where another has two?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Weeks later, lost in the Subura with Felix and a basket of fish, Aelia
stopped at a junction and considered the three streets available to her.

"**ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός**," said Felix.

"Yes," said Aelia. "And you are the fish."

She was wrong about the case ending, and she got them home.
:::
