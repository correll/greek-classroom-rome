# The Verb Appears

::: {.story time="5 min"}
The pupils introduce themselves in Greek. Aelia, who claims to have heard Greek spoken at home, volunteers first and produces a sentence that means something other than she intended. Felix repeats it for the rest of the term.

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
**Grammar for this lesson.** Present indicative active of **-ω** verbs, all six persons; the verb **εἰμί**; personal endings as information.

> **To be written.** Present one main grammatical idea with a paradigm
> table, two or three worked examples, and a **From Latin** box wherever
> the Latin parallel either helps or misleads.
:::

**Text for this lesson.** **γνῶθι σαυτόν** revisited — now that the pupils have verbs, Theodoros points out that the Delphic maxim is a command, not a statement, and that they will not be able to form one themselves until Lesson 26.

::: {.question time="5 min"}
The Greek verb already says who is acting. English needs a separate word. What does a language gain, and what does it lose, by folding the subject into the verb?
