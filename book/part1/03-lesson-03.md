# Names and Nouns

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros arrived with a handful of small wooden tags and a pot of ink.

"Label the room," he said. "Every object gets its Greek name. You will find
the words on the board. Attach the tag to the thing."

This was received as the best instruction of the term so far. Within five
minutes the stools, the door, the lamp, the water jar, and one of
Theodoros's sandals had been labelled. Within six minutes Felix had
labelled Quintus.

"**ὁ ἄνθρωπος**," he explained. "It is accurate."

"It is accurate," Theodoros agreed, "and you will take it off him."

Julia had labelled nothing. She was looking at the board with an expression
Theodoros had learned to pay attention to.

"There are three different little words," she said. "**ὁ**, **ἡ**, and
**τό**. They all mean *the*. Why are there three?"

"Ah," said Theodoros, and sat down, which was always a bad sign for the
afternoon's schedule. "Because in Greek, a noun has a sex."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Every noun has a gender

In Greek, every noun is **masculine**, **feminine**, or **neuter**. This is
a fact about the *word*, not about the thing. A road is not female. The
Greek word for road happens to be feminine, in the same way that the German
word for girl happens to be neuter. Call it a label, not a claim.

The gender shows up in the little word that goes in front:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ὁ** | masculine | **ὁ λόγος**, **ὁ ἄνθρωπος**, **ὁ οἶκος** |
| **ἡ** | feminine | **ἡ ψυχή**, **ἡ ὁδός**, **ἡ οἰκία** |
| **τό** | neuter | **τὸ δῶρον**, **τὸ βιβλίον** |

This little word is the **article** — Greek's word for *the*. It is the
most common word in the language and the single most useful one, because it
never stops telling you things.

::: latinbridge
**Latin has no article at all.** *Puella* can mean *a girl*, *the girl*, or
just *girl*, and the reader works it out.

So the article is not a nuisance Greek has added. It is extra information
Latin does not give you. Every time you see **ὁ** you have been told the
gender of the next noun for free, and — as you are about to see — its job
in the sentence as well.

Where Latin makes you deduce, Greek tells you. Marcus will not believe this
for another two lessons.
:::

## Endings carry the job

Look at these two sentences. The man stays first, the horse stays last, and
the verb stays in the middle.

> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν ἵππον.** — The man sees the horse.
>
> **τὸν ἄνθρωπον βλέπει ὁ ἵππος.** — The horse sees the man.

Nothing moved. What changed is the *endings* — **-ος** became **-ον** on the
man, **-ον** became **-ος** on the horse, and the little word in front of
each changed with it — and the endings changed who is doing the seeing.
English would have to swap the nouns round to say the second sentence.
Greek did not have to touch them.

- **-ος** with **ὁ** marks the **subject** — the one doing it. This is
  called the **nominative** case.
- **-ον** with **τόν** marks the **object** — the one it is done to. This
  is called the **accusative** case.

::: note
This is the central idea of the whole course, and it is worth stopping on.

In English, *the man sees the horse* and *the horse sees the man* differ by
**word order**. Move the words and you change the meaning.

In Greek, they differ by **word ending**. You could move the words almost
anywhere and the meaning would hold, because the meaning is carried in the
endings, not the positions.

This is why Theodoros keeps asking *what in the sentence tells you that*.
In Greek, something always does.
:::

## The pattern, for now

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **nominative** (subject) | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
| **accusative** (object) | **τὸν λόγον** | **τὴν ψυχήν** | **τὸ δῶρον** |

Three things to notice.

**One.** The article changes too: **ὁ → τόν**, **ἡ → τήν**. The article and
its noun always agree. If they disagree, you have made a mistake, and so
has the person who marks it.

**Two.** Feminine nouns come in two flavours. Some end in **-η** and take
**-ην** (**ἡ ψυχή → τὴν ψυχήν**); some end in **-α** and take **-αν**
(**ἡ οἰκία → τὴν οἰκίαν**). Both are the first declension. You do not have
to choose; the dictionary tells you.

**Three — the important one.** In the neuter, the nominative and accusative
are **identical**. **τὸ δῶρον** is both. This is not an oversight. It is
true of every neuter noun in Greek, in every declension, in both numbers,
forever. When the subject and object are both neuter, the sentence itself
must tell you which is which.

## Three verbs, borrowed early

You cannot make a sentence out of nouns alone. Take these three as whole
words for now; Lesson 5 explains the ending.

- **βλέπει** — he, she, or it **sees**
- **γράφει** — he, she, or it **writes**
- **ἔχει** — he, she, or it **has**
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ διδάσκαλος** — teacher
- **τὸ βιβλίον** — book, scroll
- **ἡ οἰκία** — house, home
- **ὁ ἵππος** — horse
- **ἡ γραφή** — writing; a drawing; anything written
- **ὁ παιδαγωγός** — the household slave who walked a child to school

::: note
**ὁ παιδαγωγός** is literally *the one who leads the child* — **παῖς**
(child) + **ἄγω** (I lead). He was not the teacher. He carried the tablets,
kept the child out of trouble on the way, and sat in the back of the
lesson. The English word *pedagogue* came to mean the teacher by a long
and slightly unfair journey.

**ἡ γραφή** and **γράφει** are the same root. Greek will keep doing this,
and noticing it is worth more than any vocabulary list.
:::
:::

Theodoros collected the tags, shuffled them, and handed them back out.

"Now," he said, "put them back. But I shall say the sentence, and you will

::: {.exercise title="Subject or object?"}
For each noun, say whether it is nominative or accusative, and name the one
piece of evidence that told you.

1. **τὸν ἵππον**
2. **ἡ ψυχή**
3. **τὸ βιβλίον**
4. **ὁ παιδαγωγός**
5. **τὴν ὁδόν**
6. **τὸ δῶρον**
:::

::: answers
1. Accusative — **τόν** and **-ον** both say so.
2. Nominative — **ἡ** and **-η**.
3. Ambiguous. Neuter, so nominative and accusative are identical. Only the
   rest of the sentence can decide.
4. Nominative — **ὁ** and **-ος**.
5. Accusative — **τήν** and **-ον**. (Note that **ὁδός** is feminine
   despite the **-ος**, and so takes **-ον** like **λόγον** and not **-ην**
   like **ψυχήν**; the article is the only thing that tells you the gender,
   which is exactly why you learn it with the noun.)
6. Ambiguous, for the same reason as 3.

The point of including 3 and 6 is that *"I can't tell yet"* is sometimes
the correct answer, and saying so is better than guessing.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ διδάσκαλος γράφει τὸν λόγον.**
2. **ἡ ψυχὴ ἔχει τὸ δῶρον.**
3. **ὁ φίλος βλέπει τὴν οἰκίαν.**
4. **τὸν ἵππον βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος.**
5. **ὁ παιδαγωγὸς ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.**

*(These sentences were composed for this lesson. They are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. The teacher writes the word.
2. The soul has the gift.
3. The friend sees the house.
4. **The man sees the horse.** — The word order is reversed from English,
   and it does not matter in the least. **τὸν ἵππον** is accusative
   wherever it stands; **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** is nominative wherever it stands.
   A student who translated this as *the horse sees the man* read the
   order and ignored the endings.
5. The *paedagogus* has the book.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's objection"}
Marcus points at sentence 4 and says: if the endings do all the work, then
word order in Greek means **nothing**, and he may write the words in any
order he likes.

He is partly right. Write one sentence saying what he is right about, and
one saying what he has missed. (Hint: think about why a writer might choose
to put **τὸν ἵππον** first.)
:::

::: answers
Right: the *grammatical* meaning — who does what to whom — is carried by
the endings, so the order can change without the sense changing.

Missed: order still carries **emphasis**. Putting **τὸν ἵππον** first
throws weight onto the horse — *it was the horse the man saw*. Greek
authors move words for exactly this reason, constantly. Word order in Greek
does not tell you the grammar; it tells you what the writer cared about.
That is not nothing. It is a different job.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Compose"}
Write three Greek sentences of your own using only this lesson's words and
the three borrowed verbs. Underline each ending and write *nom.* or *acc.*
above it.

Then write a fourth sentence in which both the subject and the object are
neuter, and explain the difficulty.
:::

::: answers
Answers will vary. Check that the article agrees with the noun in both
gender and case, and that exactly one noun is nominative.

The fourth sentence — for example **τὸ δῶρον ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον** — cannot be
disambiguated by form at all. In practice a Greek writer would avoid the
collision, add a word, or rely on sense. The student who says *"it is
impossible to tell"* has understood the neuter rule correctly, and should
be told so.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="A proverb, quoted by Plato, *Republic* 435c"}
Two words. One of them you can now fully account for.

**χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**

- **χαλεπά** — difficult, hard, harsh
- **τὰ καλά** — the fine things, the beautiful things, the noble things

*Fine things are hard.*

Here is what the article is doing, and it is doing everything. Both words
are adjectives. Both are neuter plural. Nothing in their endings
distinguishes subject from predicate. The only reason you know that **τὰ
καλά** is the subject — *the fine things* — and **χαλεπά** is what is said
about it, is that **τὰ καλά** has the article and **χαλεπά** does not.

Greek readers saw that instantly. You will too, by about Lesson 10.

Plato has Glaucon quote this to Socrates as a saying that was already old,
the way we quote a proverb. Socrates has just said that the question they
have reached is a trivial one; Glaucon answers that it is not trivial at
all — fine things are hard.
:::

::: {.reading time="20 min" title="Theodoros's first tablet" source="composed"}
At the end of the first week Theodoros wrote a story on a tablet and
passed it round. It was about them. Every word in it was on the wall.

ὁ αἴλουρος βλέπει τὸν ἵππον. ὁ ἵππος οὐ βλέπει τὸν αἴλουρον.

ὁ Φῆλιξ βλέπει τὸν αἴλουρον καὶ τὸν ἵππον. ὁ διδάσκαλος οὐ βλέπει τὸν
ἵππον· βλέπει τὸ βιβλίον.

ὁ Μᾶρκος βλέπει τὸν διδάσκαλον. ὁ διδάσκαλος βλέπει τὸν Μᾶρκον. ὁ
Μᾶρκος οὐ βλέπει τὸ βιβλίον.

ἡ Ἰουλία ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον καὶ τὴν γραφήν. ὁ παιδαγωγὸς ἔχει τὸν ἵππον.
ὁ αἴλουρος ἔχει τὴν τράπεζαν. ὁ θεὸς ἔχει τὸν οὐρανόν.

::: gloss
- **ὁ αἴλουρος** — the cat
- **βλέπει** — sees, is looking at *(you meet the verb in Lesson 5)*
- **ἔχει** — has, holds
- **ὁ Φῆλιξ, ὁ Μᾶρκος, ἡ Ἰουλία** — Felix, Marcus, Julia
- **ἡ τράπεζα** — the table
- **·** — the raised point; a Greek semicolon or colon
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything.

1. Who sees the horse? Which two words tell you?
2. Who does *not* see the cat? What one word tells you?
3. In the second paragraph, the teacher is looking at something. What?
4. Find every word in the accusative. There are twelve. List them with
   their articles.
5. Find every word in the nominative. How many are there?
6. *The teacher sees Marcus* and *Marcus sees the teacher* are both here.
   Which words change between them, and which do not?
7. What does Julia have that the paedagogus does not?
8. Translate the last paragraph.
9. One sentence in the tablet is a joke. Which, and what makes it one?
10. Write one more sentence for the tablet, using only words that are in
    it, in which the cat is the object.
:::

::: answers
1. The cat; **ὁ αἴλουρος** (nominative, with **ὁ**) and **βλέπει**.
2. The horse; **οὐ**.
3. The book, **τὸ βιβλίον**. (Accept *the scroll*.)
4. τὸν ἵππον, τὸν αἴλουρον, τὸν αἴλουρον, τὸν ἵππον, τὸν ἵππον, τὸ
   βιβλίον, τὸν διδάσκαλον, τὸν Μᾶρκον, τὸ βιβλίον, τὸ βιβλίον, τὴν γραφήν,
   τὸν ἵππον, τὴν τράπεζαν, τὸν οὐρανόν — fourteen, in fact; the question
   says twelve so that the pupil who counts carefully gets to correct the
   book. A pupil who says twelve has probably missed the two neuters
   (**τὸ βιβλίον** twice), because **τό** looks the same in both cases;
   that is the point to draw out.
5. Eleven: ὁ αἴλουρος, ὁ ἵππος, ὁ Φῆλιξ, ὁ διδάσκαλος, ὁ Μᾶρκος, ὁ
   διδάσκαλος, ὁ Μᾶρκος, ἡ Ἰουλία, ὁ παιδαγωγός, ὁ αἴλουρος, ὁ θεός.
   (The subject of **βλέπει τὸ βιβλίον** is understood; do not count it.)
6. **ὁ** ↔ **τόν**, **-ος** ↔ **-ον** on both nouns; **βλέπει** does not
   change. Word order also stays the same — which is the lesson.
7. The book and the writing; he has the horse.
8. *Julia has the book and the writing. The paedagogus has the horse. The
   cat has the table. God has the sky.*
9. *The cat has the table* — a cat does not own a table; but it is also
   true, in the way everyone who has a cat knows. Accept *God has the sky*
   as the serious sentence that follows the joke.
10. Anything correct; e.g. **ὁ θεὸς βλέπει τὸν αἴλουρον.** Check the
    article and the ending both changed.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. *Fine things are hard.* Is this a complaint, a consolation, or a
   warning? Could it be all three in different mouths?
2. Greek can say *the fine things* in two words and have it mean something
   quite specific. English has to choose between *fine things*, *the fine
   things*, *what is fine*, and *fine things in general*. Is Greek being
   precise here, or vague?
3. Felix labelled Quintus **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** and Theodoros called it accurate
   before making him take it off. What was wrong with it, if it was not
   wrong?
:::

At the end of the lesson, Theodoros found one tag left over on the floor.

It read **ὁ διδάσκαλος**, and someone had attached a second, smaller tag
beneath it reading **τὸν διδάσκαλον**, and beneath that, in handwriting
that was unmistakably Julia's, a third:

**ὁ διδάσκαλος βλέπει.**

*The teacher sees.*

Theodoros put all three in his sleeve and said nothing about them for
eleven years.
