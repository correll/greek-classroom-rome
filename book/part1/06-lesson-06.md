# The First Real Sentence

::: story
Theodoros was at the board before anyone sat down, and he did not greet
anyone.

He wrote seventeen words, put the charcoal down, and sat.

> **Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

"Read it."

"We cannot read that," said Marcus, before he had finished looking at it.

"You have not looked at it."

"I have looked at it enough. There are words there we have never seen. That
one, and that one, and whatever that is." He pointed at three of them,
correctly, and missed a fourth. "You have given us a sentence we do not
have the words for.
That is not a test, it is a trick."

"Is it the whole sentence you cannot read," said Theodoros, "or four words
of it?"

Marcus opened his mouth and shut it again.

Julia had already started writing. Sabina was reading it aloud under her
breath, which was the correct instinct. Quintus was arguing with himself.
Livia had not moved at all; she was looking at the board the way she looked
at things she was about to be right about.

"**ὁ λόγος**," she said eventually. "Three times. And it has **ὁ** every
time."

"Go on."

"And that one" — **θεός** — "has no **ὁ**. Not in the last part. So in the
last part **ὁ λόγος** is the one we are talking about, and **θεός** is what
is being said about it. Like **ὁ διδάσκαλός ἐστι φίλος**. Like the fine
things being hard."

The room went quiet in a way it had not done before.

"I still do not know what **ἦν** means," Livia added.

"No," said Theodoros. "But you have just construed a sentence of which you
did not know four words, and you got the hardest thing in it right. **ἦν**
I shall simply tell you. The rest you have done yourselves."

Marcus looked at the board for a long moment.

"Who wrote it?" he said.
:::

::: newgreek
## Reading a sentence you cannot yet read

This lesson teaches no new machinery. It teaches a method, and the method is
worth more than any single ending.

**Read to the end before you translate any of it.** Greek routinely puts
words in an order English cannot copy, and the word that settles everything
is often the last one. A student who translates word by word from the left
is guessing with increasing confidence and decreasing accuracy.

**Find the article.** It is the most generous word in the language. Every
**ὁ**, **ἡ**, **τό** tells you gender, case, and — when something nearby
lacks one — which of two nouns is the subject.

**Mark what you are certain of and leave the rest alone.** You are allowed
to write *"I do not know this word"* and carry on. Livia read seventeen words
without knowing four of them, because she did not need those four to answer
the question she had been asked.

**Ask the question.** *What in the sentence tells you that?* If the answer
is "the order of the words" or "it sounded right", you have not finished.

::: note
This is the single most transferable habit in the course, and it has very
little to do with Greek. You are being taught to work out what a difficult
piece of text *does* say, from evidence on the page, rather than what it
probably says, from the general shape of it.

Keep this in mind when something fluent and confident is telling you
something and you cannot immediately say how it knows.
:::

## Saying no

One small word you met in Lesson 2, now with its rule in full, and one
small complication.

**οὐ** — *not*. It goes in front of the word it denies, and usually in front
of the verb.

> **οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.** — I do not know the road.

The complication is purely about sound. Greek dislikes **οὐ** sitting
against a vowel, so it grows a consonant:

| before | form | example |
|:--|:--|:--|
| a consonant | **οὐ** | **οὐ γράφω** — I am not writing |
| a smooth breathing | **οὐκ** | **οὐκ ἔχω** — I do not have |
| a rough breathing | **οὐχ** | **οὐχ ἡ ὁδός** — not the road |

This is the same instinct that gives English *a book* but *an apple*. It
carries no meaning whatever. Get it right anyway: it is one of the things a
reader notices.

::: latinbridge
Latin negates with *non*, which never changes its shape. Greek's three forms
look like extra work and are not: the form is chosen entirely by the sound
of the next word, so you never have to think about meaning to pick one.

The useful difference is elsewhere. Latin *non* and Greek **οὐ** both deny a
fact. Greek has a *second* negative, **μή**, for things wished, feared,
forbidden, or supposed — and the choice between them tells a reader what
kind of statement they are in. You meet **μή** in Part V.
:::

## Everything in Part I, in one line

You now have all of this, and it took six lessons:

- the alphabet, and the breathings and accents that go with it
- gender, and the article that announces it
- the nominative, for the one doing
- the accusative, for the one it is done to
- word order, which carries emphasis rather than grammar
- the vocative, for speaking to someone
- **καί** and **ἀλλά** to join, **οὐ** to deny
- six person-endings on the verb, and the six noises of **εἰμί**
- and the article used as a knife: *with* it, the subject; *without* it,
  what is said about the subject

That last one is why Livia could read a sentence containing four unknown
words. It is the most powerful single fact in Greek, and you have had it
since Lesson 3.
:::

::: vocab
- **ἡ ἀρχή** — beginning; origin; rule, power
- **πρός** — towards, with, in the presence of *(with the accusative)*
- **ἀναγιγνώσκω** — I read *(literally *I know again*: **ἀνα-** + **γιγνώσκω**)*
- **ναί** — yes
- **οὐδέ** — and not, nor; not even
- **ἔτι** — still, yet
- **ἤδη** — already; by now
- **ἐνταῦθα** — here; there
- **θαυμάζω** — I wonder at, am amazed; I admire
- **διώκω** — I chase, pursue
- **λείπω** — I leave, leave behind
- **ὁ δεσπότης** — master *(of a household; of slaves)*

The first two belong to the sentence on the board. The rest are what the
fourth tablet needs: a verb for reading, a *yes* to answer the *no*, a
*nor* to go with it, three small words that say where and when, and three more verbs for the endings
you now own. Otherwise this lesson is review: the negative and the word
for God come back from Lesson 2 for a closer look. Every other word in it
has been met before, and the cumulative list at the back of this book is
now worth reading straight through.

::: note
**ἡ ἀρχή** is the ancestor of English *arch-* and *-archy*: an *archbishop*
is a first bishop, an *anarchy* is a state with no **ἀρχή** at all. The word
means *beginning* and *rule* at the same time, because to a Greek the thing
that comes first is the thing that governs. That is an argument buried in a
noun, and you will meet it again.

**ἀναγιγνώσκω** is **γιγνώσκω** with *again* or *up* in front of it: to
read is to recognise, letter by letter, what someone else already knew. It
is the ordinary Attic word for reading aloud, which is the only kind of
reading most Greeks did.

**ὁ δεσπότης** goes like **ὁ κριτής**, and like **ὁ κριτής** you should use
it as a subject only for now. **πρός** with **βλέπω** is the ordinary way
to say *look towards*, *look at*: **βλέπω πρὸς τὴν θύραν**.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From Lessons 2 to 5, and from memory.

1. Give the person and number of **γράφομεν**, and translate it.
2. **ὁ φίλος ἐστὶ κριτής.** Which noun is the subject, and what tells you?
3. Put **ὁ ξένος** into the vocative.
4. What case comes after **εἰμί** — accusative or nominative?
5. Translate **οὐ βλέπεις τὴν θύραν.**
6. What is **εἰσίν**, and why does it end in **-ν** here?
7. What does **ἀλλά** mean, and what does it join?
8. Supply the breathing: **_ώρα** — *hour*. <!--nocheck-->
9. Turn **ὁ αἴλουρος βλέπει τὸν ἵππον** round so that the horse sees the
   cat, changing endings only.
10. What does **ἐγώ** add to **ἐγὼ γράφω** that **γράφω** does not have?
:::

::: answers
1. First plural; *we write*.
2. **ὁ φίλος**: it has the article and **κριτής** has not. *The friend is a
   judge.*
3. **ὦ ξένε**.
4. Nominative. **εἰμί** links; it does not act upon.
5. *You do not see the door* — one person. A pupil who wrote *he* has read
   **-εις** as **-ει**.
6. Third plural of **εἰμί**, *they are*; the **-ν** is movable, and is there
   because the word stands at the end.
7. *But*; it joins clauses, or words, and sets them against each other.
8. **ὥρα** — rough, as the Latin *hora* remembers.
9. **τὸν αἴλουρον βλέπει ὁ ἵππος.** Two articles, two endings, nothing
   moved.
10. Emphasis: ***I*** *write*, as against somebody else. Nothing else; the
    **-ω** already said *I*.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
In the sentence on the board, **ὁ λόγος** appears three times and **θεός**
twice — once as **τὸν θεόν** and once as **θεός**.

1. What case is **τὸν θεόν**, and what two things tell you?
2. In the final clause, **καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**, which of the two nouns is
   the subject? Give your evidence.
3. Marcus says the final clause must mean *and God was the word*, because
   that is the order the words are in. What is wrong with his reasoning?
4. Count the articles in the sentence. How many are there, and what gender
   and case is each?
5. **πρὸς τὸν θεόν** — the accusative here is not the object of a verb.
   What is it there for?
6. How many verbs are in the sentence, and what is unusual about them?
7. **ἐν ἀρχῇ** has no article. Does the knife from Lesson 3 apply to it?
8. Rewrite the last clause with the article moved — **καὶ ὁ θεὸς ἦν λόγος**
   — and say what it would then mean.
9. Felix says that because **ὁ λόγος** stands first in the first two clauses
   and last in the third, the third clause must be about something else.
   Answer him.
10. Livia did not know what **ἦν** meant and construed the sentence anyway.
    List what she needed to know, and what she did not.
:::

::: answers
1. Accusative. The article **τόν** and the ending **-ον** both say so. It
   follows **πρός**, which takes the accusative.
2. **ὁ λόγος** is the subject, because it has the article and **θεός** does
   not. The sentence is telling us something about **ὁ λόγος**; what it
   tells us is **θεός**.
3. He is reading the word order and ignoring the article — the same mistake
   the court made in Lesson 4, in a sentence where it matters a great deal
   more. Word order in Greek carries emphasis, not grammar. The fronting of
   **θεός** does mean something, and what it means is emphasis, not
   subjecthood.

   Students who get this right should be told plainly that they have just
   done, unaided, something that is argued about by people with degrees.
4. Four: **ὁ** three times, masculine nominative, each with **λόγος**; and
   **τόν** once, masculine accusative, with **θεόν**. A pupil who counts
   three has missed **τόν** because it does not look like **ὁ**.
5. It is what **πρός** requires. A small word like **πρός** takes a case
   after it the way a verb takes an object, and this one takes the
   accusative; the vocabulary box says so.
6. Three, and all three are the same word, **ἦν** — one verb for each
   clause, which is how you know there are three clauses. It was glossed
   whole; you will own its endings in Part III.
7. Not as you have it. The knife works on a noun that could be the subject
   of **εἰμί** or what is said about the subject; **ἀρχῇ** sits inside a
   phrase after **ἐν**, and such phrases very often go without an article.
   *I cannot tell yet* is the correct answer, and the rules that would let
   you tell are Part II's.
8. *And God was a word* — or *was word*. The article has moved, so the
   subject has moved with it: the claim is now about God, and what it says
   of him is **λόγος**, a description. Exactly reversed from the board.
9. The subject is identified by the article, not by its position, and
   **ὁ λόγος** carries the article in all three clauses. The third clause
   is about the same thing as the other two; what has changed is that
   **θεός** has been put first for weight. Felix has made Marcus's
   mistake from the other end.
10. Needed: that **ὁ** marks the subject and its absence marks what is said
    about the subject; that **τόν … -ον** is an accusative; that
    **ὁ λόγος** is the same word three times. Not needed: the meaning of
    **ἦν**, of **ἐν**, of **ἀρχῇ**, or who wrote it. A pupil who lists the
    four unknown words under *needed* has not yet seen what she did.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Deny it"}
Put **οὐ** in its correct form in front of each, and translate.

1. ... **γράφω τὸν λόγον.**
2. ... **ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.**
3. ... **γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
4. ... **ἡ ὁδός.**
5. ... **διώκει τὸν αἴλουρον.**
6. ... **εὑρίσκεις τὴν ὁδόν.**
7. ... **ἀναγιγνώσκετε τὴν δέλτον.**
8. ... **ὁ ἑταῖρος.**
9. ... **λείπομεν τὴν οἰκίαν.**
10. ... **ἐνταῦθα μένει ὁ δεσπότης.**
:::

::: answers
1. **οὐ γράφω τὸν λόγον.** — *I am not writing the word.* (γ is a consonant)
2. **οὐκ ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.** — *He does not have the book.* (**ἔχει** has a
   smooth breathing)
3. **οὐ γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — *We do not know the truth.*
4. **οὐχ ἡ ὁδός.** — *Not the road.* (**ἡ** has a rough breathing)
5. **οὐ διώκει τὸν αἴλουρον.** — *He is not chasing the cat.*
6. **οὐχ εὑρίσκεις τὴν ὁδόν.** — *You are not finding the road.* A pupil
   who wrote **οὐκ** has seen a vowel and not read its breathing: **εὑ-**
   is rough.
7. **οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκετε τὴν δέλτον.** — *You are not reading the tablet.*
8. **οὐχ ὁ ἑταῖρος.** — *Not the companion.* It is the article that
   decides, not the noun behind it; **ὁ** is rough.
9. **οὐ λείπομεν τὴν οἰκίαν.** — *We are not leaving the house.*
10. **οὐκ ἐνταῦθα μένει ὁ δεσπότης.** — *The master does not stay here* —
    or better, *it is not here that the master stays*: **οὐ** stands in
    front of the word it denies, and here that word is *here*. **ἐνταῦθα**
    has a smooth breathing, so **οὐκ**.

The only thing being tested is whether the student looked at the next word.
That is the entire rule.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Read to the end"}
Translate, and then say which word you had to reach before you could be sure
of the rest.

1. **τὸν διδάσκαλον οὐ βλέπει ὁ μάρτυς.**
2. **ὁ κριτὴς οὐκ ἔχει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ἀλλὰ γιγνώσκει τὴν ὁδόν.**
3. **τὸν αἴλουρον διώκει ὁ ἵππος.**
4. **ὁ δεσπότης οὐ λείπει τὴν οἰκίαν, ἀλλὰ μένει.**
5. **οὐχ ὁ δοῦλος ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον, ἀλλὰ ὁ δεσπότης.**
6. **τὴν γραφὴν θαυμάζει ὁ ξένος.**
7. **ἐνταῦθα μένομεν καὶ ἀναγιγνώσκομεν.**
8. **τὸν νόμον οὐ γιγνώσκει ὁ δοῦλος, ἀλλὰ τὴν ὁδόν.**
9. **ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει οὐδὲ γράφει, ἀλλὰ καθεύδει.**
10. **ἡ Ἰουλία ἤδη ἀναγιγνώσκει, ἀλλὰ ὁ Μᾶρκος ἔτι βλέπει.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The witness does not see the teacher.* The sentence opens with an
   accusative, so until **ὁ μάρτυς** arrives at the very end you do not know
   who is failing to see. A left-to-right translator produces *the teacher
   does not see* and is then stranded.
2. *The judge does not have the truth, but he knows the road.* The second
   clause has no stated subject; **γιγνώσκει** supplies it. Students should
   be able to say that the subject of the second verb is still the judge,
   and that the **-ει** is what tells them.
3. *The horse is chasing the cat.* As 1: the subject is the last word, and
   a pupil who has the cat chasing the horse never got there.
4. *The master does not leave the house, but stays.* The subject of
   **μένει** is still the master; the **-ει** carries him across the
   **ἀλλά**.
5. *It is not the slave who reads the tablet, but the master.* **οὐχ**
   denies **ὁ δοῦλος**, not the reading — the reading happens; the question
   is who does it — and the second half has no verb of its own, so you must
   reach **ὁ δεσπότης** to learn the answer and then carry
   **ἀναγιγνώσκει** back to him.
6. *The stranger admires the writing.* Object first; **ὁ ξένος** at the end
   settles it.
7. *Here we stay and read.* No noun at all: the first ending, **-ομεν**,
   is the word you had to reach, and it tells you everything.
8. *The slave does not know the law, but he does know the road.* The second
   half supplies no verb; the first half lends it one, and the second
   object is in the same case as the first because it has the same job.
9. *The cat does not read, nor write, but sleeps.* **οὐδέ** carries the
   **οὐ** on into the second verb; **ἀλλά** then turns the sentence
   towards what the cat does do.
10. *Julia is already reading, but Marcus is still looking.* Both subjects
    are stated and both verbs are third singular, so the endings cannot
    tell them apart; it is **ἤδη** and **ἔτι** that carry the contrast,
    and **ἀλλά** that announces it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Compose, and then break it"}
1. Write one Greek sentence using an article, a noun, and a verb.
2. Write it again with the article removed from one noun, and say what —
   if anything — changes about who is doing what.
3. Write a sentence with **εἰμί** in which the article decides the subject.
   Then move the article to the other noun and translate both.
4. Write a sentence that uses **οὐκ** correctly. Then change one word so
   that it needs **οὐχ** instead.
5. Take **ὁ δεσπότης ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον** and deny it three ways, by
   placing the negative before the verb, before the subject, and before
   the object. Say what each version denies.
6. Write a sentence whose first word is an accusative, so that a reader has
   to wait for the subject.
7. Write a sentence with no noun in it at all, and say how a reader knows
   who the subject is.
8. Write Marcus's **οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκω** in the third person plural, and then
   write *they are not looking*.
9. Write a sentence that joins two verbs with **οὐδέ**.
10. Write one sentence that is correct Greek and untrue, and one that is
    correct Greek and true. Say how a reader could tell them apart from the
    Greek alone — if a reader can.
:::

::: answers
1. Answers will vary. Check the article agrees with its noun.
2. The point of this is that removing the article from the *object*
   usually changes very little, while removing it from the *subject* of a
   sentence with **εἰμί** can change which noun is the subject altogether.
   A student who discovers this on their own has found the mechanism
   behind the sentence on the board, and should be asked to explain it to
   Marcus.
3. For example **ὁ δεσπότης ἐστὶ κριτής** — *the master is a judge* —
   against **ὁ κριτής ἐστι δεσπότης** — *the judge is a master*. Check that
   the noun without the article is nominative in both; a pupil who gave it
   **-ην** has made **εἰμί** take an object.
4. For example **οὐκ ἔχω τὴν δέλτον** → **οὐχ εὑρίσκω τὴν δέλτον**. The
   change must be to the word after the negative; changing the object
   changes nothing about **οὐ**.
5. **ὁ δεσπότης οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον** — denies the reading: he
   does something else with it. **οὐχ ὁ δεσπότης ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον**
   — denies the master: someone else is reading it. **ὁ δεσπότης οὐ τὴν
   δέλτον ἀναγιγνώσκει** — denies the tablet: he is reading something
   else. The breathing on the next word changed the negative's shape each
   time; a pupil who kept **οὐκ** throughout has done the placing and not
   the looking.
6. For example **τὴν θύραν διώκει ὁ αἴλουρος.** Check that the first word
   really is accusative and the last really is nominative.
7. For example **μένομεν καὶ θαυμάζομεν.** The ending says *we*; nothing
   else does, and nothing else needs to.
8. **οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκουσιν.** **οὐ βλέπουσιν.** The negative changed shape
   because the verb changed: **ἀ-** against **β-**. A pupil who wrote
   **οὐκ βλέπουσιν** has copied the first and not looked at the second.
9. For example **ὁ δοῦλος οὐ λείπει τὴν οἰκίαν οὐδὲ φεύγει.** Check that
   **οὐ** or **οὐκ** or **οὐχ** opens the first denial and **οὐδέ** the
   second; **καὶ οὐ** is not wrong, but **οὐδέ** is what Greek says.
10. For example **ὁ λίθος ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον** and **ὁ διδάσκαλος
    ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον**. A reader cannot tell them apart from the
    Greek: both are faultless. What tells them apart is the world. The
    pupil who says *you cannot* has understood the difference between
    construing a sentence and believing it, which is the lesson, and the
    course.
:::

Write each in Greek, choosing the right form of **οὐ** by looking at the
word that follows it.

1. I do not know the road.
2. He does not have the book.
3. The judge does not tell the truth.
4. In the beginning was the word.
5. The slave is not reading the tablet.

::: answers
1. **οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.**
2. **οὐκ ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.** — **ἔχει** has a smooth breathing.
3. **ὁ κριτὴς οὐ λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — **λ** is a consonant, so plain
   **οὐ**.
4. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.** — They have just read it. Writing it out from
   English is a different act from recognising it on a board, and a pupil
   who can do the second but not the first has not yet learned it.
5. **ὁ δοῦλος οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει τὴν δέλτον.**
6. **οὐχ εὑρίσκομεν τὴν ὁδόν.** — Rough breathing on **εὑ-**; the usual
   miss is **οὐκ**.
7. **οὐχ ὁ ἑταῖρος, ἀλλὰ ὁ ξένος.** — No verb in the English, and none
   needed in the Greek.
8. **ὁ δεσπότης οὐκ ἐνταῦθα μένει** or **ὁ δεσπότης οὐ μένει ἐνταῦθα.**
   Accept either; the first denies *here*, the second denies *staying*,
   and the pupil should be able to say which they meant.
9. **ναί· ἀναγιγνώσκεις, ὦ Μᾶρκε.** — Vocative **-ε**, no article.
10. **ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει οὐδὲ βλέπει.** — **οὐκ** before the
    smooth breathing; **οὐδέ** for the second *nor*. A pupil who wrote
    **καὶ οὐ βλέπει** has said it, but not the Greek way.

Accept **οὐ γιγνώσκει τὴν ὁδόν** in 1 only if the pupil can say why they
chose the third person; the English says *I*.

::: {.voice source="John 1:1"}
Here it is, with the four words you were missing.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

- **ἐν ἀρχῇ** — in the beginning *(a case you meet in Part II; take the
  phrase whole for now)*
- **ἦν** — was *(the past of **εἰμί**; its endings come in Part III)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — the word, the account, the reasoning *(Lesson 1)*
- **καί** — and *(Lesson 2)*
- **πρός** — with, towards, in the presence of
- **τὸν θεόν** — God *(accusative, after **πρός**)*
- **θεός** — God *(nominative, and without the article)*

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
God.*

You have probably met that sentence in English. You have almost certainly
never been able to say what the Greek was doing, and now you can.

**The last clause is the one to look at.** Word for word it runs *and God
was the word*. It does not mean that, and you can prove it does not mean
that: **ὁ λόγος** carries the article and **θεός** does not, so **ὁ λόγος**
is the subject and **θεός** is what is being said about it. The order puts
**θεός** first for emphasis — the weight of the sentence falls on it — but
emphasis is not grammar, as Sabina's court established in Lesson 4.

So the Greek says: *the Word was God.* With **θεός** shouted.

**This is not a small point.** Translators have argued about that clause for
centuries, and a good deal of the argument is about the missing article —
whether leaving it off makes **θεός** a description rather than an
identification, and what the difference would amount to. You are not going
to settle that in this lesson. But you are now able to see what
they are arguing *about*, which is further than most readers of this
sentence ever get.

::: note
Koine, like the two sentences before it, and from the opening of the same
gospel. Its author is writing plain Greek on purpose: short words, no
subordinate clauses, the same four words repeated. It is the easiest great
sentence in the language, which is why it is the first one you can read.
:::

Marcus asked who wrote it. Theodoros told him, and then made him read it
again.
:::

::: {.reading title="Theodoros's fourth tablet" source="composed"}
The fourth tablet went round once Marcus had stopped being pleased
with himself. It was about the seventeen words on the board.

ὁ διδάσκαλος γράφει τὸν λόγον· «ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.» ὁ Μᾶρκος λέγει·
«οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκω. οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν γραφήν.»

ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγει· «οὐ βλέπεις, ὦ Μᾶρκε. λέγεις, ἀλλὰ οὐ βλέπεις. οὐχ ὁ
Μᾶρκος βλέπει, ἀλλὰ ἡ Λιβία· ἡ Λιβία ἤδη ἀναγιγνώσκει, ὁ Μᾶρκος ἔτι
λέγει.»

ὁ Μᾶρκος βλέπει. βλέπει τὸν λόγον, τὴν ἀρχήν, τὸν θεόν, καὶ
ἀναγιγνώσκει· «ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.»

«ναί,» λέγει ὁ διδάσκαλος, «ἀναγιγνώσκεις.» ὁ Μᾶρκος θαυμάζει.

ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει οὐδὲ θαυμάζει· λείπει τὴν τράπεζαν καὶ
διώκει τὸν παιδαγωγόν. ὁ δεσπότης οὐκ ἐνταῦθά ἐστιν.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Μᾶρκος, ἡ Λιβία** — Marcus, Livia; **ὦ Μᾶρκε** is the vocative
- **ἐν ἀρχῇ** — in the beginning *(take the phrase whole, as in The
  Ancient Voice)*
- **ἦν** — was *(the past of **εἰμί**, glossed whole; its endings come in
  Part III)*
- **ἐνταῦθά ἐστιν** — is here; take the accents as printed: **ἐστιν**
  leans on the word before it and lends it a second accent
- **« »** — quotation marks, a modern editor's
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything.

1. What does the teacher write, and where have you seen it before? How many
   of its words does the tablet expect you to know?
2. Marcus says two things he does not do. Translate both, and say why the
   negative has a different shape in each.
3. What does the teacher say Marcus is not doing? What does he say Marcus
   *is* doing instead?
4. **οὐχ ὁ Μᾶρκος βλέπει, ἀλλὰ ἡ Λιβία.** Why **οὐχ**? What exactly is
   being denied — the looking, or the looker?
5. **ἡ Λιβία ἤδη ἀναγιγνώσκει, ὁ Μᾶρκος ἔτι λέγει.** Which two small
   words carry the contrast, and what does each mean?
6. Marcus sees three things before he reads. Name them, give the case of
   each, and say where on the board they come from.
7. What does Marcus finally do, and what single word does the teacher
   answer with? Which word in the vocabulary box is it answering?
8. **ὁ Μᾶρκος θαυμάζει.** Translate it, and say what you think he is
   amazed at.
9. **οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκει οὐδὲ θαυμάζει** — what does **οὐδέ** do that
   **καὶ οὐ** would not? Who is the subject, and what does he do instead?
10. The last sentence is about somebody who is not in the room. Translate
    it, and explain the accent on **ἐνταῦθά**.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος** — the first clause of the sentence on the board,
   from The Ancient Voice. All of them: **ἐν ἀρχῇ** and **ἦν** are glossed,
   and **ὁ λόγος** has been theirs since Lesson 1.
2. **οὐκ ἀναγιγνώσκω** — *I do not read* (or *I cannot read*); **οὐ
   γιγνώσκω τὴν γραφήν** — *I do not know the writing*. **οὐκ** before the
   smooth breathing of **ἀ-**, plain **οὐ** before the consonant **γ**. The
   meaning is the same; only the next sound has changed.
3. That he is not looking: **οὐ βλέπεις**. That he is talking: **λέγεις,
   ἀλλὰ οὐ βλέπεις** — *you are talking, but you are not looking*. Then
   he says who *is* looking, which is the next question.
4. Because the next word is **ὁ**, with a rough breathing. The looker:
   **οὐχ** stands in front of **ὁ Μᾶρκος**, so it is Marcus who is denied,
   and **ἀλλὰ ἡ Λιβία** supplies the one who is looking instead. The
   looking itself is not denied — somebody is doing it.
5. **ἤδη** — *already* — and **ἔτι** — *still*. Livia is already reading;
   Marcus is still talking. Both verbs are third singular, so the endings
   cannot tell the two apart; the adverbs do.
6. **τὸν λόγον**, **τὴν ἀρχήν**, **τὸν θεόν** — all accusative, the
   objects of **βλέπει**. They are the three nouns of the sentence on the
   board: *word*, *beginning*, *God* — two the class knew already, and
   **ἀρχή**, which this lesson's box has just given them. He
   sees the words he knows, and then he can read.
7. He reads — **ἀναγιγνώσκει** — the very clause he said he could not.
   **ναί**, *yes*: the answer to his **οὐκ**, and the word the vocabulary
   box said was there for that purpose.
8. *Marcus is amazed* — or *wonders*. At the sentence, or at himself;
   accept either, and prefer a pupil who says both.
9. **οὐδέ** carries the negative on into the second verb — *nor* — where
   **καὶ οὐ** would start again from scratch; it is what Greek says. The
   subject is the cat, **ὁ αἴλουρος**, and it leaves the table and chases
   the paedagogus: **λείπει τὴν τράπεζαν καὶ διώκει τὸν παιδαγωγόν**.
10. *The master is not here.* **ἐνταῦθα** has a circumflex on its middle
    syllable; **ἐστιν**, which has no accent of its own, leans on it and
    lends it an acute on the last syllable. A pupil who asks why the word
    has two accents has noticed the right thing; the rule itself waits
    until Lesson 25, and until then the gloss says *take it as printed*.
:::
:::

::: question
1. Livia read a sentence containing four words she did not know. What did
   she use instead of the words? Could you do the same with a sentence in a
   language you have never studied?
2. **λόγος** has been translated *word*, *reason*, *account*, *argument*,
   *speech*, and simply left as *Logos*. If one Greek word needs six English
   ones, which language is being imprecise?
3. The last clause puts **θεός** first for emphasis. English cannot move
   words like that without changing the grammar. How does English show
   emphasis instead — and is its way better or worse?
4. Theodoros gave the class a sentence he knew they could not fully read.
   Was that fair?
:::

::: story
At the end of the lesson Theodoros rubbed out fifteen of the seventeen words.

He left **ὁ λόγος** on the board, in the corner, and it stayed there until
a slave cleaning the room wiped it off without knowing what
it was, which Theodoros said afterwards was the most appropriate thing that
had ever happened in that house.
:::
