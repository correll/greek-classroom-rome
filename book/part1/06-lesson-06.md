# The First Real Sentence

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros was at the board before anyone sat down, and he did not say good
morning.

He wrote seventeen words, put the charcoal down, and sat.

> **Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

"Read it."

"We cannot read that," said Marcus, before he had finished looking at it.

"You have not looked at it."

"I have looked at it enough. There are words there we have never seen. That
one, and that one, and whatever that is." He pointed at three of them,
correctly, and missed a fourth. "You have given us a sentence we do not
have the words for.
That is not a test, it is a trick."

"Is it the whole sentence you cannot read," said Theodoros, "or four words
of it?"

Marcus opened his mouth and shut it again.

Julia had already started writing. Sabina was reading it aloud under her
breath, which was the correct instinct. Quintus was arguing with himself.
Livia had not moved at all; she was looking at the board the way she looked
at things she was about to be right about.

"**ὁ λόγος**," she said eventually. "Three times. And it has **ὁ** every
time."

"Go on."

"And that one" — **θεός** — "has no **ὁ**. Not in the last part. So in the
last part **ὁ λόγος** is the one we are talking about, and **θεός** is what
is being said about it. Like **ὁ διδάσκαλός ἐστι φίλος**. Like the fine
things being hard."

The room went quiet in a way it had not done all term.

"I still do not know what **ἦν** means," Livia added.

"No," said Theodoros. "But you have just construed a sentence of which you
did not know four words, and you got the hardest thing in it right. **ἦν**
I shall simply tell you. The rest you have done yourselves."

Marcus looked at the board for a long moment.

"Who wrote it?" he said.
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Reading a sentence you cannot yet read

This lesson teaches no new machinery. It teaches a method, and the method is
worth more than any single ending.

**Read to the end before you translate any of it.** Greek routinely puts
words in an order English cannot copy, and the word that settles everything
is often the last one. A student who translates word by word from the left
is guessing with increasing confidence and decreasing accuracy.

**Find the article.** It is the most generous word in the language. Every
**ὁ**, **ἡ**, **τό** tells you gender, case, and — when something nearby
lacks one — which of two nouns is the subject.

**Mark what you are certain of and leave the rest alone.** You are allowed
to write *"I do not know this word"* and carry on. Livia read seventeen words
without knowing four of them, because she did not need those four to answer
the question she had been asked.

**Ask the question.** *What in the sentence tells you that?* If the answer
is "the order of the words" or "it sounded right", you have not finished.

::: note
This is the single most transferable habit in the course, and it has very
little to do with Greek. You are being taught to work out what a difficult
piece of text *does* say, from evidence on the page, rather than what it
probably says, from the general shape of it.

Keep this in mind when something fluent and confident is telling you
something and you cannot immediately say how it knows.
:::

## Saying no

One small word you met in Lesson 2, now with its rule in full, and one
small complication.

**οὐ** — *not*. It goes in front of the word it denies, and usually in front
of the verb.

> **οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.** — I do not know the road.

The complication is purely about sound. Greek dislikes **οὐ** sitting
against a vowel, so it grows a consonant:

| before | form | example |
|:--|:--|:--|
| a consonant | **οὐ** | **οὐ γράφω** — I am not writing |
| a smooth breathing | **οὐκ** | **οὐκ ἔχω** — I do not have |
| a rough breathing | **οὐχ** | **οὐχ ἡ ὁδός** — not the road |

This is the same instinct that gives English *a book* but *an apple*. It
carries no meaning whatever. Get it right anyway: it is one of the things a
reader notices.

::: latinbridge
Latin negates with *non*, which never changes its shape. Greek's three forms
look like extra work and are not: the form is chosen entirely by the sound
of the next word, so you never have to think about meaning to pick one.

The useful difference is elsewhere. Latin *non* and Greek **οὐ** both deny a
fact. Greek has a *second* negative, **μή**, for things wished, feared,
forbidden, or supposed — and the choice between them tells a reader what
kind of statement they are in. You meet **μή** in Part V.
:::

## Everything in Part I, in one line

You now have all of this, and it took six lessons:

- the alphabet, and the breathings and accents that go with it
- gender, and the article that announces it
- the nominative, for the one doing
- the accusative, for the one it is done to
- word order, which carries emphasis rather than grammar
- the vocative, for speaking to someone
- **καί** and **ἀλλά** to join, **οὐ** to deny
- six person-endings on the verb, and the six noises of **εἰμί**
- and the article used as a knife: *with* it, the subject; *without* it,
  what is said about the subject

That last one is why Livia could read a sentence containing four unknown
words. It is the most powerful single fact in Greek, and you have had it
since Lesson 3.
:::

::: vocab
- **ἡ ἀρχή** — beginning; origin; rule, power
- **πρός** — towards, with, in the presence of *(with the accusative)*

Otherwise this lesson is review: the negative and the word for God come
back from Lesson 2 for a closer look. Every other word in it has been met before,
and the cumulative list at the back of this book is now worth reading
straight through.

::: note
**ἡ ἀρχή** is the ancestor of English *arch-* and *-archy*: an *archbishop*
is a first bishop, an *anarchy* is a state with no **ἀρχή** at all. The word
means *beginning* and *rule* at the same time, because to a Greek the thing
that comes first is the thing that governs. That is an argument buried in a
noun, and you will meet it again.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros refused to translate the sentence on the board until the class had
finished with it.

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
In the sentence on the board, **ὁ λόγος** appears three times and **θεός**
twice — once as **τὸν θεόν** and once as **θεός**.

1. What case is **τὸν θεόν**, and what two things tell you?
2. In the final clause, **καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**, which of the two nouns is
   the subject? Give your evidence.
3. Marcus says the final clause must mean *and God was the word*, because
   that is the order the words are in. What is wrong with his reasoning?
:::

::: answers
1. Accusative. The article **τόν** and the ending **-ον** both say so. It
   follows **πρός**, which takes the accusative.
2. **ὁ λόγος** is the subject, because it has the article and **θεός** does
   not. The sentence is telling us something about **ὁ λόγος**; what it
   tells us is **θεός**.
3. He is reading the word order and ignoring the article — the same mistake
   the court made in Lesson 4, in a sentence where it matters a great deal
   more. Word order in Greek carries emphasis, not grammar. The fronting of
   **θεός** does mean something, and what it means is emphasis, not
   subjecthood.

   Students who get this right should be told plainly that they have just
   done, unaided, something that is argued about by people with degrees.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Deny it"}
Put **οὐ** in its correct form in front of each, and translate.

1. ... **γράφω τὸν λόγον.**
2. ... **ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.**
3. ... **γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
4. ... **ἡ ὁδός.**
:::

::: answers
1. **οὐ γράφω τὸν λόγον.** — *I am not writing the word.* (γ is a consonant)
2. **οὐκ ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.** — *He does not have the book.* (**ἔχει** has a
   smooth breathing)
3. **οὐ γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — *We do not know the truth.*
4. **οὐχ ἡ ὁδός.** — *Not the road.* (**ἡ** has a rough breathing)

The only thing being tested is whether the student looked at the next word.
That is the entire rule.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Read to the end"}
Translate, and then say which word you had to reach before you could be sure
of the rest.

1. **τὸν διδάσκαλον οὐ βλέπει ὁ μάρτυς.**
2. **ὁ κριτὴς οὐκ ἔχει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ἀλλὰ γιγνώσκει τὴν ὁδόν.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The witness does not see the teacher.* The sentence opens with an
   accusative, so until **ὁ μάρτυς** arrives at the very end you do not know
   who is failing to see. A left-to-right translator produces *the teacher
   does not see* and is then stranded.
2. *The judge does not have the truth, but he knows the road.* The second
   clause has no stated subject; **γιγνώσκει** supplies it. Students should
   be able to say that the subject of the second verb is still the judge,
   and that the **-ει** is what tells them.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Compose, and then break it"}
Write one Greek sentence using an article, a noun, and a verb.

Then write it again with the article removed from one noun, and say what —
if anything — changes about who is doing what.
:::

::: answers
Answers will vary. The point of the second half is that removing the article
from the *object* usually changes very little, while removing it from the
*subject* of a sentence with **εἰμί** can change which noun is the subject
altogether.

A student who discovers this on their own has found the mechanism behind the
sentence on the board, and should be asked to explain it to Marcus.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
Write each in Greek, choosing the right form of **οὐ** by looking at the
word that follows it.

1. I do not know the road.
2. He does not have the book.
3. The judge does not tell the truth.
4. In the beginning was the word.
:::

::: answers
1. **οὐ γιγνώσκω τὴν ὁδόν.**
2. **οὐκ ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.** — **ἔχει** has a smooth breathing.
3. **ὁ κριτὴς οὐ λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — **λ** is a consonant, so plain
   **οὐ**.
4. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.** — They have just read it. Writing it out from
   English is a different act from recognising it on a board, and a pupil
   who can do the second but not the first has not yet learned it.

Accept **οὐ γιγνώσκει τὴν ὁδόν** in 1 only if the pupil can say why they
chose the third person; the English says *I*.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 1:1"}
Here it is, with the four words you were missing.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

- **ἐν ἀρχῇ** — in the beginning *(a case you meet in Part II; take the
  phrase whole for now)*
- **ἦν** — was *(the past of **εἰμί**; its endings come in Part III)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — the word, the account, the reasoning *(Lesson 1)*
- **καί** — and *(Lesson 2)*
- **πρός** — with, towards, in the presence of
- **τὸν θεόν** — God *(accusative, after **πρός**)*
- **θεός** — God *(nominative, and without the article)*

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
God.*

You have probably met that sentence in English. You have almost certainly
never been able to say what the Greek was doing, and now you can.

**The last clause is the one to look at.** Word for word it runs *and God
was the word*. It does not mean that, and you can prove it does not mean
that: **ὁ λόγος** carries the article and **θεός** does not, so **ὁ λόγος**
is the subject and **θεός** is what is being said about it. The order puts
**θεός** first for emphasis — the weight of the sentence falls on it — but
emphasis is not grammar, as Sabina's court established in Lesson 4.

So the Greek says: *the Word was God.* With **θεός** shouted.

**This is not a small point.** Translators have argued about that clause for
centuries, and a good deal of the argument is about the missing article —
whether leaving it off makes **θεός** a description rather than an
identification, and what the difference would amount to. You are not going
to settle that this afternoon. But you are, as of today, able to see what
they are arguing *about*, which is further than most readers of this
sentence ever get.

::: note
Koine, like the two sentences before it, and from the opening of the same
gospel. Its author is writing plain Greek on purpose: short words, no
subordinate clauses, the same four words repeated. It is the easiest great
sentence in the language, which is why it is the first one you can read.
:::

Marcus asked who wrote it. Theodoros told him, and then made him read it
again.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Livia read a sentence containing four words she did not know. What did
   she use instead of the words? Could you do the same with a sentence in a
   language you have never studied?
2. **λόγος** has been translated *word*, *reason*, *account*, *argument*,
   *speech*, and simply left as *Logos*. If one Greek word needs six English
   ones, which language is being imprecise?
3. The last clause puts **θεός** first for emphasis. English cannot move
   words like that without changing the grammar. How does English show
   emphasis instead — and is its way better or worse?
4. Theodoros gave the class a sentence he knew they could not fully read.
   Was that fair?
:::

At the end of the hour Theodoros rubbed out fifteen of the seventeen words.

He left **ὁ λόγος** on the board, in the corner, and it stayed there until
the winter, when a slave cleaning the room wiped it off without knowing what
it was, which Theodoros said afterwards was the most appropriate thing that
had ever happened in that house.
:::
