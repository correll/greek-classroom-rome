# Who Did What?

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Sabina had rearranged the furniture before anyone else arrived.

The stools were in two rows facing a bench, and on the bench was
Theodoros's own chair, which she had moved without asking. Marcus and Felix
were standing at the front looking as though they had been accused of
something, which they had.

"A theft," Sabina announced. "Someone has taken the lamp. These two are
accused. The court will decide which."

"There is no theft," said Marcus. "The lamp is on the shelf."

"The court," said Sabina, "is not interested in the shelf."

Theodoros sat down on a stool at the back, in the manner of a man who has
decided not to interfere with something that is going well.

Sabina wrote on the board:

> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν φίλον.**

"The witness saw this," she said. "Who was seen?"

"The friend," said Livia.

"Good. Now." She rubbed out the sentence and wrote it again, in a different
order:

> **τὸν φίλον βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος.**

"Now who was seen?"

Felix said "the man" with great confidence, and was wrong, and knew it
about half a second later, which is the most instructive way to be wrong.

"The friend," said Livia again. "The endings did not move. Only the words
moved."

Sabina rubbed it out and wrote it a third time, and a fourth, with the words
in every order she could manage, and each time the verdict came back the
same. By the fifth she was visibly annoyed.

"Then the order does nothing at all," she said.

"Ask it a better question," said Theodoros, from the back. "Not *what does
the order mean*. Ask *what does the order do*."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The order moved; the meaning did not

You met this at the end of the last lesson, and Marcus got half of it. Here
is the whole of it.

These four sentences are all correct Greek, and all four mean the same
thing — *the man sees the friend*:

> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν φίλον.**
>
> **τὸν φίλον βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος.**
>
> **βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος τὸν φίλον.**
>
> **τὸν φίλον ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει.**

In every one of them, **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** is nominative and so is the subject,
and **τὸν φίλον** is accusative and so is the object. That is settled by the
endings before word order gets a vote.

So what is the order for? **Emphasis.** Greek puts first whatever it wants
you to notice first.

| Order | Nearest English |
|:--|:--|
| **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν φίλον** | The man sees the friend. |
| **τὸν φίλον βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος** | It is the *friend* the man sees. |
| **βλέπει ὁ ἄνθρωπος τὸν φίλον** | He *does* see him — the man sees the friend. |

English has to reach for extra words — *it is*, *does* — to do what Greek
does by moving two words to the front (**τὸν φίλον**), or one (**βλέπει**). This is not a quirk. It is most of
what makes Greek prose worth reading in Greek.

::: latinbridge
Latin does this too, and for the same reason: *hominem videt puer* puts the
weight on *hominem*. If you have met Latin word order and been told it is
"free", the word is misleading in both languages. It is not free. It is
*available* — free of grammatical duty, and therefore usable for something
better.
:::

## Speaking to someone: the vocative

So far every noun has been talked *about*. When you talk *to* someone,
Greek marks that too, with a case called the **vocative**.

For the nouns you know, the rule is small:

| | nominative | vocative |
|:--|:--|:--|
| masculine **-ος** | **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** | **ὦ ἄνθρωπε** |
| | **ὁ φίλος** | **ὦ φίλε** |
| | **ὁ διδάσκαλος** | **ὦ διδάσκαλε** |

Two things to notice.

**The ending changes from -ος to -ε.** That is the whole pattern for this
very common group of nouns.

**The article disappears, and ὦ takes its place.** You do not say *the man!*
when you call to someone, in Greek any more than in English. **ὦ** is not
really a word; it is a marker saying *I am addressing you*. Greek uses it
constantly and English has nearly lost it — we have only the slightly
theatrical *O*. Leave it untranslated, or translate it as nothing at all.

Other kinds of noun form the vocative differently, and the dictionary will
tell you when you need one. For now, **-ος → -ε** will carry you.

## Joining things: καί and ἀλλά

Two small words that do a great deal of work.

- **καί** — and
- **ἀλλά** — but

They join words to words, and whole clauses to whole clauses:

> **ὁ μάρτυς βλέπει καὶ λέγει.** — The witness sees and speaks.
>
> **ὁ κριτὴς λέγει, ἀλλὰ ὁ μάρτυς γράφει.** — The judge speaks, but the
> witness writes.

Notice the second one. Two clauses, each with its own nominative subject
and its own verb. Each half stands up on its own.

::: note
**One subject, one verb, and they must match.** Every verb you have met —
**βλέπει**, **γράφει**, **ἔχει**, **λέγει** — ends in **-ει**, and every
one of them means *he, she, or it does this*: one person, doing it now.

So each of these verbs needs exactly one singular subject. If you write a
sentence with two nominatives and one **-ει** verb, something has gone
wrong, and the sentence will tell you so. Lesson 5 gives you the rest of
the endings and the freedom that comes with them.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ κριτής** — judge; one who decides
- **ὁ μάρτυς** — witness
- **ἡ ἀλήθεια** — truth
- **λέγει** — he, she, or it says, speaks, tells
- **ὦ** — O (used before a name or noun when addressing someone)
- **ἀλλά** — but

::: note
**ὁ μάρτυς** is given here as a subject only. Its accusative belongs to a
family of endings you meet in Lesson 15, and there is no sense in learning
it twice. Until then, let the witness do the seeing and the speaking.

**ἡ ἀλήθεια** is worth a second look. It is built from **ἀ-** (*not*) and a
root meaning *escape notice*, *be hidden* — so *the unhidden thing*, *what
has not got past you*. Greek did not have a word meaning *truth* and then
explain it. It had a word meaning *not hidden*, and that became the word
for truth. Keep this in your pocket for the rest of the course.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Sabina reconvened the court, on the condition that Theodoros be allowed to
set the questions.

::: {.exercise title="Who did what?"}
For each sentence, say who is doing the action and who is receiving it, and
name the one piece of evidence that told you. Then translate.

1. **ὁ μάρτυς βλέπει τὸν ἄνθρωπον.**
2. **τὸν ἄνθρωπον βλέπει ὁ μάρτυς.**
3. **ὁ κριτὴς ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον.**
4. **τὴν ἀλήθειαν λέγει ὁ διδάσκαλος.**
5. **τὸ δῶρον ἔχει ὁ φίλος.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. Subject **ὁ μάρτυς** (**ὁ** and **-ς**); object **τὸν ἄνθρωπον**
   (**τόν** and **-ον**). *The witness sees the man.*
2. Exactly the same answer, on exactly the same evidence. The order has
   changed and the grammar has not. *The witness sees the man* — with the
   weight on the man.
3. Subject **ὁ κριτής**; object **τὸ βιβλίον**. *The judge has the book.*
   Note that **τὸ βιβλίον** is neuter and therefore ambiguous in form; it
   is the object because **ὁ κριτής** has already claimed the nominative.
4. Subject **ὁ διδάσκαλος**; object **τὴν ἀλήθειαν**. *The teacher tells
   the truth* — with the weight thrown hard onto the truth.
5. Subject **ὁ φίλος**; object **τὸ δῶρον**. *The friend has the gift.*

A student who answered 2 differently from 1 read the order and ignored the
endings. This is the single commonest mistake in the first term, it is
entirely reasonable, and it is why Sabina's court kept returning the same
verdict.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Address the court"}
Put each noun into the vocative, as if speaking to the person.

1. **ὁ φίλος**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος**
3. **ὁ ἄνθρωπος**

Then say what changed, in both the noun and the little word in front of it.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὦ φίλε**
2. **ὦ διδάσκαλε**
3. **ὦ ἄνθρωπε**

In each, the ending went from **-ος** to **-ε**, and the article **ὁ**
dropped away and was replaced by **ὦ**. The point worth drawing out: the
vocative is not a politeness. It is a grammatical statement that this
person is being spoken to rather than spoken about.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Join them up"}
Join each pair into one sentence using **καί** or **ἀλλά**, whichever makes
better sense, and say why you chose it.

1. **ὁ μάρτυς βλέπει.** / **ὁ μάρτυς λέγει.**
2. **ὁ κριτὴς γράφει.** / **ὁ φίλος βλέπει.**
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ μάρτυς βλέπει καὶ λέγει.** — One subject doing two things, and the
   second follows naturally from the first: **καί**. Note that the subject
   need not be repeated.
2. **ὁ κριτὴς γράφει, ἀλλὰ ὁ φίλος βλέπει.** — Two different subjects
   doing two different things, set against each other: **ἀλλά** is the
   better choice. **καί** is not *wrong* here; it is merely duller, and
   saying which is duller is a real grammatical judgement.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus tries it on"}
Marcus writes:

> **ὁ κριτὴς ὁ μάρτυς λέγει.**

He says it means *the judge and the witness speak*, and that since the
order does not matter he has done nothing wrong.

Say what is wrong with it, using the rule from this lesson, and then write
two correct sentences that say what he meant.
:::

::: answers
There are two nominative subjects and one **-ει** verb, which can have only
one. Nothing in the sentence says how the two nouns are related, because he
has left out the word that would say it.

What he meant needs either a join or two clauses:

> **ὁ κριτὴς λέγει καὶ ὁ μάρτυς λέγει.**

Students may also offer **ὁ κριτὴς καὶ ὁ μάρτυς λέγει**, which is the
natural thing to try. It is close, and the verb is the problem: two
subjects need a plural verb, which arrives in Lesson 5. Tell them they have
found the next lesson, and that finding it one lesson early is the right
kind of mistake.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
Write each of these in Greek. The English-to-Greek list at the back will
give you the words; you must supply the endings.

1. The judge sees the man.
2. The witness tells the truth.
3. Friend! *(addressing him)*
4. The teacher has the book, but the friend sees the house.
5. *It is the truth* the witness tells. *(put the weight on the truth)*
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ κριτὴς βλέπει τὸν ἄνθρωπον.**
2. **ὁ μάρτυς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
3. **ὦ φίλε.**
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἔχει τὸ βιβλίον, ἀλλὰ ὁ φίλος βλέπει τὴν οἰκίαν.**
5. **τὴν ἀλήθειαν λέγει ὁ μάρτυς.**

Only 5 has a wrong answer that is also correct Greek. A pupil who writes
**ὁ μάρτυς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν** has produced a faultless sentence that does
not do what was asked: the English said *it is the truth*, and that weight
has to be carried by putting **τὴν ἀλήθειαν** first. Mark it as a miss and
explain why — this is the lesson.

Composition is where the endings stop being a puzzle to solve and start
being a decision to make. Expect the first attempts to be slow.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 18:38"}
Here is a sentence somebody else wrote. It is three words long, it was
written down about three hundred years before Theodoros taught this lesson,
and you can already account for one of the three.

The setting is a courtroom — a real one, with a Roman governor presiding.
The man standing trial has just said that he came into the world to bear
witness to the truth. The governor answers him:

**τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια;**

- **τί** — what?
- **ἐστιν** — is *(borrowed whole for now, like the verbs in Lesson 3;
  Lesson 5 explains it)*
- **ἀλήθεια** — truth

*What is truth?*

Two words of it are new. The third, **ἀλήθεια**, is yours — you learned it
this afternoon, nominative, and here with no article: *what is truth?*,
not *what is the truth?*

::: note
**This is not Attic.** It is **Koine** — the ordinary Greek of the eastern
Mediterranean about four hundred years after Plato, and the Greek the
evangelists wrote. It is close enough to Attic that you can read it already,
and different enough that you will be able to point at the differences by
Part IV. This book will always tell you which kind of Greek you are looking
at. A course about checking claims cannot be vague about its own sources.
:::

Notice what the governor does *not* do. He does not answer the man, and he
does not wait for the man to answer him. Three words of Greek, and the trial
moves on.

Sabina's court spent an afternoon deciding who had done what, and could do
it because the endings told her. This court is asked a question the endings
cannot settle at all.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. If moving the words does not change who is guilty, what *does* the order
   change? When Sabina puts the accused first, what is she doing to her
   audience?
2. **ἀλήθεια** means *the thing that is not hidden*. Is that the same as
   *the thing that is true*? Can something be true and still hidden?
3. Theodoros told Sabina to ask what the order *does* rather than what it
   *means*. Was that a fair distinction, or was he avoiding her question?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
The court was adjourned without a verdict, on the grounds that no lamp had
been taken.

Sabina stayed behind to put the stools back. Theodoros, returning his chair
to its usual place, found that she had written one more sentence in the
bottom corner of the board, small, where it would not be noticed until
somebody came close.

> **ὦ διδάσκαλε, τὴν ἀλήθειαν λέγει ὁ μάρτυς.**

He left it there for the rest of the week.
:::
