# The School on the Caelian Hill {.unnumbered}

::: note
**Location** · The Caelian Hill, Rome   **Date** · Autumn, AD 400
**School** · A private household school for the children of a prosperous
Christian family and several families in its circle
**Class** · Eight pupils, approximately 12–14 years old
**Teacher** · Theodoros, a Greek-speaking Christian grammarian from Antioch

The characters and their school are fictional. The world around them is not.
:::

## The world outside the door

The Roman Empire is still powerful, and the children inhabit a changing
world. Their parents live among senators, officials, merchants, clergy, and
scholars. The old Roman educational tradition remains prestigious: an
educated person is expected to know Virgil and Cicero by heart. Their
teacher insists that the next step is Greek.

Rome in AD 400 is a Christian city that is still thoroughly a classical
one. The same household that keeps a copy of the Gospels also keeps Virgil,
and sees no contradiction in it. This is not a monastery and these are not
monks; the medieval world is centuries away. What the children are being
given is a late-antique education in which Homer and the Psalms sit on the
same shelf and are read with the same attention, though not with the same
authority.

## The room

The school occupies a room opening onto a courtyard. The children sit on
stools with wax tablets on their knees. They have reed pens, papyrus for
more important work, and a battered copy of Homer that Theodoros guards as
if it were a relic.

There is no blackboard, no textbook in every student's hands, and no
dictionary. The pupils learn by recitation, copying, memorisation,
translation, questions, and short compositions — which is to say, by very
nearly the methods still used today, minus the paper.

The experience of the classroom, however, feels surprisingly modern: a
bored student, an ingenious excuse, a competitive classmate, a
misunderstood instruction, and the teacher who somehow sees through
everything.

## The dramatic premise

Theodoros has a particular ambition. He wants his pupils to *understand*
Greek rather than merely memorise Greek forms. He believes that a child who
can explain why a sentence means what it means has begun to think like a
reader rather than a parrot.

The pupils regard this as a dangerous innovation.

Their parents expect discipline. Theodoros expects effort. The children
expect to get away with things. All three expectations will be tested.

## A note on language

The characters speak Latin among themselves. The narrative and the
classroom explanations are presented in English for the modern reader.
Their target language is Classical Attic Greek, with carefully introduced
Koine passages from the Septuagint and New Testament arriving in Part IV.

# Theodoros of Antioch {.unnumbered}

*Greek grammarian · approximately 45 years old*

Theodoros grew up in Antioch, where Greek was part of everyday life and
Christian communities lived alongside a rich classical literary culture. As
a young man he studied grammar and rhetoric, learned Homer by heart, and
developed an appreciation for the precision of Attic prose. He later taught
in the eastern Mediterranean. After travelling to Rome he found work
teaching the children of prosperous Latin-speaking families. His Christian
faith is sincere, his classical education extensive, and his patience
substantial — but not unlimited.

## His manner

He is quietly funny rather than theatrical. He rarely raises his voice; an
especially long silence is more alarming than shouting would be. He
respects Homer and Plato without pretending that every pagan author is
right about everything. He treats scripture with reverence and will not
permit the pupils to turn sacred passages into cheap jokes. He likes
questions, including difficult ones, provided they are honest. He is not
fooled by the children, but he remembers being a mischievous pupil himself.

## His teaching

His favourite principle is that grammar is not the enemy of thought. It is
what makes careful thought possible.

He asks, more often than anything else, **"What in the sentence tells you
that?"** When a pupil gives a correct translation by guessing, he asks for
the evidence. When a pupil makes a mistake for a good reason, he treats it
as an opportunity. When a pupil mocks someone else's mistake, he makes the
mocker explain the grammar.

He has one exploitable weakness: he can be provoked into defending the
finer points of Greek literature at considerable length. The pupils have
discovered this and occasionally ask innocent questions about Homer when
they wish to postpone their exercises.

## His history

Theodoros's father copied and sold manuscripts. As a boy, Theodoros learned
to tell a good copy from a bad one, and discovered that a single misplaced
word could change a sentence's meaning. His mother was a Christian who
taught him passages of scripture by memory. In his family, classical
literature and Christian faith were not rival possessions; both demanded
careful reading, though they were not identical in authority.

He came to Rome partly for work and partly out of curiosity about the
Latin-speaking Christian world. He is fascinated that his pupils can recite
Virgil but find Greek letters intimidating.

His long-term ambition is modest: to leave behind students who can read a
difficult text without being afraid of it.

# The Eight Pupils {.unnumbered}

Each child has a different relationship with language, authority,
friendship, and faith, so that the lessons generate character development
as well as grammar practice.

## Marcus Valerius — the clever skeptic

*Boy · 13 · son of the household*

Marcus is quick, competitive, and accustomed to doing well in Latin. He can
quote Virgil, has a fine memory, and believes that most problems can be
solved by finding the shortest route to the correct answer. His father
expects him to enter public life eventually; Marcus sees Greek as an
unnecessary obstacle on that road.

**His mischief** · He invents alternative translations that are technically
possible but absurd in context, hoping Theodoros will have to concede that
Greek is ambiguous.

**His growth** · He learns that cleverness without attention is a
sophisticated form of laziness. His rivalry with the teacher turns into
genuine intellectual respect.

## Julia Valeria — the precise one

*Girl · 13 · Marcus's older sister*

Julia is observant, disciplined, and quietly formidable. She has studied
Latin grammar seriously and notices endings and patterns before anyone else
in the room. She enjoys literature but dislikes being praised merely for
being diligent; she wants her answers taken seriously on their merits.

**Her mischief** · She writes immaculate, perfectly grammatical corrections
to Marcus's deliberately ridiculous translations and leaves them where he
will find them.

**Her growth** · She learns to take intellectual risks before she is
certain. Her difficulty is not competence but the fear of being publicly
wrong.

## Felix — the professional troublemaker

*Boy · 12 · son of a merchant family*

Felix's father trades in imported goods and travels widely. Felix has heard
sailors and merchants use unfamiliar words and is more curious about real
speech than about formal grammar. He is inventive, sociable, and not
especially interested in sitting still, and he is often the first to notice
that a textbook rule does not explain every sentence.

**His mischief** · He gives Greek nicknames to everyone, including the
teacher. He once labels the household cat with a Greek noun and insists
this counts as vocabulary practice.

**His growth** · He discovers that his ear for language is a real talent,
and becomes the pupil most interested in comparing everyday Koine with
literary Attic.

## Sabina — the storyteller

*Girl · 12 · daughter of a family friend*

Sabina loves stories, especially those in which a seemingly weak person
outwits someone powerful. She remembers plots and dialogue exceptionally
well but becomes impatient when asked to parse a sentence. She has a strong
sense of justice and will defend a classmate who is being ridiculed, even
when it is inconvenient.

**Her mischief** · She turns grammar exercises into miniature dramas,
assigning roles to nouns and verbs and making the class act out sentences.

**Her growth** · She comes to understand that grammar is not separate from
storytelling: case, tense, and word order are precisely what determine who
does what to whom.

## Quintus — the aspiring philosopher

*Boy · 14 · son of a minor official*

Quintus is serious, reflective, and eager to be thought mature. He likes
discussions about courage, duty, justice, and what makes a good life. He
has met Latin summaries of philosophical ideas and is thrilled to learn
that he may read some of the original Greek.

**His mischief** · He sometimes deploys a philosophical argument to explain
why he should not have to complete an ordinary exercise.

**His growth** · He learns that understanding a profound idea requires
attention to small details, and that the meaning of a sentence cannot be
separated from the words in which it is expressed.

## Livia — the quiet observer

*Girl · 14 · daughter of a physician*

Livia is patient and analytical. Her father has taught her to observe
carefully before drawing conclusions. She is fascinated by the relationship
between words and the things they describe, and is less interested in
winning arguments than in discovering what is actually true.

**Her mischief** · She rarely starts trouble, but she can end it with one
understated observation. She is particularly good at pointing out when
Marcus's elaborate explanation contradicts his own translation.

**Her growth** · She learns to speak up earlier. Her careful reasoning
becomes an example for the class.

## Theodosius — the earnest Christian

*Boy · 12 · nephew of a local deacon*

Theodosius takes his faith seriously and knows several passages of
scripture by heart in Latin. He is excited to discover the Greek behind
familiar readings, but worries that studying pagan authors might distract
him from what matters most. He is sincere rather than sanctimonious, though
he sometimes feels responsible for maintaining order in the classroom.

**His mischief** · He can be unexpectedly competitive about memorisation,
and occasionally quotes a biblical proverb to settle an argument that has
nothing to do with the proverb.

**His growth** · He learns to approach classical literature with
discernment rather than fear, and to distinguish literary appreciation from
religious agreement.

## Aelia — the adventurous one

*Girl · 13 · daughter of a family with eastern Mediterranean connections*

Aelia has relatives who travel between Rome and the eastern provinces. She
has heard Greek spoken and knows a few expressions, but has never studied
the language systematically — which gives her a small advantage that she
sometimes exaggerates. She loves riddles, puzzles, and practical
challenges, and is first to volunteer when Theodoros proposes an experiment
or a translation contest.

**Her mischief** · She claims to understand a Greek phrase from having
overheard it, then has to improvise when Theodoros asks her to explain its
grammar.

**Her growth** · She discovers that familiarity is not the same as mastery.
Her curiosity becomes a bridge between the Latin-speaking pupils and the
Greek world.

## The class dynamic

Marcus and Julia compete over accuracy. Felix and Sabina conspire to make
lessons more entertaining. Quintus turns everything into a philosophical
debate. Livia quietly catches the errors everyone else misses. Theodosius
protects the seriousness of scripture but discovers humour in ordinary
learning. Aelia is the bridge between the unfamiliar language and the world
beyond the classroom.

The girls are not present to balance the boys. They have distinct
intellectual strengths, ambitions, and flaws. Their presence in this
particular private household school is plausible, though it was not
universal Roman practice.

No pupil is permanently confined to a strength. Julia may excel at forms,
Sabina at narrative interpretation, Felix at vocabulary, and Quintus at
reflection — but over thirty lessons each of them improves at something
they began by being bad at. That is the arc.
