# To the Student {.unnumbered}

You are about to learn a language that nobody speaks.

That is a strange thing to do, and it is worth being honest about it at the
start. Nobody will ever ask you for directions in Classical Greek. You will
not need it to order dinner. If the only point of learning a language were
to talk to people who are alive, this book would be a waste of your time.

Here is the point instead.

For about a thousand years, a great many of the most interesting things
anyone thought were written down in this language. Some of them were
arguments about what makes a person good. Some were stories about a man who
spent ten years trying to get home. Some were letters written in a hurry to
small groups of people in the first century, by men who expected the world
to end soon. All of these have been translated, and the translations are
good. But a translation is someone else's reading. When you can read the
Greek, the reading is yours.

## What this is going to be like

Difficult at first, and then differently difficult.

The first six lessons are mostly about the alphabet. This is the part that
looks hardest and is actually easiest: it is twenty-four letters and some
marks, and almost everyone has it within a fortnight. Do not be discouraged
in week one and do not be overconfident in week three.

The middle of the course is where Greek asks something real of you. Greek
nouns change their endings depending on what they are doing in the
sentence, and so do the adjectives that go with them, and the verbs change
depending on who is acting and when. This means that in Greek, the
*ending of a word is information*. If you skip the endings and guess from
the vocabulary, you will produce translations that are roughly right and
sometimes catastrophically wrong, and you will not be able to tell which.

Your teacher in this story, Theodoros, has one question he asks more than
any other: **What in the sentence tells you that?** It is a fair question
and you should start asking it of yourself now. A correct answer arrived at
by guessing is a lucky accident. A correct answer you can defend is Greek.

## About the children in this book

The eight pupils in these lessons are not models of good behaviour. They
are bored, competitive, clever, occasionally unkind to each other, and
extremely good at inventing reasons why the homework was impossible.

They also get things wrong in instructive ways. When Marcus produces a
translation that is grammatically possible and obviously absurd, he is
making an error you are about to make. When Julia refuses to answer until
she is certain, she is doing something that will slow her down for the
whole first term. Watch them. Some of their mistakes will save you from
making your own.

## Three pieces of practical advice

**Say it out loud.** Greek was written to be heard. Reading silently is
how you lose the accents, the rhythm, and about half of what makes a line
of Homer worth reading. You will feel ridiculous for a week.

**Learn the vocabulary the way it is printed.** When a noun is given as
**ὁ λόγος**, the little word **ὁ** is part of what you are learning — it
tells you the noun is masculine, which you cannot work out from the ending
alone. Learning the bare word costs you nothing now and costs you an hour
later.

**Do not translate word by word.** Read to the end of the sentence first.
Greek routinely puts things in an order that English cannot copy, and the
word that explains everything is often the last one.

## One more thing

You will meet, fairly early, a sentence you can read without help. It will
be short. It will probably be something a Greek child also had to read.
When it happens, notice it. That is the thing this whole book is for.
