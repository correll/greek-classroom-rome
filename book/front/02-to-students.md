# To the Student {.unnumbered}

You are about to learn a language that nobody speaks.

That is a strange thing to do, and it is worth being honest about it at the
start. Nobody will ever ask you for directions in Classical Greek. You will
not need it to order dinner. If the only point of learning a language were
to talk to people who are alive, this book would be a waste of your time.

Here is the point instead.

## Greek shows its working

In English, you know what a sentence means mostly by where the words sit.
*The dog bit the man* and *the man bit the dog* use the same five words; only
the order tells you who is in trouble. English hides its grammar inside
habits you absorbed before you could read, and which you have never had to
examine.

Greek does not hide anything. In Greek, every noun carries a visible mark
saying what job it is doing in the sentence, and every verb carries a visible
mark saying who is acting and when. You can move the words almost anywhere
and the meaning holds, because the meaning is written on the outside of each
word, where you can point at it.

That has a consequence worth taking seriously. When you read a Greek
sentence correctly, you can **prove** it. You can say: this word is the
subject, and here is the letter that tells me so; this action happened once
rather than repeatedly, and here is the syllable that tells me so. Your
reading stops being an impression and becomes an argument from evidence.

Very few things you study let you do that with *meaning*. Mathematics lets
you prove things about quantity. Greek lets you prove things about what a
sentence says.

## Why that is worth having now

You are growing up surrounded by machines that produce fluent language on
demand. They are useful, and they will be more useful still. They also have
two properties you need to understand early.

The first is that **fluent is not the same as correct**. A machine can
produce a paragraph that sounds authoritative and is wrong, and nothing in
how it sounds will tell you which it is. The only defence is the habit of
checking a claim against the evidence instead of against how confident it
seems. That is the same habit as reading Greek: *do not accept a reading
because it sounds right; find the thing on the page that makes it right.*

The second is that **you get what you ask for, not what you meant**. Saying
precisely what you want — no looser, no vaguer — turns out to be a learnable
skill and an uncommon one. Greek teaches it by punishing imprecision
immediately. One letter is the difference between the man doing something and the
man having it done to him. An accent in the wrong place turns *who?* into *someone*. There is no
partial credit from a language; the sentence either says what you meant or
it says something else, and it will tell you which.

Your teacher in this story, Theodoros, asks one question more than any other:
**What in the sentence tells you that?** It is the whole course in seven words,
and it is a question worth being able to ask of anything that is confidently
telling you something — a classmate, a textbook, a politician, or a machine.

## And then there is what the Greeks wrote

For about a thousand years, a great many of the most interesting things
anyone thought were written down in this language. Some were arguments about
what makes a person good. Some were stories about a man who spent ten years
trying to get home. Some were letters written in a hurry, in the first
century, by people who expected the world to end shortly.

All of these have been translated, and the translations are good. But a
translation is someone else's reading — someone else's decision about what
an ambiguous sentence meant. When you can read the Greek, the reading is
yours, and so is the responsibility.

## What this is going to be like

Difficult at first, and then differently difficult.

The first two lessons are the alphabet. This is the part that looks hardest
and is actually easiest: twenty-four letters and some marks, and almost
everyone has it within a fortnight. Do not be discouraged in week one
and do not be overconfident in week three.

The middle of the course is where Greek asks something real of you. If you
skip the endings and guess from the vocabulary, you will produce translations
that are roughly right and occasionally catastrophically wrong, and — this is
the dangerous part — you will not be able to tell which kind you have just
produced. A correct answer arrived at by guessing is a lucky accident. A
correct answer you can defend is Greek.

## Three pieces of practical advice

**Say it out loud.** Greek was written to be heard. Reading silently is how
you lose the accents, the rhythm, and about half of what makes a line of
Homer worth reading. You will feel ridiculous for a week.

**Learn the vocabulary exactly as it is printed.** When a noun is given as
**ὁ λόγος**, the little word **ὁ** is part of what you are learning — it
tells you the noun is masculine, which you cannot work out from the ending
alone. Learning the bare word costs you nothing now and costs you an hour
later.

**Read to the end of the sentence before you translate any of it.** Greek
routinely puts things in an order English cannot copy, and the word that
explains everything is often the last one.

## One more thing

You will meet, fairly early, a sentence you can read without help. It will be
short. It will probably be something a Greek child also had to read.

When it happens, notice it. That is the thing this whole book is for.
