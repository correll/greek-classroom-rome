# How This Course Pronounces Greek {.unnumbered}

This course teaches a **reconstructed Classical Attic** pronunciation: an
approximation of how educated Athenians sounded in the fifth and fourth
centuries BC.

That choice needs defending, because it is not the only option and not the
most common one in American classrooms.

::: note
**Why it matters that you know which system you are learning.**
Greek has been read aloud in at least three widely used ways, and they
differ substantially. A student who is taught one system without being told
it is a system will later meet another and conclude, wrongly, that someone
is making mistakes. Nobody is. They are different conventions, chosen for
different reasons.
:::

## The three conventions

**Reconstructed Classical Attic** — what this book teaches. It aims at the
sound of Athens around 400 BC, reconstructed from spelling errors in
inscriptions, ancient grammarians' descriptions, puns in comedy, loanwords
into and out of Greek, and the development of the modern language. It is
the only one of the three that tries to be historically accurate for the
literature this course reads.

**Erasmian** — the most common convention in English-speaking classrooms,
named for Erasmus, who described it in 1528. It is a teaching compromise
rather than a reconstruction: it keeps every vowel and diphthong
distinct so that spelling can be heard, which is pedagogically convenient
and historically approximate. Most American Greek textbooks use some form
of it.

**Modern Greek** — how the language is pronounced today, and how it is
read aloud in the Greek Orthodox world. Twenty-four centuries of sound
change separate it from Plato; in particular, **ι η υ ει οι υι** have all <!--nocheck-->
fallen together as *ee*, and **β δ γ θ φ χ** have become fricatives.

This course chooses reconstruction because the course is about the ancient
world and because the sound of the language is part of what we are reading
— Homer's hexameters and Greek wordplay stop working if the vowels are
wrong. Where the reconstruction differs sharply from Erasmian, a note says
so, since students may meet Erasmian elsewhere.

| Ψ ψ | **ψῖ** | [ps] | la*ps* |
| Ω ω | **ὦ μέγα** | [ɔː] long | *saw* held long |

::: note
**σ and ς.** The same letter. **ς** is used at the end of a word,
**σ** everywhere else: **σοφός**, not *σοφοσ* or *ςοφός*. <!--nocheck-->

**Three aspirated stops.** **θ φ χ** are *not* the English sounds in
*thin*, *fin*, and the Scottish *loch*. In Classical Attic they are plain
**τ π κ** with a puff of breath after them — the difference between English
*top* (aspirated) and *stop* (not). Reading **φ** as *f* is the single most
common departure from the reconstruction, and it is what Erasmian and
Modern Greek both do.

**γ before a velar.** Before **κ γ χ ξ**, gamma is [ŋ], the *ng* of *sing*:
**ἄγγελος** is *angelos*, not *ag-gelos*.
:::

## Vowel length

Greek distinguishes long and short vowels, and the distinction changes
meaning. Four letters are unambiguous:

- **ε** is always short; **η** is its long partner.
- **ο** is always short; **ω** is its long partner.

Three letters are ambiguous in writing: **α ι υ** may be long or short, and
the spelling does not say which. This book marks the length in vocabulary
lists where it matters, using a macron for long (**ᾱ**) when the
distinction affects accentuation or scansion.

Hold a long vowel roughly twice as long as a short one. This feels
exaggerated and is correct.

## Diphthongs

| | Sound | Roughly as in |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **αι** | [ai] | *aisle* |
| **αυ** | [au] | *how* |
| **ει** | [eː] | long close *e*; German *See* |
| **ευ** | [eu] | *eh* + *oo*, run together |
| **οι** | [oi] | *boil* |
| **ου** | [oː] → [uː] | *soap*, becoming *soup* |
| **υι** | [yi] | French *u* + *ee* |
| **ηυ** | [ɛːu] | long *ai* + *oo* |

::: note
**ει and ου are the odd ones.** By the fifth century these were no longer <!--nocheck-->
true diphthongs in Attic but long single vowels — **ει** a close long
*e*, **ου** a close long *o* that became a long *oo* during the fourth
century. They are spelled as digraphs for historical
reasons. Erasmian pronounces **ει** as *ay* in *day*, which is close
enough that confusion is rare.
:::

**Iota subscript.** When a long **ᾱ η ω** was followed by an **ι**, the
iota ceased to be pronounced in the Hellenistic period and, in Byzantine
manuscripts, came to be written underneath:
**ᾳ ῃ ῳ**. Under a capital it is written beside instead, and called *iota
adscript*: **ᾼ**. Do not pronounce it. Do not ignore it either — it is
often the only thing distinguishing a dative from a nominative.

## Breathing marks

Every word beginning with a vowel carries one of two marks over that vowel.

- **Smooth breathing** **᾿** — no sound at all. **ἀγαθός** = *agathos*.
- **Rough breathing** **῾** — an *h* before the vowel. **ἁγνός** =
  *hagnos*.

Two further rules: initial **υ** always takes the rough breathing
(**ὑπέρ**, *hyper*), and initial **ρ** takes it too (**ῥήτωρ**), giving a
voiceless trill.

On a diphthong the breathing sits over the *second* vowel: **αἱ**, **οὐ**.
On a capital it sits before the letter: **Ἀθῆναι**, **Ἑλλάς**.

::: latinbridge
If you have met any Latin, you already know this sound change. Greek
**ὑπέρ** is Latin *super*; Greek
**ἕξ** is Latin *sex*; Greek **ἅλς** is Latin *sal*. Where Greek has a rough
breathing, Latin often has an *s*. Both go back to an original *s*- that
Greek weakened to *h* and then, in writing, to a small hook.
:::

## Accents

Greek accents mark **pitch**, not stress. The accented syllable was sung
slightly higher, not hit harder — about a musical fifth higher, according
to Dionysius of Halicarnassus, writing in the first century BC.

- **Acute** **´** — the voice rises on that syllable: **λόγος**.
- **Circumflex** **῀** — the voice rises and falls within one long
  syllable: **δῶρον**. It can only sit on a long vowel or diphthong.
- **Grave** **`** — an acute that has been suppressed because another word
  follows immediately: **καὶ** rather than *καί* in running text.

::: note
**What to actually do in class.** Producing genuine pitch accent is hard,
and most teachers and students approximate it with stress — saying
**LO-gos** rather than singing it. That is an acceptable compromise and
this book will not pretend otherwise.

What is *not* acceptable is ignoring the accent mark. Where the accent
falls distinguishes real words: **τίς** means *who?* and **τις** means
*someone*; **ποῦ** means *where?* and **που** means *somewhere*. Whether
you sing it or stress it, put it in the right place.

Accents were not written in the classical period at all — they were
invented by Alexandrian scholars around 200 BC to help non-native readers.
Theodoros's battered Homer would have had few, if any. Yours does, and you should
be grateful.
:::

## Punctuation

| Mark | Meaning |
|:--|:--|
| **.** | full stop, as in English |
| **,** | comma, as in English |
| **·** | raised dot — colon or semicolon |
| **;** | **question mark** |

The last one catches everyone once. A Greek semicolon asks a question.

## A short reading drill

Read these aloud, slowly, applying the rules above. All are real words you
will meet.

**ἄνθρωπος** · **λόγος** · **φίλος** · **θεός** · **ψυχή** · **ἀρχή** ·
**οἶκος** · **ὁδός** · **ῥήτωρ** · **ξένος** · **Ἀθῆναι** · **ἀγγέλλω**

Check yourself on four things: **θ** and **φ** puffed, not hissed; **υ**
rounded like French *u*; the rough breathing on **ὁδός** and **ῥήτωρ**
sounded as *h*; and **ἀγγέλλω** beginning *ang-*, not *ag-g*.
