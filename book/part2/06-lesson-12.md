# The First Reading Challenge

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Four teams," said Theodoros. "Each team gets a sentence. You translate it,
and then you defend it."

"Defend it against what?" said Quintus.

"Against me."

He wrote the rules on the board, which he had never done before, and the
class understood from that alone that something had changed.

> **No marks for the translation.**
>
> **All the marks for the defence.**

"That is absurd," said Marcus. "The translation is the work."

"The translation is the *result*. Anyone can arrive at a result. Felix
arrives at results constantly." Felix bowed. "What I am buying today is the
reason. If your English is perfect and you cannot tell me which ending made
it so, you score nothing. If your English is wrong and you can show me
precisely which ending you misread and why, you score almost everything."

There was a silence while this was considered.

"So we can be wrong," said Livia slowly, "and still win."

"You can be wrong *usefully*. It is the only kind of wrong worth being."

Sabina's team got their sentence first. She read it, and then read it again,
and then said a word she had learned from the kitchen.

"It has four nouns in it."

"It has," Theodoros agreed.

"And only one of them is the subject."

"Yes."

"And you are not going to tell us which."

"I am going to ask you which," he said, "which is a different and much
better thing."
:::

## Everything you have, in one page

No new grammar today. This is the whole of Parts I and II, arranged so that
you can see it at once, and it is worth copying out by hand.

**The verb**: **-ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε, -ουσι(ν)**

**εἰμί**: **εἰμί, εἶ, ἐστί(ν), ἐσμέν, ἐστέ, εἰσί(ν)**

**The four jobs**: nominative does; accusative receives; genitive owns or
comes from; dative is given to.

**The article as a knife**: adjective inside the group describes; outside it,
asserts.

## How to attack a sentence you have not seen

The method, in the order you should use it.

**One. Read it all.** Do not translate anything until you have reached the
end. The word that settles the sentence is often the last one.

**Two. Find the verb.** It tells you the person and number of the subject
before you have found the subject.

**Three. Find the nominative.** There will be one, or it will be hiding in
the verb ending. If you think you have found two, one of them is a
predicate.

**Four. Assign every other noun a job.** Accusative: it is being acted on.
Genitive: it belongs to something. Dative: something is coming to it.

**Five. Only now, build the English.** And then check it against the
endings, not against whether it sounds plausible.

::: note
Step five is where this course differs from most. A translation that sounds
right is not evidence of anything. The endings are the evidence, and they
are still on the page after you have produced your English — which means
you can always check, and are therefore always obliged to.
:::
:::

::: note
**Learn the article perfectly.** Everything else in Greek can be looked up,
guessed at, or worked out from context. The article cannot: it is too short,
too common, and too similar to itself. A pupil who knows the twenty-four
forms of the article cold will read Part III comfortably, and a pupil who
does not will struggle with every sentence in it for reasons they will
blame on the vocabulary.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
Ten quick questions from Lessons 8 to 11. From memory.

1. Translate **περὶ τοῦ νόμου**.
2. **φέρετε** — person, number, and translation.
3. Write *in the letter* in Greek.
4. Translate **ἀγαθὸς ὁ υἱός** and **ὁ ἀγαθὸς υἱός**.
5. **σὺ μανθάνεις, ἐγὼ διδάσκω.** Translate, and say why both pronouns are
   there.
6. Give the genitive plural of **ὁ ἄγγελος**.
7. Translate **πέμπω τὸ δῶρον σὺν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ**.
8. What is the feminine of **μικρός**, and why is it not **μικρή**?
9. **ἀκούουσι** — person and number; and when does it end in **-ν**?
10. In **τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ υἱοῦ**, which word is in the genitive, and what two
    things tell you?
:::

::: answers
1. *About the law* — or *concerning the custom*.
2. Second plural; *you (all) carry*.
3. **ἐν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ** — both iota subscripts.
4. *The son is good*; *the good son*. The first asserts, the second
   describes; the article's position is the whole difference.
5. *You learn, I teach.* Because they are set against each other: the
   endings already say *you* and *I*, so the pronouns are there for the
   contrast.
6. **τῶν ἀγγέλων.**
7. *I send the gift together with the letter.*
8. **μικρά**; after **ρ** the first declension keeps alpha, as **οἰκία**
   does.
9. Third plural; before a vowel, and usually at the end of a sentence.
10. **τοῦ υἱοῦ**; **τοῦ** and **-οῦ**.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team one"}
**οἱ σοφοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων φέρουσι τοῖς υἱοῖς τὰ καλὰ δῶρα.**

Translate it, then defend it:

1. Give the translation.
2. **οἱ σοφοί** — gender, number and case; and what kind of word is doing
   the subject's job?
3. **τῶν ἀνθρώπων** — case; what does it hang on, and what does the phrase
   mean once it is attached?
4. **φέρουσι** — person and number; what does it agree with?
5. **τοῖς υἱοῖς** — case and job.
6. **τὰ καλὰ δῶρα** — **τά** could be two cases. Which is it here, and how
   do you decide?
7. Is **καλά** describing or asserting? Give the evidence.
8. Rewrite the sentence so that the sons bring the gifts to the wise.
9. Put the whole sentence into the singular.
10. Another team translated it *the wise men carry the sons' fine gifts*.
    Which ending did they misread, and what does the rule say they score?
:::

::: answers
1. *The wise among the men bring the fine gifts to the sons.*
2. Masculine nominative plural; the subject. It is an adjective with the
   article and no noun — *the wise ones* — turned into a noun by **οἱ**,
   as **οἱ σοφοί** was in Lesson 10.
3. Genitive plural, depending on **οἱ σοφοί**: *the wise of the men*, i.e.
   those among them who are wise.
4. Third plural, agreeing with **οἱ σοφοί**.
5. Dative plural; the ones the gifts go to.
6. Neuter plural, and here accusative, because the nominative is already
   taken by **οἱ σοφοί** and the verb agrees with that. **τά** alone
   cannot tell you; the rest of the sentence can.
7. Describing: **καλά** stands inside the article group, between **τά**
   and **δῶρα**. *The fine gifts*, not *the gifts are fine*.
8. **οἱ υἱοὶ φέρουσι τοῖς σοφοῖς τὰ καλὰ δῶρα.** Two phrases swap case;
   nothing else moves. A team that also moved the words has done more
   than was asked and should say why.
9. **ὁ σοφὸς τῶν ἀνθρώπων φέρει τῷ υἱῷ τὸ καλὸν δῶρον.** Eight endings
   change; **τῶν ἀνθρώπων** stays plural, because *the men* are still
   many.
10. **τοῖς υἱοῖς**: they read a dative as a genitive. Under the rule they
    score almost everything, if they can say exactly that; a team that
    translates correctly and cannot account for **τὰ καλὰ δῶρα** scores
    nothing.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team two"}
**ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ τοῦ κριτοῦ μανθάνομεν τοὺς λόγους τῆς ἀληθείας.**

Translate it, then defend it:

1. Give the translation.
2. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — case, and why; and what mark must appear on both
   words?
3. **τοῦ κριτοῦ** — case; whose what?
4. **μανθάνομεν** — person and number; where is the subject?
5. **τοὺς λόγους** — case and job.
6. **τῆς ἀληθείας** — case; which word does it attach to?
7. There is no nominative anywhere in the sentence. Is that an error in
   the question?
8. Change the verb so that *they* learn; then so that *you (one person)*
   learn. What else changes?
9. Replace *in the house* with *out of the house*. Which words change, and
   why?
10. Another team translated it *the judge learns the words of truth in the
    house*. Which word did they treat as a nominative, and what should
    have stopped them?
:::

::: answers
1. *In the house of the judge we learn the words of truth.*
2. Dative, after **ἐν**; place. The iota subscript, on **τῇ** and on
   **οἰκίᾳ**.
3. Genitive; whose house — the judge's.
4. First plural; the subject is *we*, and is nowhere written. It lives in
   **-ομεν**.
5. Accusative plural; what is learned.
6. Genitive singular; it attaches to **τοὺς λόγους** — *the words of
   truth*.
7. No. The subject is in the verb ending, which is where Greek is happy to
   leave it. A team that reports this as an error in the question should
   be told that they have made the single most important observation of
   the afternoon — and then shown **-ομεν**.
8. **μανθάνουσι** — *they learn*; **μανθάνεις** — *you learn*. Nothing
   else changes at all: the nouns are doing the same jobs for whoever the
   subject is.
9. **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας τοῦ κριτοῦ**: the preposition changes, and the case
   follows it, dative to genitive. **τοῦ κριτοῦ** does not change; it was
   genitive for its own reason.
10. **τοῦ κριτοῦ** — but **τοῦ** is genitive, and the verb is **-ομεν**,
    not **-ει**. Two pieces of evidence, both on the page, and the verb
    alone was enough.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team three"}
**κακοὶ οἱ λόγοι, ἀλλὰ ἀγαθὰ τὰ ἔργα τῶν μικρῶν θυγατέρων.**

Translate it, then defend it:

1. Give the translation.
2. **κακοὶ οἱ λόγοι** — where does **κακοί** stand, and is it describing
   or asserting?
3. How many verbs are there in the sentence? How is it a sentence at all?
4. **ἀγαθὰ τὰ ἔργα** — gender, number and case; and how do you know the
   case, when **τά** could be two?
5. **τῶν μικρῶν** — case; what does it agree with?
6. **θυγατέρων** has an ending you have not been taught. What can you say
   about it anyway, and from what evidence?
7. Rewrite the first half as **οἱ κακοὶ λόγοι**, translate, and say why
   it is no longer a sentence.
8. Put **κακοὶ οἱ λόγοι** into the singular.
9. Why **ἀγαθά** and not **ἀγαθοί**?
10. Another team translated it *the bad words, but the good deeds of the
    small daughters*. What have they missed, and what do they score?
:::

::: answers
1. *The words are bad, but the deeds of the little daughters are good.*
2. Outside the article group — in front of **οἱ**. Predicate position: it
   asserts. *The words are bad.*
3. None. Two assertions, each made by an adjective standing outside its
   article group, and Greek needs no verb for either; **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**
   is the model.
4. Neuter plural; nominative, because it is the subject of the assertion —
   nothing in the clause could act upon it, since there is no verb.
5. Genitive plural; it agrees with **θυγατέρων**, which it describes.
6. That it is a genitive plural: **τῶν** and **μικρῶν** in front of it say
   so, and so does the **-ων**, which is the one ending every declension
   shares. *I do not know this noun's endings but the article tells me its
   case* is the best possible answer, and the one the rule rewards.
7. *The bad words.* The adjective is now inside the group and describes;
   nothing is being claimed about the words, so there is a phrase and no
   sentence.
8. **κακὸς ὁ λόγος.** — *The word is bad.*
9. Because **τὰ ἔργα** is neuter, and the adjective agrees with its noun.
   **ἀγαθοί** would be looking for a masculine plural that is not there.
10. Both assertions: they have read both adjectives as if they stood inside
    their article groups, and produced two phrases and no sentence. They
    score almost everything if they can say precisely that — *we read*
    **κακοὶ οἱ** *as if it were* **οἱ κακοί** — and nothing if they cannot.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team four — into Greek"}
Compose, and then defend:

1. The good teacher teaches the sons.
2. We carry the books out of the house.
3. The gifts of the wise are small.
4. The pupils hear the words of the teacher.
5. The judge gives the letter to the son.
6. The road is long, but the days are few.
7. ***You*** *(one person)* are learning, but ***I*** am teaching.
8. The wise pupil is the measure of the words.
9. We do not find the name of the judge in the letter.
10. Now the cat is sleeping with the tablets.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ ἀγαθὸς διδάσκαλος διδάσκει τοὺς υἱούς.** — adjective inside the
   article group, because the English describes rather than asserts.
2. **φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.** — **ἐκ** demands the genitive.
3. **μικρὰ τὰ δῶρα τῶν σοφῶν.** — predicate position, no verb needed. A
   pupil who writes **τὰ μικρὰ δῶρα τῶν σοφῶν** has written *the small
   gifts of the wise*, which is a phrase and not a sentence, and should be
   asked to say what is missing.
4. **οἱ μαθηταὶ ἀκούουσι τοὺς λόγους τοῦ διδασκάλου.** — accusative for
   what is heard, genitive for whose it is.
5. **ὁ κριτὴς δίδωσι τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ υἱῷ.** — dative for the one it goes
   to; **τὸν υἱόν** is the error to expect.
6. **μακρὰ ἡ ὁδός, ἀλλὰ ὀλίγαι αἱ ἡμέραι.** — two assertions, no verb;
   **μακρά** feminine with the feminine **ὁδός**, whatever its ending looks
   like.
7. **σὺ μανθάνεις, ἀλλὰ ἐγὼ διδάσκω.** — both pronouns required, because
   the English stresses both; accept **σὺ μὲν μανθάνεις, ἐγὼ δὲ διδάσκω**
   from a pupil who has been reading ahead.
8. **ὁ σοφὸς μαθητής ἐστι τὸ μέτρον τῶν λόγων.** — the new word, in the
   nominative after **ἐστι**, with its article: *the* measure. Ask the
   team to say what **μέτρον** without the article would have meant.
9. **οὐχ εὑρίσκομεν τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ κριτοῦ ἐν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ.** — **οὐχ** before
   the rough breathing; genitive, then dative after **ἐν**.
10. **νῦν ὁ αἴλουρος καθεύδει σὺν ταῖς δέλτοις.** — **σύν** with the
    dative; **δέλτοις** is feminine, so **ταῖς**, not **τοῖς**.

The defence is the marked work here too. Ask for the gender, number and case
of every word before accepting any answer.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Protagoras, fr. 1 (as quoted by Sextus Empiricus); referred to by Plato, *Theaetetus* 152a"}
Here is a famous sentence, twice. The first version was written for you —
it is **adapted**, and you are told so.

**ὁ ἄνθρωπός ἐστι τὸ μέτρον τῶν ἔργων.**

*The man is the measure of the works.*

Every form in it is one you have had since Lesson 9, and the one new word
is in today's list. Translate it, defend it, and then look at what it cost.

Now the real one:

**πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος …**

- **πάντων χρημάτων** — of all things *(genitive plural)*
- **μέτρον** — measure *(no article)*
- **ἐστίν** — is
- **ἄνθρωπος** — man *(no article)*

*Man is the measure of all things.* (The sentence goes on — *of things that
are, that they are; of things that are not, that they are not* — and Plato,
reporting it, turns it into indirect speech: **μέτρον ἄνθρωπον εἶναι**.)

Five differences, and every one of them matters.

**The genitive has moved to the front.** **πάντων χρημάτων** is the first
thing you meet, and in Greek the first thing is the emphasised thing. The
sentence is not about man; it is about *everything*, and man turns out to be
the yardstick for it. The adapted version buried that at the end, in the
English order, and lost the emphasis entirely.

**The articles are gone.** **ἄνθρωπος**, not **ὁ ἄνθρωπος**. Greek would
normally say *man as such* with the article; without it the word can mean
*a man*, *each man*, or *man*, and readers have argued ever since about
which Protagoras meant. The adaptation's article quietly settled that
argument for you.

**μέτρον has no article either.** With neither noun marked, nothing but
order and sense tells you which is the subject; the sentence says *man is
measure*, or *a measure*, not *the measure*. The adapted version's **τὸ
μέτρον** says *the* measure, which is a stronger and different claim.

**And *all* has vanished.** **πάντων** has no counterpart in the adapted
sentence. *The works* is a bounded set; *all things* is not.

**The vocabulary was changed to suit you.** **τῶν ἔργων** is a word you
know. **χρημάτων** is not, and it does not mean *works*: it means things,
goods, matters — whatever there is.

So the adaptation was not a translation of the sentence into easier Greek.
It was a different sentence, and a smaller one.

::: note
**Was the adaptation dishonest?** It was labelled, which is the whole of the
difference. A composed sentence presented as composed is a teaching tool; a
composed sentence presented as Protagoras is a lie about the evidence, and
this course would rather you could not read at all than read that way.

You will meet adapted Greek in many textbooks. Most of it is not labelled.
Now you know what to ask.
:::
:::

The tenth tablet was written the evening after the teams, when the argument
about Protagoras had followed the class out of the room and down the
stairs. It closes Part II, and it is the first tablet that takes a side.

οἱ μαθηταὶ λέγουσι περὶ τοῦ μέτρου. ὁ Μᾶρκος λέγει· ὁ μαθητής ἐστι τὸ
μέτρον τοῦ λόγου. ἐγὼ οὐ γιγνώσκω τὸν λόγον, καὶ ὁ λόγος κακός.

ἡ Σαβῖνα λέγει· οὐχ ὁ μαθητής, ἀλλὰ ὁ κριτής ἐστι τὸ μέτρον, καὶ ἐγώ
εἰμι κριτής. ἡ γνώμη τοῦ κριτοῦ μέτρον ἐστίν.

ὁ Κόιντος λέγει· ὁ ἄνθρωπός ἐστι τὸ μέτρον τῶν ἔργων· οὕτως λέγει ὁ
σοφός. ἡ Ἰουλία λέγει· οὐ μέτρον ἡ γνώμη, ἀλλὰ ἡ ἀλήθεια.

ὁ αἴλουρος οὐ λέγει. τὸ μέτρον τοῦ αἰλούρου ἡ τράπεζα.

ἡ δέλτος λέγει· οἱ μαθηταὶ λέγουσιν, ἀλλὰ οὐ μανθάνουσιν. ὁ διδάσκαλος
διδάσκει καὶ γιγνώσκει τὴν ἀρχὴν τοῦ λόγου. ὁ διδάσκαλός ἐστι τὸ μέτρον
τοῦ λόγου, οὐχ ὁ μαθητής.

οἱ μαθηταὶ λέγουσιν· οὐ σοφὸς ὁ λόγος. ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγει· ἐγὼ τὸ
μέτρον, καὶ ὁ λόγος σοφός.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Μᾶρκος, ἡ Σαβῖνα, ὁ Κόιντος, ἡ Ἰουλία** — Marcus, Sabina, Quintus,
  Julia
- **ὁ λόγος** — here, *the lesson*: the word has stretched again
- **ὁ σοφός** — the wise man; Quintus means Protagoras, whom you have just
  met
- **οὕτως** — thus, so *(Lesson 7)*
- **μαθητής ἐστι, ἄνθρωπός ἐστι, διδάσκαλός ἐστι, μέτρον ἐστίν** — take
  the accents as printed; **ἐστί** leans on the word before it
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything. Then find the
sentence that is almost Protagoras.

1. What are the pupils talking about? Give the case of **τοῦ μέτρου** and
   say what put it there.
2. Translate Marcus's claim. Which noun is the subject, and which word
   tells you? Then translate his reason, and say what **ἐγώ** is doing.
3. **ὁ λόγος κακός** — a claim or a description? What has **λόγος** come
   to mean on this tablet?
4. Sabina moves the measure. From whom to whom, and what two words make
   the move? Why is she pleased with the result?
5. **ἡ γνώμη τοῦ κριτοῦ μέτρον ἐστίν.** Compare this with Protagoras's
   real sentence in The Ancient Voice. What has Sabina kept, and what has
   she dropped?
6. Quintus quotes the adapted Protagoras from The Ancient Voice. Quote him
   exactly, say who **ὁ σοφός** is, and say which of the two versions he
   has chosen and why that is a weakness in his case.
7. **οὐ μέτρον ἡ γνώμη, ἀλλὰ ἡ ἀλήθεια.** No verb: how is it a sentence?
   Which noun has no article, and what does that make it? Whom is Julia
   answering — Quintus, or Sabina?
8. What is the cat's measure? Translate the sentence, and say what is
   missing from it and why nothing needs to be supplied.
9. The tablet gives its verdict in three sentences. Translate them. Which
   two verbs are set against each other, and why does the tablet think the
   teacher is the measure?
10. Everyone on the tablet has a measure: Marcus, Sabina, Quintus, Julia,
    the cat, the teacher. List all six. Then say which one Protagoras
    would have agreed with, and whether the tablet does.
:::

::: answers
1. The measure, **περὶ τοῦ μέτρου** — genitive, after **περί** (Lesson 8).
2. *The pupil is the measure of the lesson.* **ὁ μαθητής** is the subject,
   because — as in Lesson 5 — both nouns have the article here, so the
   first is taken as subject; but **ἐστι** links, so **τὸ μέτρον** is
   nominative too. His reason: *I do not know the lesson, and the lesson
   is bad* — **ἐγώ** throws the weight onto himself: *I* do not know it,
   so it is bad. Which is the whole of his argument.
3. A claim: **κακός** stands outside the article group — *the lesson is
   bad*. **λόγος** has stretched from *word* to *the thing the teacher
   says*, the lesson; the gloss warns you.
4. From the pupil to the judge: **οὐχ ὁ μαθητής, ἀλλὰ ὁ κριτής**, with
   **οὐχ** denying the first and **ἀλλά** supplying the second. She is
   pleased because she then says **ἐγώ εἰμι κριτής** — *I am a judge*: the
   measure has moved to her.
5. She has kept **μέτρον ἐστίν** with no article on **μέτρον**, which is
   exactly Protagoras's shape — *is a measure*, not *the measure*. She has
   dropped *man* and *all things* and put in *the judge's opinion*, which
   is smaller and more convenient.
6. **ὁ ἄνθρωπός ἐστι τὸ μέτρον τῶν ἔργων** — word for word the adapted
   sentence. **ὁ σοφός** is Protagoras, *the wise man*, with the article
   making an adjective into a noun. Quintus has quoted the adaptation, not
   the original — *the works*, with articles on both nouns — so he is
   quoting the textbook and calling it the wise man, which The Ancient
   Voice has just warned the class about.
7. Two nouns and a negative, with the verb left out as Greek allows when
   **εἰμί** is meant. **μέτρον** has no article, so it is what is being
   said about the other two: *opinion is not a measure; truth is*. She is
   answering Sabina, whose measure was **ἡ γνώμη τοῦ κριτοῦ**; Quintus
   she ignores.
8. *The cat's measure is the table.* There is no verb; **ἐστί** is
   understood, as in the proverb. The cat says nothing, **οὐ λέγει**,
   which is the tablet's opinion of everyone else's opinion.
9. *The pupils talk, but do not learn. The teacher teaches and knows the
   beginning of the lesson. The teacher is the measure of the lesson, not
   the pupil.* **λέγουσιν** against **μανθάνουσιν** — talking against
   learning. The teacher is the measure because he knows **τὴν ἀρχήν**,
   the beginning — and, since Lesson 6, **ἀρχή** also means *rule*: the one
   who knows where a thing starts is the one who governs it.
10. Marcus: the pupil. Sabina: the judge, meaning herself. Quintus: man.
    Julia: truth. The cat: the table. The teacher: the teacher. Protagoras
    said man — Quintus's answer, and Marcus's if every pupil is a man. The
    tablet does not agree with him: it ends **ἐγὼ τὸ μέτρον**, which is one
    man, and the one holding the stylus. Whether that is a joke or a claim
    is for the class to decide, and Theodoros will not help them.
:::

   adaptation? Was the adaptation dishonest, or necessary, or both?
2. *Man is the measure of all things* has been read as a liberation and as a
   catastrophe. Which did Protagoras mean? Can the Greek tell you?
3. Theodoros gave all the marks for the defence and none for the result. Is

Sabina's team lost.

They had the translation exactly right and could not account for the dative,
and Theodoros, who had written the rule on the board himself an hour
earlier, gave them nothing and looked unhappy about it.

Livia's team won with a translation that had two mistakes in it, both of
which Livia identified, explained, and attributed to the correct endings
before anyone asked her to.

"That is not winning," said Sabina afterwards. "We were right."

"You were right," Livia agreed. "You did not know why."

Sabina thought about this for most of the walk home, and then went back the
