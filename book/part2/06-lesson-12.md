# The First Reading Challenge

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Four teams," said Theodoros. "Each team gets a sentence. You translate it,
and then you defend it."

"Defend it against what?" said Quintus.

"Against me."

He wrote the rules on the board, which he had never done before, and the
class understood from that alone that something had changed.

> **No marks for the translation.**
>
> **All the marks for the defence.**

"That is absurd," said Marcus. "The translation is the work."

"The translation is the *result*. Anyone can arrive at a result. Felix
arrives at results constantly." Felix bowed. "What I am buying today is the
reason. If your English is perfect and you cannot tell me which ending made
it so, you score nothing. If your English is wrong and you can show me
precisely which ending you misread and why, you score almost everything."

There was a silence while this was considered.

"So we can be wrong," said Livia slowly, "and still win."

"You can be wrong *usefully*. It is the only kind of wrong worth being."

Sabina's team got their sentence first. She read it, and then read it again,
and then said a word she had learned from the kitchen.

"It has four nouns in it."

"It has," Theodoros agreed.

"And only one of them is the subject."

"Yes."

"And you are not going to tell us which."

"I am going to ask you which," he said, "which is a different and much
better thing."
:::

**The four jobs**: nominative does; accusative receives; genitive owns or
comes from; dative is given to.

**The article as a knife**: adjective inside the group describes; outside it,
asserts.

## How to attack a sentence you have not seen

The method, in the order you should use it.

**One. Read it all.** Do not translate anything until you have reached the
end. The word that settles the sentence is often the last one.

**Two. Find the verb.** It tells you the person and number of the subject
before you have found the subject.

**Three. Find the nominative.** There will be one, or it will be hiding in
the verb ending. If you think you have found two, one of them is a
predicate.

**Four. Assign every other noun a job.** Accusative: it is being acted on.
Genitive: it belongs to something. Dative: something is coming to it.

**Five. Only now, build the English.** And then check it against the
endings, not against whether it sounds plausible.

::: note
Step five is where this course differs from most. A translation that sounds
right is not evidence of anything. The endings are the evidence, and they
are still on the page after you have produced your English — which means
you can always check, and are therefore always obliged to.
:::

**Learn the article perfectly.** Everything else in Greek can be looked up,
guessed at, or worked out from context. The article cannot: it is too short,
too common, and too similar to itself. A pupil who knows the twenty-four
forms of the article cold will read Part III comfortably, and a pupil who
does not will struggle with every sentence in it for reasons they will
blame on the vocabulary.

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
The four sentences Theodoros set. Translate each, and then defend every
ending in it.

::: {.exercise title="Team one"}
**οἱ σοφοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων φέρουσι τοῖς υἱοῖς τὰ καλὰ δῶρα.**
:::

::: answers
*The wise among the men bring the fine gifts to the sons.*

The defence, which is what is being marked:

- **οἱ σοφοί** — masculine nominative plural; the subject. **οἱ** and
  **-οι** agree.
- **τῶν ἀνθρώπων** — genitive plural, depending on **οἱ σοφοί**: *the wise
  of the men*, i.e. those among them who are wise.
- **φέρουσι** — third plural, agreeing with **οἱ σοφοί**.
- **τοῖς υἱοῖς** — dative plural; the ones the gifts go to.
- **τὰ καλὰ δῶρα** — neuter plural, and here accusative, because the
  nominative is already taken. **τά** alone cannot tell you; the rest of
  the sentence can.

A team that translates this correctly and cannot account for **τὰ καλὰ
δῶρα** scores nothing, by the rule.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team two"}
**ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ τοῦ κριτοῦ μανθάνομεν τοὺς λόγους τῆς ἀληθείας.**
:::

::: answers
*In the house of the judge we learn the words of truth.*

- **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — dative after **ἐν**; place. Iota subscript required.
- **τοῦ κριτοῦ** — genitive; whose house.
- **μανθάνομεν** — first plural; the subject is *we*, and is nowhere
  written.
- **τοὺς λόγους** — accusative plural; what is learned.
- **τῆς ἀληθείας** — genitive singular; *of truth*.

The sentence has no nominative at all. A team that reports this as an error
in the question should be told that they have made the single most
important observation of the afternoon.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team three"}
**κακοὶ οἱ λόγοι, ἀλλὰ ἀγαθὰ τὰ ἔργα τῶν μικρῶν θυγατέρων.**
:::

::: answers
*The words are bad, but the deeds of the little daughters are good.*

- **κακοὶ οἱ λόγοι** — predicate position: **κακοί** stands outside the
  article group, so it asserts. *The words are bad*, with no verb.
- **ἀγαθὰ τὰ ἔργα** — the same construction again.
- **τῶν μικρῶν θυγατέρων** — genitive plural; whose deeds. **θυγατέρων**
  is third declension and has not been taught: a team that says *I do not
  know this ending but the **τῶν** tells me it is a genitive plural* has
  given the best possible answer.

Two assertions and no verb in the entire sentence. This is the hardest of
the four and the one most worth discussing.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Team four — into Greek"}
Compose, and then defend:

1. The good teacher teaches the sons.
2. We carry the books out of the house.
3. The gifts of the wise are small.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ ἀγαθὸς διδάσκαλος διδάσκει τοὺς υἱούς.** — adjective inside the
   article group, because the English describes rather than asserts.
2. **φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.** — **ἐκ** demands the genitive.
3. **μικρὰ τὰ δῶρα τῶν σοφῶν.** — predicate position, no verb needed. A
   pupil who writes **τὰ μικρὰ δῶρα τῶν σοφῶν** has written *the small
   gifts of the wise*, which is a phrase and not a sentence, and should be
   asked to say what is missing.

The defence is the marked work here too. Ask for the gender, number and case
of every word before accepting any answer.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Protagoras, quoted by Plato, *Theaetetus* 152a"}
Here is a famous sentence, twice. The first version was written for you.

**ὁ ἄνθρωπός ἐστι τὸ μέτρον τῶν ἔργων.**

*The man is the measure of the works.*

Every form in it is one you have had since Lesson 9. Translate it, defend
it, and then look at what it cost.

Now the real one:

**πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος.**

- **πάντων χρημάτων** — of all things *(genitive plural)*
- **μέτρον** — measure *(no article)*
- **ἐστίν** — is
- **ἄνθρωπος** — man *(no article)*

*Man is the measure of all things.*

Four differences, and every one of them matters.

**The genitive has moved to the front.** **πάντων χρημάτων** is the first
thing you meet, and in Greek the first thing is the emphasised thing. The
sentence is not about man; it is about *everything*, and man turns out to be
the yardstick for it. The adapted version buried that at the end, in the
English order, and lost the emphasis entirely.

**The articles are gone.** **ἄνθρωπος**, not **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — not *the man*,
not any particular man, but man as such. The adaptation put an article in
because you had been taught articles, and in doing so it narrowed a claim
about humanity into a remark about somebody.

**μέτρον has no article either**, which by this lesson's rule makes it a
predicate: *man is a measure*, or *man is measure*. The adapted version's
**τὸ μέτρον** says *the* measure, which is a stronger and different claim.

**The vocabulary was changed to suit you.** **τῶν ἔργων** is a word you
know. **χρημάτων** is not, and it does not mean *works*: it means things,
goods, matters — whatever there is.

So the adaptation was not a translation of the sentence into easier Greek.
It was a different sentence, and a smaller one.

::: note
**Was the adaptation dishonest?** It was labelled, which is the whole of the
difference. A composed sentence presented as composed is a teaching tool; a
composed sentence presented as Protagoras is a lie about the evidence, and
this course would rather you could not read at all than read that way.

You will meet adapted Greek in many textbooks. Most of it is not labelled.
Now you know what to ask.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. You read an adapted sentence and then the real one. What was lost in the
   adaptation? Was the adaptation dishonest, or necessary, or both?
2. *Man is the measure of all things* has been read as a liberation and as a
   catastrophe. Which did Protagoras mean? Can the Greek tell you?
3. Theodoros gave all the marks for the defence and none for the result. Is
   that a fair way to mark? Would you want your other subjects marked that
   way?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Sabina's team lost.

They had the translation exactly right and could not account for the dative,
and Theodoros, who had written the rule on the board himself an hour
earlier, gave them nothing and looked unhappy about it.

Livia's team won with a translation that had two mistakes in it, both of
which Livia identified, explained, and attributed to the correct endings
before anyone asked her to.

"That is not winning," said Sabina afterwards. "We were right."

"You were right," Livia agreed. "You did not know why."

Sabina thought about this for most of the walk home, and then went back the
next morning and asked about the dative.
:::
