# The Article's Many Disguises

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"**ἡ λόγος**," said Marcus.

"**ὁ** λόγος," said Julia, without looking up.

"That is what I said."

"It is not what you said. You have been saying **ἡ λόγος** for a fortnight.
I have been counting."

Marcus turned round. "Why would you count that?"

"Because you are wrong every time, and nobody tells you, and it annoys me."

"It does not *matter*," said Marcus, with the air of a man producing a
long-held position. "It is a little word. It is decoration. The noun does
the work and the little word sits in front of it looking important. If I
leave it off entirely you still know what I mean."

Theodoros, who had been letting this run, stood up and wrote three words on
the board.

> **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**

"You saw this in your third week. Translate it."

"*Fine things are hard*," said Marcus. "We did it. It is a proverb."

"Good." Theodoros rubbed out nothing, moved nothing, and simply wrote the
same three words again underneath in a different order:

> **τὰ χαλεπὰ καλά**

"And this?"

Marcus looked at it for some time.

"*Hard things are fine*," said Livia quietly.

"Which is a different proverb," said Theodoros, "and arguably a better one.
The words are identical. Not one letter has changed. What moved?"

"The **τά**," said Marcus, and then, hearing himself: "...oh."

"The decoration," said Theodoros, "is the load-bearing wall."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The whole article, at last

You have been using five or six forms of the article. There are more, and they
are the most useful words in the language. Here is everything you need for
the next six lessons:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **nom. sing.** | **ὁ** | **ἡ** | **τό** |
| **acc. sing.** | **τόν** | **τήν** | **τό** |
| **nom. plur.** | **οἱ** | **αἱ** | **τά** |
| **acc. plur.** | **τούς** | **τάς** | **τά** |

Learn it as a shape, down the columns and across the rows, until you can say
it without thinking. Everything in Part II depends on it.

Three observations worth having now.

**Neuter never distinguishes subject from object** — **τό** in the singular,
**τά** in the plural, both cases. You met this in Lesson 3 and it remains
true forever.

**The masculine and feminine nominatives do not begin with τ.** **ὁ, ἡ**
and **οἱ, αἱ** are the odd ones out: they have a rough breathing and no
**τ**, while every other form has one. If you are ever lost, ask whether a
masculine or feminine form starts with **τ**. If it does, it is not a
nominative. (The neuter **τό**, **τά** begin with **τ** in every case —
that is the neuter rule again.)

**The article agrees with its noun and nothing else.** Not with the thing in
the world, not with the nearest word, not with what you expected. Gender,
number, case — all three, every time.

## Nouns in the plural

The nouns follow, in the two patterns you already half know:

| | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **ἡ οἰκία** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **nom. sing.** | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **ἡ οἰκία** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
| **acc. sing.** | **τὸν λόγον** | **τὴν ψυχήν** | **τὴν οἰκίαν** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
| **nom. plur.** | **οἱ λόγοι** | **αἱ ψυχαί** | **αἱ οἰκίαι** | **τὰ δῶρα** |
| **acc. plur.** | **τοὺς λόγους** | **τὰς ψυχάς** | **τὰς οἰκίας** | **τὰ δῶρα** |

The second declension (**λόγος**, **δῶρον**) and the first (**ψυχή**,
**οἰκία**) are the two you will meet most. The third arrives in Lesson 15 and
is worth not worrying about yet.

## Why ἄνθρωπος moves its accent

One oddity, and it has a rule behind it.

> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος** → **τοὺς ἀνθρώπους**

The accent has walked forward one syllable. It did not do this to annoy
you. Greek accents obey a single mechanical restriction:

**An accent cannot stand on the third syllable from the end if the last
syllable is long.**

**ἄν-θρω-πος** has a short last syllable, so the accent may sit three back.
**ἀν-θρώ-πους** has a long one — **-ους** — so the accent is forced forward
to the second. Nothing about the meaning has changed; the word is simply
obeying a rule about weight.

You will see this happen to many nouns. When it does, it is this rule, and
you need not learn each case separately.

::: latinbridge
Latin's accent obeys a very similar law — the accent never goes further back
than the third syllable, and the length of the penultimate syllable decides
where it lands. If you have ever been taught *penultimate long, accent on
the penult; penultimate short, accent on the antepenult*, you have already
met the idea.

Greek adds one twist Latin does not have: in Greek the length of the **last**
syllable also constrains the accent. That is the rule above, and it is the
only new thing here.
:::

## What the article is for

Marcus was wrong, but he was wrong in an interesting way. The article is not
doing what *the* does in English. It is doing four things at once, and the
fourth is the valuable one.

**It gives gender** — **ὁ** says masculine before you have seen the noun.

**It gives case** — **τούς** says accusative plural and nothing else.

**It makes a thing definite** — *the* book, not *a* book.

**And it marks which of two nouns is the subject.** This is the one that
decided Marcus's proverb, and it works because Greek is content to leave a
predicate bare. Where two nouns or two adjectives stand together and only
one has the article, the one with the article is what you are talking
*about*, and the other is what is being said about it.
:::

::: vocab
- **τὰ γράμματα** — letters; writing; literature
- **ὁ χρόνος** — time
- **ἡ ἡμέρα** — day
- **τὸ ἔργον** — work, deed, task
- **πολλοί** — many
- **μόνος** — only, alone

::: note
**τὰ γράμματα** is given in the plural because that is how it is normally
used. The word belongs to the third declension (**τὸ γράμμα**,
**γράμματος**), which you meet in Lesson 15; its plural happens to end in
**-α** and so goes with **τά**, which is why you can use it now. Until
Lesson 15, recognise it and leave it alone.

**πολλοί** and **μόνος** are adjectives, and adjectives have endings that
must agree with their noun. That is Lesson 10. For now take them in the
forms printed — and note **μόνον**, the neuter, which is used as an adverb,
*only*.

**τὰ γράμματα** is the Greek for *literature*, and literally means *the
letters*. A **γραμματικός** was, to begin with, simply a person who knew
their letters. English *grammar* and *glamour* are both descended from it,
by routes that are worth looking up when you have ten minutes.
:::
:::

Julia produced her list. It had fourteen entries on it and Marcus asked to
see it only once.

::: {.exercise title="Name all three"}
For each, give the gender, number, and case. Where a form could be more than
one thing, say so and say why.

1. **τοὺς λόγους**
2. **αἱ ἡμέραι**
3. **τὰ ἔργα**
4. **τὴν οἰκίαν**
5. **οἱ ἄνθρωποι**
6. **τὰς ψυχάς**
:::

::: answers
1. Masculine, plural, accusative.
2. Feminine, plural, nominative.
3. Neuter, plural — nominative *or* accusative. **τά** cannot distinguish
   them and neither can **-α**. Only the sentence can.
4. Feminine, singular, accusative.
5. Masculine, plural, nominative. Note the accent has stayed on the
   antepenult, because **-οι** is short.
6. Feminine, plural, accusative.

3 is the one to dwell on. A pupil who answers "neuter plural accusative"
without qualification has forgotten the rule from Lesson 3 and been lucky
rather than right.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **οἱ ἄνθρωποι γράφουσι τὰ γράμματα.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος βλέπει τοὺς λόγους.**
3. **αἱ ἡμέραι μόνον τὸν χρόνον ἔχουσιν.**
4. **τὰ ἔργα οὐ λέγει ἡ ψυχή, ἀλλὰ γιγνώσκει.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The men write the letters.* Both plural; **οἱ** and **τά** settle it.
2. *The teacher sees the words.*
3. *The days have only time.* — **μόνον** here is adverbial; accept *only
   the days have time* only if the pupil can defend it from the endings,
   which they cannot, because **μόνον** is neuter singular and does not
   agree with **αἱ ἡμέραι**; *only the days* would need **μόναι**.
4. *The soul does not speak the deeds, but it knows them.* **τὰ ἔργα** is
   fronted for emphasis and is the object throughout; **ἡ ψυχή** is the
   subject of both verbs although it is stated only once.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
Use the English-to-Greek list at the back for the words. You must supply the
article and the endings yourself.

1. The days.  *(subject)*
2. The works.  *(object)*
3. The men see the house.
4. The teacher writes the letters.
5. The souls have time.
:::

::: answers
1. **αἱ ἡμέραι**
2. **τὰ ἔργα**
3. **οἱ ἄνθρωποι βλέπουσι τὴν οἰκίαν.**
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος γράφει τὰ γράμματα.**
5. **αἱ ψυχαὶ ἔχουσι τὸν χρόνον.**

Three things to watch for. A singular verb after a plural subject (3, 5)
— the ending must move too. **τοὺς ἔργα**, which is an article and a noun
that do not agree in case; and **ὁ ἡμέραι**, which agrees in nothing at all.

A pupil who writes **τὰ ἔργα** for 2 and cannot say why it is not **τοὺς
ἔργα** should be sent back to the neuter rule rather than told the answer.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's defence"}
Marcus claimed the article was decoration, and produced **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**
and **τὰ χαλεπὰ καλά** as evidence that he was wrong.

Write one sentence of your own in which moving the article — and changing
nothing else — changes which noun is the subject. Then translate both
versions.
:::

::: answers
Answers will vary. The pattern to look for is two neuter plurals, or a noun
and an adjective, with the article attached first to one and then to the
other.

A pupil who cannot produce one has usually not understood that the article
is what makes a word the subject here, rather than its position. Give them
**καλὰ τὰ χαλεπά** / **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά** and ask what changed.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="A proverb, quoted by Plato, *Republic* 435c"}
You met this in Lesson 3, when you could translate it and not explain it.

**χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**

- **χαλεπά** — difficult, hard, harsh *(neuter plural)*
- **τὰ καλά** — the fine things, the beautiful things, the noble things
  *(neuter plural)*

*Fine things are hard.*

Now say why.

Both words are neuter plural. Neither ending distinguishes subject from
anything else — you established that this afternoon, in exercise 1, question
3. The two words are grammatically interchangeable and the sentence should
be unreadable.

It is not unreadable, and the reason is two letters long. **τὰ καλά**
carries the article, so **τὰ καλά** is the thing being talked about.
**χαλεπά** does not, so **χαλεπά** is what is being said about it.

Reverse the article and you reverse the proverb, which is exactly what
Theodoros did to Marcus this afternoon:

**τὰ χαλεπὰ καλά** — *hard things are fine.*

Plato has Glaucon quote the first version to Socrates as a saying already
current, when Socrates has just called their inquiry a trivial one — and
Glaucon suspects it is anything but. Plato does not explain it. He did not need to: every reader he had could see the article
doing its work, in the same half-second you now can.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Latin manages without an article for a thousand years of literature.
   What is Greek buying with it, and is the price worth paying?
2. *Fine things are hard* and *hard things are fine* are both proverbs
   people have lived by. Which is the more dangerous?
3. Marcus said the article was decoration and that you would still know what
   he meant. He was right about most sentences and wrong about this one. Is
   a rule you only need occasionally still a rule worth learning?
:::
