# One Verb, Several Clues

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros handed out a sheet with five sentences on it. Every one of them
was missing its subject.

> **... φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.**
>
> **... ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους.**
>
> **... μανθάνετε τὰ γράμματα.**

"Who is doing these things?"

Marcus put his hand up before he had read to the bottom.

"This cannot be done. You have removed the subject. You have removed the
thing the question is asking for. It is like asking who stole something
after burning the evidence."

"Answer the first one."

"*We* carry the books. Obviously **we**, it says **φέρομεν** —" He stopped.

The room waited.

"That is one," said Livia.

"It is a trick. The ending is doing it."

"That is four," said Livia, "because you did the next three out loud while
you were objecting."

Marcus looked at his sheet. He had, in fact, written *you*, *he* and *they*
beside three of them without noticing.

"Then why," he said, with the air of a man changing position while
pretending not to, "does anyone ever write **ἐγώ** at all? If the ending
already says it?"

Theodoros sat down, which the class had learned to recognise.

"That," he said, "is the first genuinely good question anybody has asked
this term."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The six endings, drilled

You met these in Lesson 5. Here they are again, because everything in Part
III depends on knowing them without stopping to think:

| | **λύω** *I loose* | **ἀκούω** *I hear* | **φέρω** *I carry* |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύω** | **ἀκούω** | **φέρω** |
| 2 sing. | **λύεις** | **ἀκούεις** | **φέρεις** |
| 3 sing. | **λύει** | **ἀκούει** | **φέρει** |
| 1 plur. | **λύομεν** | **ἀκούομεν** | **φέρομεν** |
| 2 plur. | **λύετε** | **ἀκούετε** | **φέρετε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύουσι(ν)** | **ἀκούουσι(ν)** | **φέρουσι(ν)** |

The stem changes; the endings do not. **-ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε,
-ουσι(ν)** is the whole of the present active, and it will carry you through
several hundred verbs.

**λύω** is the verb grammars drill on, because it is short and entirely
regular. You will meet it in every Greek book you ever open.

## Verbs that contract

Some verbs have a stem ending in a vowel, and when that vowel meets the
ending's vowel the two fuse. These are **contract verbs**, and they look
alarming and are not.

**ἀγαπάω**, *I love*, has a stem in **-α**, so **ἀγαπά-ω** becomes
**ἀγαπῶ**:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἀγαπῶ** |
| 2 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷς** |
| 3 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷ** |
| 1 plur. | **ἀγαπῶμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἀγαπᾶτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἀγαπῶσι(ν)** |

The endings underneath are the same six you have just drilled. Only the
surface has changed, because two vowels have run into each other and come
out as one long one. The circumflex is the scar.

You are not expected to produce these yet. You are expected to recognise
that **ἀγαπῶμεν** is a first person plural, because the **-μεν** is still
visible, and to stop being frightened of the accent.

::: note
**Dictionaries list contract verbs uncontracted.** You will find **ἀγαπάω**
in the vocabulary and **ἀγαπῶ** in the text, and they are the same word. The
dictionary shows you the machinery; the text shows you the result.
:::

## Marcus's question

So why write **ἐγώ**?

Because the ending gives you the subject for free, and therefore a writer
who spends a word on it is spending it on something else: **contrast**.

> **λέγω** — I say.
>
> **ἐγὼ λέγω** — ***I*** say. As against you. As against him. As against
> what you were told.

The pronoun is not information. It is a raised voice. When you meet one, do
not translate it as *I* and move on — ask who is being argued with, because
somebody is.

The same is true of **σύ** (*you*), and it will be true of every pronoun you
meet for the rest of the course.

::: latinbridge
Latin does this too, and your Latin master almost certainly told you that
*ego* is "unnecessary". That was a simplification.

*Ego dico* is not bad Latin for *dico*; it is *I* say, in contrast to
somebody else. Cicero uses it exactly where an English speaker would lean on
the word. Greek merely does it more often, and with more verbs, and expects
you to notice.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἀκούω** — I hear, listen to
- **φέρω** — I carry, bear, bring
- **μανθάνω** — I learn, understand
- **διδάσκω** — I teach
- **ἐθέλω** — I am willing, I consent
- **σύ** — you *(one person; emphatic, like **ἐγώ**)*
- **νῦν** — now
- **πράττω** — I do, act; I fare *(with **καλῶς** or **κακῶς**: I do
  well, I do badly)*
- **κλέπτω** — I steal
- **σπεύδω** — I hurry, am eager
- **ὁ πόνος** — toil, hard work; trouble
- **καλῶς** — well, finely
- **κακῶς** — badly

::: note
**ἀκούω** gives English *acoustic*. **μανθάνω** is behind *mathematics* —
which began as simply *the things learned*, and acquired its narrower
meaning later.

**διδάσκω** and **ὁ διδάσκαλος** are the same root, which you may have
suspected since Lesson 3. A **διδάσκαλος** is one who **διδάσκει**.

**ἐθέλω** is *I am willing*, not *I want*. The difference matters in
tragedy, where people are frequently willing to do things they do not want
to do.

**πράττω** is the verb behind *practical* and *practice*, and it is the
verb of this lesson's question — *who is doing it?* — so it is on the
ninth tablet. **κλέπτω** is there because Marcus mentioned stealing, and
because the cat does. **καλῶς** and **κακῶς** are **καλός** and **κακός**
turned into adverbs by the ending **-ως**: most adjectives will do this,
and you may treat **-ως** as English *-ly* until told otherwise.
:::
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
Ten quick questions from Lessons 7 to 10. From memory.

1. Give the gender, number and case of **τὰς δέλτους**.
2. Translate **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας**.
3. **ἡ σοφὴ θυγάτηρ** or **σοφὴ ἡ θυγάτηρ** — which one makes a claim?
4. Put **τὸ ἔργον** into the nominative plural, and say what is odd about
   the verb it takes.
5. Translate **δίδωσι τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ κριτῇ**.
6. What case follows **σύν**, and what does it mean?
7. **τοῦ υἱοῦ** — case, and translation.
8. Translate **μικρὰ ἡ ψυχή**.
9. Put **ὁ μαθητὴς γράφει τὸ γράμμα** into the plural throughout.
10. Of **ἐκ**, **ἐν**, **περί** and **πρός**, which takes the dative?
:::

::: answers
1. Feminine, plural, accusative — feminine despite the **-ους**, because
   **δέλτος** goes like **ὁδός**.
2. *Out of the house.*
3. The second: **σοφή** stands outside the article group and asserts —
   *the daughter is wise*. The first is a label, *the wise daughter*.
4. **τὰ ἔργα**; a neuter plural subject takes a *singular* verb.
5. *He, she or it gives the letter to the judge.*
6. The dative; *together with*.
7. Genitive — *of the son*, *the son's*.
8. *The soul is small.* A pupil who wrote *the small soul* has put the
   article where it is not.
9. **οἱ μαθηταὶ γράφουσι τὰ γράμματα.** Five changes; the verb is the one
   most often left behind.
10. **ἐν**. The other three take the genitive, the genitive, and the
    accusative.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Who is acting?"}
Give the person and number, and translate. No subject is supplied and none
is needed.

1. **φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.**
2. **ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους.**
3. **μανθάνετε τὰ γράμματα.**
4. **διδάσκουσι τοὺς υἱούς.**
5. **ἐθέλω μανθάνειν.** *(**μανθάνειν** = to learn)*
6. **βλέπεις τὸν αἴλουρον.**
7. **φέρετε τὰ δῶρα τῷ κριτῇ.**
8. **ἀκούουσι τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
9. **κλέπτει τὰ δῶρα.**
10. **διδάσκει τοὺς μαθητὰς τὰ γράμματα.**
:::

::: answers
1. 1st plural — *we carry the books.*
2. 3rd singular — *he, she, or it hears the words.*
3. 2nd plural — *you (all) learn the letters.*
4. 3rd plural — *they teach the sons.*
5. 1st singular — *I am willing to learn.* The second verb is an
   infinitive, which arrives properly in Part III; take it whole.
6. 2nd singular — *you see the cat.*
7. 2nd plural — *you (all) bring the gifts to the judge.* **τῷ κριτῇ** is
   the dative, the one the gifts go to.
8. 3rd plural — *they hear the teacher's voice.* The **-ν** on **ἀκούουσιν**
   is missing because the next word begins with a consonant.
9. 3rd singular — *he, she or it steals the gifts.* The class will know
   who; the sentence does not say.
10. 3rd singular — *he, she or it teaches the pupils the letters.* Two
    accusatives: **διδάσκω** takes one for the person taught and one for
    the thing taught, exactly as English *teach the pupils the letters*
    does. A pupil who tries to make one of them a dative has reasoned
    well and is wrong.

In 2, a pupil who insists on *he* rather than *he, she or it* should be
asked what in the sentence tells them the subject is male. Nothing does.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **νῦν μανθάνομεν τὰ τῶν σοφῶν ἔργα.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος διδάσκει τοὺς ἀγαθοὺς υἱούς.**
3. **οὐκ ἀκούετε τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τοῦ κριτοῦ.**
4. **ἐγὼ φέρω τὰ δῶρα, ἀλλὰ σὺ οὐκ ἐθέλεις.**
5. **σὺ ἀκούεις, ἀλλὰ ἐγὼ μανθάνω.**
6. **οἱ μαθηταὶ φέρουσι τὰς δέλτους ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.**
7. **ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους τοῦ διδασκάλου, ἀλλὰ καθεύδει.**
8. **νῦν διδάσκεις, ὦ Μᾶρκε, καὶ οἱ μαθηταὶ μανθάνουσιν.**
9. **σπεύδετε, ἀλλὰ κακῶς πράττετε· μέγας ὁ πόνος.**
10. **ὁ σοφὸς διδάσκαλος οὐ πολλοὺς λόγους λέγει, ἀλλὰ καλῶς διδάσκει.**

*(Composed for this lesson. These are not quotations.)*
:::

::: answers
1. *Now we learn the works of the wise.* — **τὰ τῶν σοφῶν ἔργα** has the
   genitive tucked inside the article group, which Greek does constantly
   and English cannot copy.
2. *The teacher teaches the good sons.*
3. *You do not hear the judge's letter.*
4. *I carry the gifts, but you are not willing.* — Both pronouns are
   present and both are doing work: the sentence is a contrast, and the
   **ἀλλά** confirms it. A translation that drops the pronouns loses the
   argument the sentence is having.
5. *You hear, but I learn.* — The same shape as 4, the other way round;
   whoever says it is claiming to be the better pupil.
6. *The pupils carry the tablets out of the house.* — **ἐκ** with the
   genitive, Lesson 8.
7. *The cat does not listen to the teacher's words, but sleeps.* — The
   subject of **καθεύδει** is still the cat; the **-ει** carries it
   across the **ἀλλά**.
8. *Now you are teaching, Marcus, and the pupils are learning.* — The
   vocative tells you who the **-εις** is; without it the sentence would
   be addressed to whoever was reading it.
9. *You are hurrying, but you are doing badly: the toil is great.* — Three
   clauses; the last has no verb, and **μέγας** outside the article group
   asserts, as in Lesson 10. **κακῶς πράττετε** is the idiom for *faring
   badly* as well as *doing badly*, and here it is both.
10. *The wise teacher does not say many words, but teaches well.* — **οὐ**
    stands in front of **πολλούς**, not the verb: it is the *many* that is
    denied, not the saying.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. We hear the words.
2. They teach the sons.
3. You *(one person)* are learning the letters.
4. Now I carry the gifts.
5. ***I*** teach, but ***you*** learn.  *(the stress is on both)*
6. You *(more than one)* carry the tablets.
7. The cat does not listen to the teacher's words.
8. The cat is stealing the gifts of the judge.
9. Now the pupils are learning well.
10. We are not willing, but we hurry.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἀκούομεν τοὺς λόγους.**
2. **διδάσκουσι τοὺς υἱούς.**
3. **μανθάνεις τὰ γράμματα.**
4. **νῦν φέρω τὰ δῶρα.**
5. **ἐγὼ μὲν διδάσκω, σὺ δὲ μανθάνεις.** — accept **ἐγὼ διδάσκω, ἀλλὰ σὺ
   μανθάνεις** without comment; **μέν … δέ** is the idiomatic pair for a
   contrast like this and arrives in Part III. The point being tested is
   that both pronouns must be there, because the English stresses both.
6. **φέρετε τὰς δέλτους.** — One word for *you carry*; a pupil who wants a
   word for *you* has only **σύ**, which is singular and emphatic, and
   should be told that the plural pronoun is not theirs yet.
7. **ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
8. **ὁ αἴλουρος κλέπτει τὰ δῶρα τοῦ κριτοῦ.**
9. **νῦν οἱ μαθηταὶ καλῶς μανθάνουσιν.** — **-ν** on the verb, because it
   stands at the end.
10. **οὐκ ἐθέλομεν, ἀλλὰ σπεύδομεν.** — Two first plurals and no pronoun;
    the English *we* is in the endings twice over.

In 1–4 and 6–10 a supplied pronoun is an error of emphasis. In 5 its
absence is.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's question"}
Marcus asked why anyone writes **ἐγώ** if the ending already says it.

1. Give the best answer you can in one sentence.
2. Write a pair of Greek sentences, one with the pronoun and one without,
   and translate both so that the difference shows in English.
3. English always has to supply *I*. Does that mean English can never make
   the emphasis Greek makes? How does it manage?
4. **σὺ λέγεις** against **λέγεις**. Translate both so that the difference
   shows, and say who **σύ** is being set against.
5. Write *we carry the books* in Greek. Then say what you would have to do
   to stress the *we* — and what you cannot do yet.
6. Theodoros's sheet had no subjects. Marcus said that made it impossible.
   What exactly had the sheet left out, and what had it kept?
7. In **ἐγὼ φέρω τὰ δῶρα, ἀλλὰ σὺ οὐκ ἐθέλεις**, which word confirms that
   the two pronouns are there for contrast?
8. Could a Greek write **ἐγὼ φέρομεν**? Say why not.
9. Marcus wrote *you*, *he* and *they* beside three sentences without
   noticing. Which endings had he read in order to do that?
10. **ὁ Μᾶρκος λέγει** has a subject written out, and nobody calls that
    emphasis. What is the difference between a noun as subject and a
    pronoun as subject?
:::

::: answers
1. Because the ending supplies the subject, so the pronoun is free to supply
   something else, and what it supplies is contrast.
2. Answers will vary. **λέγω τὴν ἀλήθειαν** / **ἐγὼ λέγω τὴν ἀλήθειαν** —
   *I tell the truth* / *I tell the truth* (as against whoever does not).
3. English uses stress in speech and italics in writing, and in both cases
   it is adding something outside the words. Greek does it with a word.
   Worth five minutes: which method survives being copied out by hand?
4. *You say so* against *you say so* — or *it is you who say so*. Against
   the speaker, or whoever else might have said it; the point is that
   somebody else is in view.
5. **φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.** To stress *we* you would need a word for *we*
   standing in front, as **ἐγώ** does for *I*; the course has not given
   it yet (it comes in Part IV), so for now the ending is all the *we*
   there is.
6. It had left out the nouns and pronouns — the *words* for the subject.
   It had kept the endings, which is where the subject actually lives. A
   pupil who says the sheet left out nothing has understood the lesson.
7. **ἀλλά**. It announces that the second half is set against the first,
   and the pronouns then say what is being set against what.
8. No. **ἐγώ** is singular and **-ομεν** is plural; a pronoun must agree
   with its verb in person and number, because it is restating the subject
   the ending already names. A mismatch is not emphasis, it is a mistake.
9. **-ετε** (or **-εις**) for *you*, **-ει** for *he*, **-ουσι** for
   *they*. He read the endings while denying that they could be read.
10. The noun is information: **-ει** says *he, she or it*, and only
    **ὁ Μᾶρκος** can say *which* he. The pronoun is not information,
    because **-ω** already says *I* and nothing but *I*; so when it
    appears, it must be there for something else. Nouns tell you who;
    pronouns tell you against whom.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 5:44"}
A sentence in which the pronoun is doing exactly what Marcus asked about.

**ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν· ἀγαπᾶτε τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν …**

*(The verse goes on: and pray for those who persecute you.)*

- **ἐγώ** — I *(emphatic, and the point of the sentence)*
- **δέ** — but, and *(a connecting word; it never comes first)*
- **λέγω** — I say *(your verb, first person singular)*
- **ὑμῖν** — to you *(dative plural — Lesson 9)*
- **ἀγαπᾶτε** — love! *(a contract verb, and a command — Lesson 26)*
- **τοὺς ἐχθρούς** — the enemies *(accusative plural)*
- **ὑμῶν** — of you, your *(genitive plural — Lesson 8)*

*But I say to you, love your enemies.*

**λέγω** on its own would have been *I say*. The speaker has added **ἐγώ**,
and the whole force of the line is in that one unnecessary word. It is
unnecessary grammatically and indispensable rhetorically, because the
sentence it belongs to is one half of a contrast: *you have heard that it
was said …* — **ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν**.

Marcus asked why anyone would write **ἐγώ**. This is why. Somebody is being
argued with.

Notice also **ἀγαπᾶτε**, with its circumflex. That is the contract verb from
this lesson, in a form you cannot make yet. You can see the **-τε** and say
*second person plural*, which is most of the way there.

::: note
**δέ** is one of the small words that hold Greek prose together, and it has
a rule: it never stands first in its clause. It comes second, leaning on
the word in front of it. You will see **δέ** in almost every sentence of
Part III, and from now on you should expect the first word of a Greek clause
to be the important one.
:::
:::

The ninth tablet was the sheet again, with the class's answers written in,
and the class did not come out of it well.

ὁ διδάσκαλος γράφει ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ λόγους, ἀλλὰ τὰ ὀνόματα οὐ γράφει. λέγει
τοῖς μαθηταῖς· τίς πράττει; ἀκούετε καὶ μανθάνετε.

«φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.» ὁ Μᾶρκος λέγει· οὐ γιγνώσκομεν· τὸ ὄνομα οὐκ ἔστιν
ἐνταῦθα. ἡ Λιβία λέγει· φέρομεν. τὸ ὄνομα οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ, ἀλλὰ ἐν
τῷ λόγῳ.

«ἀκούεις τοὺς λόγους, ἀλλὰ οὐ μανθάνεις.» ὁ Φῆλιξ λέγει· σύ, ὦ Μᾶρκε. ὁ
Μᾶρκος λέγει· οὐκ ἐγώ. οἱ ἄλλοι μαθηταὶ λέγουσιν· ναί, σύ.

«κλέπτει τὰ δῶρα καὶ καθεύδει.» πολλοὶ λέγουσιν· ὁ αἴλουρος. ναί·
κλέπτει καὶ καθεύδει, καὶ οὐ σπεύδει.

«οὐκ ἐθέλετε μανθάνειν· κακῶς πράττετε.» οἱ μαθηταὶ οὐ λέγουσιν· μέγας ὁ
πόνος.

«διδάσκω τοὺς μαθητὰς τὰ γράμματα καὶ καλῶς πράττω.» ὁ Κόιντος λέγει· σὺ
λέγεις, ὦ διδάσκαλε. ἐγώ, λέγει ὁ διδάσκαλος· καὶ νῦν καλῶς μανθάνετε.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Μᾶρκος, ἡ Λιβία, ὁ Φῆλιξ, ὁ Κόιντος** — Marcus, Livia, Felix,
  Quintus
- **« »** — the sentences on the sheet, as the teacher wrote them; the
  rest is what the class said about them
- **τίς;** — who? *(a question word; Lesson 25)*
- **οὐκ ἔστιν** — is not *(**ἐστίν** after **οὐκ** takes the accent on
  its first syllable; take it as printed)*
- **μανθάνειν** — to learn *(the infinitive, as in the first exercise)*
- **διδάσκω τοὺς μαθητὰς τὰ γράμματα** — two accusatives, the person and
  the thing: *I teach the pupils the letters*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything. Five sentences on
the sheet; the rest is the class.

1. What does the teacher write, and what does he leave out? Which two
   verbs does he tell the class to do about it?
2. Marcus says the first sentence cannot be answered. Translate his reason.
   Then translate Livia's two-word answer, and say what is clever about it.
3. **τὸ ὄνομα οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ, ἀλλὰ ἐν τῷ λόγῳ.** Where does Livia
   say the name is? What does she mean by it?
4. Who is the second sentence about, according to Felix? What in the
   sentence lets him say so, and what does Marcus answer?
5. **οὐκ ἐγώ** — translate it, and say why **ἐγώ** is there at all.
6. The third sentence has a subject nobody has to guess. Who, and what
   three things does the tablet say it does and does not do?
7. **οὐκ ἐθέλετε μανθάνειν· κακῶς πράττετε.** Who is being spoken to?
   Give the person and number of both verbs, and translate.
8. **μέγας ὁ πόνος** — is this a description or a claim? Whose toil, and
   is the tablet being kind?
9. The last sentence of the sheet is the only one in the first person.
   Who does Quintus say it is about, and how does he say it? What one word
   does the teacher answer with?
10. Go through the five sentences on the sheet and list the person and
    number of each verb. Which person is missing, and who in the story
    might it have been about?
:::

::: answers
1. Sentences — **λόγους** — on the tablet, and leaves out the names,
   **τὰ ὀνόματα**. Listen and learn: **ἀκούετε καὶ μανθάνετε**, both
   second plural.
2. *We do not know: the name is not here.* Livia says **φέρομεν** — *we
   carry* — which is the sentence's own verb handed back as the answer.
   The ending *is* the subject, so the verb answers the question by
   itself.
3. Not on the tablet, but *in the word*, **ἐν τῷ λόγῳ**: inside the verb,
   in its ending. **λόγος** is doing double duty — the word on the sheet,
   and the word that holds the name.
4. Marcus: **σύ, ὦ Μᾶρκε** — *you, Marcus*. The **-εις** of **ἀκούεις**
   and **μανθάνεις** says *you (one person)*, and Felix has decided which
   one. Marcus answers **οὐκ ἐγώ** — *not I*; the other pupils answer
   **ναί, σύ**.
5. *Not I.* There is no verb to carry the *I*, so the pronoun has to; and
   it is a contrast anyway — not *me*, somebody else — which is what
   **ἐγώ** is for.
6. The cat: **ὁ αἴλουρος**. It steals, **κλέπτει**, and sleeps,
   **καθεύδει**, and does not hurry, **οὐ σπεύδει**. Nobody had to guess
   because nobody else in the room does all three.
7. The pupils — the whole class. **ἐθέλετε** and **πράττετε**, both second
   plural: *you are not willing to learn; you are doing badly.*
8. A claim: **μέγας** stands outside the article group — *the toil is
   great*. The pupils' toil, since they have just fallen silent. The tablet
   is not being kind; it is saying that silence is hard work.
9. The teacher: **σὺ λέγεις, ὦ διδάσκαλε** — *you are saying it, teacher*
   — with the vocative to show who **σύ** is. The teacher answers **ἐγώ**,
   *I*: one word, because the verb was already on the sheet.
10. **φέρομεν** 1st plural; **ἀκούεις, μανθάνεις** 2nd singular;
    **κλέπτει, καθεύδει** 3rd singular; **ἐθέλετε, πράττετε** 2nd plural;
    **διδάσκω, πράττω** 1st singular. Missing: the 3rd plural, *they*.
    Accept any sensible guess — the other pupils, who say **ναί, σύ**; or
    the slaves; or the cats of Rome.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. If the verb ending already tells you the subject, why would a Greek
   writer ever add **ἐγώ** or **σύ**? What are they doing when they do?
2. Marcus answered four questions correctly while arguing that they could
   not be answered. What does that suggest about the difference between
   knowing something and believing you know it?
3. **ἀγαπᾶτε τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν** is a command. Can a feeling be commanded?
   Does the Greek word help?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Marcus kept the sheet.

Years later, teaching a class of his own in a province he had not expected
to end up in, he set the same exercise, with the subjects removed, and
waited for somebody to tell him it could not be done.

Somebody did, in the second week. He said it was the first genuinely good
question anybody had asked that term, and then had to go and stand by the
window for a moment.
:::
