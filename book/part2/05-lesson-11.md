# One Verb, Several Clues

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros handed out a sheet with five sentences on it. Every one of them
was missing its subject.

> **... φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.**
>
> **... ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους.**
>
> **... μανθάνετε τὰ γράμματα.**

"Who is doing these things?"

Marcus put his hand up before he had read to the bottom.

"This cannot be done. You have removed the subject. You have removed the
thing the question is asking for. It is like asking who stole something
after burning the evidence."

"Answer the first one."

"*We* carry the books. Obviously **we**, it says **φέρομεν** —" He stopped.

The room waited.

"That is one," said Livia.

"It is a trick. The ending is doing it."

"That is four," said Livia, "because you did the next three out loud while
you were objecting."

Marcus looked at his sheet. He had, in fact, written *you*, *he* and *they*
beside three of them without noticing.

"Then why," he said, with the air of a man changing position while
pretending not to, "does anyone ever write **ἐγώ** at all? If the ending
already says it?"

Theodoros sat down, which the class had learned to recognise.

"That," he said, "is the first genuinely good question anybody has asked
this term."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The six endings, drilled

You met these in Lesson 5. Here they are again, because everything in Part
III depends on knowing them without stopping to think:

| | **λύω** *I loose* | **ἀκούω** *I hear* | **φέρω** *I carry* |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύω** | **ἀκούω** | **φέρω** |
| 2 sing. | **λύεις** | **ἀκούεις** | **φέρεις** |
| 3 sing. | **λύει** | **ἀκούει** | **φέρει** |
| 1 plur. | **λύομεν** | **ἀκούομεν** | **φέρομεν** |
| 2 plur. | **λύετε** | **ἀκούετε** | **φέρετε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύουσι(ν)** | **ἀκούουσι(ν)** | **φέρουσι(ν)** |

The stem changes; the endings do not. **-ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε,
-ουσι(ν)** is the whole of the present active, and it will carry you through
several hundred verbs.

**λύω** is the verb grammars drill on, because it is short and entirely
regular. You will meet it in every Greek book you ever open.

## Verbs that contract

Some verbs have a stem ending in a vowel, and when that vowel meets the
ending's vowel the two fuse. These are **contract verbs**, and they look
alarming and are not.

**ἀγαπάω**, *I love*, has a stem in **-α**, so **ἀγαπά-ω** becomes
**ἀγαπῶ**:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἀγαπῶ** |
| 2 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷς** |
| 3 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷ** |
| 1 plur. | **ἀγαπῶμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἀγαπᾶτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἀγαπῶσι(ν)** |

The endings underneath are the same six you have just drilled. Only the
surface has changed, because two vowels have run into each other and come
out as one long one. The circumflex is the scar.

You are not expected to produce these yet. You are expected to recognise
that **ἀγαπῶμεν** is a first person plural, because the **-μεν** is still
visible, and to stop being frightened of the accent.

::: note
**Dictionaries list contract verbs uncontracted.** You will find **ἀγαπάω**
in the vocabulary and **ἀγαπῶ** in the text, and they are the same word. The
dictionary shows you the machinery; the text shows you the result.
:::

## Marcus's question

So why write **ἐγώ**?

Because the ending gives you the subject for free, and therefore a writer
who spends a word on it is spending it on something else: **contrast**.

> **λέγω** — I say.
>
> **ἐγὼ λέγω** — ***I*** say. As against you. As against him. As against
> what you were told.

The pronoun is not information. It is a raised voice. When you meet one, do
not translate it as *I* and move on — ask who is being argued with, because
somebody is.

The same is true of **σύ** (*you*), and it will be true of every pronoun you
meet for the rest of the course.

::: latinbridge
Latin does this too, and your Latin master almost certainly told you that
*ego* is "unnecessary". That was a simplification.

*Ego dico* is not bad Latin for *dico*; it is *I* say, in contrast to
somebody else. Cicero uses it exactly where an English speaker would lean on
the word. Greek merely does it more often, and with more verbs, and expects
you to notice.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἀκούω** — I hear, listen to
- **φέρω** — I carry, bear, bring
- **μανθάνω** — I learn, understand
- **διδάσκω** — I teach
- **ἐθέλω** — I am willing, I consent
- **σύ** — you *(one person; emphatic, like **ἐγώ**)*
- **νῦν** — now

::: note
**ἀκούω** gives English *acoustic*. **μανθάνω** is behind *mathematics* —
which began as simply *the things learned*, and acquired its narrower
meaning later.

**διδάσκω** and **ὁ διδάσκαλος** are the same root, which you may have
suspected since Lesson 3. A **διδάσκαλος** is one who **διδάσκει**.

**ἐθέλω** is *I am willing*, not *I want*. The difference matters in
tragedy, where people are frequently willing to do things they do not want
to do.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros gave out the rest of the sheet.

::: {.exercise title="Who is acting?"}
Give the person and number, and translate. No subject is supplied and none
is needed.

1. **φέρομεν τὰ βιβλία.**
2. **ἀκούει τοὺς λόγους.**
3. **μανθάνετε τὰ γράμματα.**
4. **διδάσκουσι τοὺς υἱούς.**
5. **ἐθέλω μανθάνειν.** *(**μανθάνειν** = to learn)*
:::

::: answers
1. 1st plural — *we carry the books.*
2. 3rd singular — *he, she, or it hears the words.*
3. 2nd plural — *you (all) learn the letters.*
4. 3rd plural — *they teach the sons.*
5. 1st singular — *I am willing to learn.* The second verb is an
   infinitive, which arrives properly in Part III; take it whole.

In 2, a pupil who insists on *he* rather than *he, she or it* should be
asked what in the sentence tells them the subject is male. Nothing does.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **νῦν μανθάνομεν τὰ τῶν σοφῶν ἔργα.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος διδάσκει τοὺς ἀγαθοὺς υἱούς.**
3. **οὐκ ἀκούετε τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τοῦ κριτοῦ.**
4. **ἐγὼ φέρω τὰ δῶρα, ἀλλὰ σὺ οὐκ ἐθέλεις.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Now we learn the works of the wise.* — **τὰ τῶν σοφῶν ἔργα** has the
   genitive tucked inside the article group, which Greek does constantly
   and English cannot copy.
2. *The teacher teaches the good sons.*
3. *You do not hear the judge's letter.*
4. *I carry the gifts, but you are not willing.* — Both pronouns are
   present and both are doing work: the sentence is a contrast, and the
   **ἀλλά** confirms it. A translation that drops the pronouns loses the
   argument the sentence is having.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. We hear the words.
2. They teach the sons.
3. You *(one person)* are learning the letters.
4. Now I carry the gifts.
5. ***I*** teach, but ***you*** learn.  *(the stress is on both)*
:::

::: answers
1. **ἀκούομεν τοὺς λόγους.**
2. **διδάσκουσι τοὺς υἱούς.**
3. **μανθάνεις τὰ γράμματα.**
4. **νῦν φέρω τὰ δῶρα.**
5. **ἐγὼ μὲν διδάσκω, σὺ δὲ μανθάνεις.** — accept **ἐγὼ διδάσκω, ἀλλὰ σὺ
   μανθάνεις** without comment; **μέν … δέ** is the idiomatic pair for a
   contrast like this and arrives in Part III. The point being tested is
   that both pronouns must be there, because the English stresses both.

In 1–4 a supplied pronoun is an error of emphasis. In 5 its absence is.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's question"}
Marcus asked why anyone writes **ἐγώ** if the ending already says it.

1. Give the best answer you can in one sentence.
2. Write a pair of Greek sentences, one with the pronoun and one without,
   and translate both so that the difference shows in English.
3. English always has to supply *I*. Does that mean English can never make
   the emphasis Greek makes? How does it manage?
:::

::: answers
1. Because the ending supplies the subject, so the pronoun is free to supply
   something else, and what it supplies is contrast.
2. Answers will vary. **λέγω τὴν ἀλήθειαν** / **ἐγὼ λέγω τὴν ἀλήθειαν** —
   *I tell the truth* / *I tell the truth* (as against whoever does not).
3. English uses stress in speech and italics in writing, and in both cases
   it is adding something outside the words. Greek does it with a word.
   Worth five minutes: which method survives being copied out by hand?
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 5:44"}
A sentence in which the pronoun is doing exactly what Marcus asked about.

**ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν· ἀγαπᾶτε τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν …**

*(The verse goes on: and pray for those who persecute you.)*

- **ἐγώ** — I *(emphatic, and the point of the sentence)*
- **δέ** — but, and *(a connecting word; it never comes first)*
- **λέγω** — I say *(your verb, first person singular)*
- **ὑμῖν** — to you *(dative plural — Lesson 9)*
- **ἀγαπᾶτε** — love! *(a contract verb, and a command — Lesson 26)*
- **τοὺς ἐχθρούς** — the enemies *(accusative plural)*
- **ὑμῶν** — of you, your *(genitive plural — Lesson 8)*

*But I say to you, love your enemies.*

**λέγω** on its own would have been *I say*. The speaker has added **ἐγώ**,
and the whole force of the line is in that one unnecessary word. It is
unnecessary grammatically and indispensable rhetorically, because the
sentence it belongs to is one half of a contrast: *you have heard that it
was said …* — **ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν**.

Marcus asked why anyone would write **ἐγώ**. This is why. Somebody is being
argued with.

Notice also **ἀγαπᾶτε**, with its circumflex. That is the contract verb from
this lesson, in a form you cannot make yet. You can see the **-τε** and say
*second person plural*, which is most of the way there.

::: note
**δέ** is one of the small words that hold Greek prose together, and it has
a rule: it never stands first in its clause. It comes second, leaning on
the word in front of it. You will see **δέ** in almost every sentence of
Part III, and from now on you should expect the first word of a Greek clause
to be the important one.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. If the verb ending already tells you the subject, why would a Greek
   writer ever add **ἐγώ** or **σύ**? What are they doing when they do?
2. Marcus answered four questions correctly while arguing that they could
   not be answered. What does that suggest about the difference between
   knowing something and believing you know it?
3. **ἀγαπᾶτε τοὺς ἐχθροὺς ὑμῶν** is a command. Can a feeling be commanded?
   Does the Greek word help?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Marcus kept the sheet.

Years later, teaching a class of his own in a province he had not expected
to end up in, he set the same exercise, with the subjects removed, and
waited for somebody to tell him it could not be done.

Somebody did, in the second week. He said it was the first genuinely good
question anybody had asked that term, and then had to go and stand by the
window for a moment.
:::
