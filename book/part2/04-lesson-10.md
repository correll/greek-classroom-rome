# Adjectives Must Agree

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Everyone gets an adjective," said Sabina. "I have chosen them."

"On what authority?" said Quintus.

"On the authority of having chosen them already."

She had written the list out, which meant there was no arguing with it.

"Julia is **ἡ σοφὴ θυγάτηρ**. Livia is **ἡ μικρὰ ψυχή** — small soul, which
is not an insult, it is because she is quiet. Quintus is **ὁ ἀγαθὸς
ἄνθρωπος**." Quintus looked pleased. "And Felix is **ὁ κακὸς υἱός**."

"That is slander."

"It is agreement," said Sabina. "**ὁ** is masculine, **κακός** is
masculine, **υἱός** is masculine. Three for three. Theodoros, is it
correct?"

Theodoros was in difficulty, and the class could see it.

"...The grammar is correct."

"Thank you."

"The grammar being correct," he went on, "is not the same as it being—"

"Marcus," said Sabina, consulting her list, "is **ὁ μέγας λόγος**."

The room took a moment to work this out, and then a noise went round it that
Theodoros decided not to notice.

"*The great word*," said Marcus. "I accept that."

"*The big talk*," said Julia.

"Both," said Sabina. "That is the point of the word."

Theodoros wrote two things on the board, one above the other.

> **ὁ κακὸς υἱός**
>
> **κακὸς ὁ υἱός**

"Sabina," he said, "has accused Felix of being a bad son. The second one
says it rather more plainly. Before anyone writes anything else about anyone
else, you are going to learn the difference, because today it is funny and
in two weeks it will be in your examination."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## An adjective takes the gender of its noun

An adjective must match its noun in **gender**, **number**, and **case** —
all three, every time. It is not a fixed word; it is a word with the same
set of endings you already know.

**ἀγαθός** — *good* — has three columns, and you have seen all of them
before:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **nom. sing.** | **ἀγαθός** | **ἀγαθή** | **ἀγαθόν** |
| **acc. sing.** | **ἀγαθόν** | **ἀγαθήν** | **ἀγαθόν** |
| **gen. sing.** | **ἀγαθοῦ** | **ἀγαθῆς** | **ἀγαθοῦ** |
| **dat. sing.** | **ἀγαθῷ** | **ἀγαθῇ** | **ἀγαθῷ** |
| **nom. plur.** | **ἀγαθοί** | **ἀγαθαί** | **ἀγαθά** |
| **acc. plur.** | **ἀγαθούς** | **ἀγαθάς** | **ἀγαθά** |

The masculine is **ὁ λόγος**. The feminine is **ἡ ψυχή**. The neuter is
**τὸ δῶρον**. There is nothing new in this table at all — it is three nouns
you already know, wearing one meaning.

So: **ὁ ἀγαθὸς λόγος**, **ἡ ἀγαθὴ ψυχή**, **τὸ ἀγαθὸν δῶρον**. The
adjective is not choosing its own ending. The noun is choosing it.

::: note
**The adjective agrees with its noun, not with the thing.** **ἡ ὁδός** is
feminine, so a good road is **ἡ ἀγαθὴ ὁδός**, with a feminine adjective and
a noun that looks masculine. Gender is a fact about words, as it was in
Lesson 3, and the adjective follows the word.
:::

## Where it stands changes what it says

This is the lesson. Greek says two quite different things with the same two
words, and the difference is position.

**Inside the article-noun group** — the adjective describes the noun:

> **ὁ ἀγαθὸς ἄνθρωπος** — the good man
>
> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὁ ἀγαθός** — the good man *(the same, with the article
> repeated)*

**Outside it** — the adjective is being *asserted* of the noun, and the
sentence needs no verb at all:

> **ἀγαθὸς ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — the man is good
>
> **ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἀγαθός** — the man is good

The test is simple and mechanical. **Is the adjective sheltering under an
article?** If yes, it describes. If no, it asserts.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ὁ σοφὸς κριτής** | the wise judge |
| **σοφὸς ὁ κριτής** | the judge is wise |
| **ὁ κριτὴς ὁ σοφός** | the wise judge |
| **ὁ κριτὴς σοφός** | the judge is wise |

Two of those are descriptions and two are claims, and the only evidence is
where **ὁ** is standing.

This is the same mechanism you met in **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά**. There the
adjective **χαλεπά** stood outside the group and asserted something about
**τὰ καλά**. You have been reading predicate position since Lesson 3; today
it gets its name.

::: latinbridge
Latin cannot do this, and the reason is Lesson 3: Latin has no article, so
it has no inside and outside of an article group to put the adjective in.

*Bonus homo* and *homo bonus* both mean *the good man*, and if a Latin
writer wants *the man is good* they must supply *est*. Greek gets the same
sentence for nothing, by moving one word.

This is the clearest case in the course of the article earning its keep.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἀγαθός** — good
- **κακός** — bad, evil
- **καλός** — fine, beautiful, noble
- **σοφός** — wise, skilled
- **μικρός** — small, little
- **μέγας** — great, large

::: note
**μικρός** has **μικρά** in the feminine, not **μικρή**: after **ε**, **ι**
or **ρ** the first declension keeps alpha. Compare **ἡ οἰκία**, which does
the same thing for the same reason.

**μέγας** is irregular and worth knowing anyway, because it is everywhere:
**μέγας**, **μεγάλη**, **μέγα**. Everything except the masculine and neuter
nominative and accusative singular is built on **μεγάλ-**. Take the three
forms printed and leave the rest until you meet it.

**καλός** is the hardest of these to translate and the most worth having.
It covers beautiful, fine, noble, admirable, and well-made, and a Greek
would not have thought of those as five things.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Sabina's list was confiscated. The exercises are Theodoros's.

::: {.exercise title="Make it agree"}
Put the adjective into the right form.

1. **ὁ** (**σοφός**) **διδάσκαλος**
2. **ἡ** (**μικρός**) **οἰκία**
3. **τὰ** (**ἀγαθός**) **ἔργα**
4. **τοὺς** (**κακός**) **λόγους**
5. **τῇ** (**καλός**) **ἡμέρᾳ**
6. **τῶν** (**σοφός**) **ἀνθρώπων**
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ σοφὸς διδάσκαλος** — masc. nom. sing.
2. **ἡ μικρὰ οἰκία** — fem. nom. sing. Note **μικρά**, not **μικρή**.
3. **τὰ ἀγαθὰ ἔργα** — neut. nom. or acc. plur.
4. **τοὺς κακοὺς λόγους** — masc. acc. plur.
5. **τῇ καλῇ ἡμέρᾳ** — fem. dat. sing. Both iota subscripts must appear.
6. **τῶν σοφῶν ἀνθρώπων** — masc. gen. plur.

The article is doing the teaching in every one of these. A pupil who reads
**τῇ** and writes a nominative has not looked at it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Describing or asserting?"}
Translate, and say whether the adjective describes or asserts.

1. **ὁ ἀγαθὸς κριτής**
2. **ἀγαθὸς ὁ κριτής**
3. **ἡ ψυχὴ ἡ καλή**
4. **ἡ ψυχὴ καλή**
5. **μικρὰ τὰ δῶρα τῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The good judge.* Describes — inside the article group.
2. *The judge is good.* Asserts — outside it.
3. *The good soul.* Describes; the repeated article keeps **καλή** inside.
4. *The soul is beautiful.* Asserts; no second article, so **καλή** is out
   in the open.
5. *The gifts of the men are small.* Asserts. **μικρά** stands outside, and
   the sentence has no verb and needs none.

3 and 4 differ by one word, **ἡ**, and are a description and a claim
respectively. Spend time here.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The wise judge.  *(subject)*
2. The judge is wise.
3. The good works.  *(object)*
4. Of the small house.
5. The great word is bad.
6. The teacher is good, but the son is small.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ σοφὸς κριτής.**
2. **σοφὸς ὁ κριτής.** — or **ὁ κριτὴς σοφός**.
3. **τὰ ἀγαθὰ ἔργα.**
4. **τῆς μικρᾶς οἰκίας.** — note the circumflex: the first declension
   genitive singular in **-ᾱς** takes one when the nominative is
   accented on the last syllable. Accept **τῆς μικρας οἰκίας** as a near
   miss and show them the mark.
5. **κακὸς ὁ μέγας λόγος.** — one adjective describing, one asserting, in
   the same sentence. This is the hardest question on the sheet.
6. **ἀγαθὸς ὁ διδάσκαλος, ἀλλὰ μικρὸς ὁ υἱός.**

1 and 2 are the examination. A pupil who produces the same Greek for both
has not understood the lesson, however well they have learned the endings.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Sabina's list"}
Sabina wrote **ὁ κακὸς υἱός** about Felix.

1. Translate it.
2. Rewrite it so that it says *the son is bad*.
3. Which of the two is the accusation? Which is merely a label?
4. Theodoros said the grammar was correct and would not say more. Write the
   sentence he could have used to say *Sabina is wise*, and then the one
   that would have said *the wise Sabina*, and say which he would have
   preferred.
:::

::: answers
1. *The bad son.*
2. **κακὸς ὁ υἱός.**
3. The second is the accusation: it asserts. The first merely names him,
   the way *the bad son* names a character in a story. Sabina chose the
   weaker of the two, whether or not she knew it.
4. *Sabina is wise* asserts: the adjective stands outside the article.
   *The wise Sabina* describes: it stands inside. He would have preferred
   the first, because it is a judgement he is willing to defend, and the
   second is a title he is not willing to award in front of Marcus.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 10:11"}
A sentence that puts the adjective inside the article group, twice over, and
could not have been written any other way.

**ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός.**

- **ἐγώ εἰμι** — I am *(Lesson 5)*
- **ὁ ποιμήν** — the shepherd *(third declension; recognise it)*
- **ὁ καλός** — the good, the fine *(this lesson)*

*I am the good shepherd.*

Look at **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός**. The article appears twice, and the second one
is not an accident or a flourish: it is there to keep **καλός** *inside* the
group, so that the phrase describes the shepherd rather than asserting
something about him.

Take the second article away and you get **ὁ ποιμὴν καλός** — *the shepherd
is good*. True, perhaps, but a different sentence, and not one that would
follow **ἐγώ εἰμι** at all.

So the three words after **εἰμι** are a single thing being claimed:
*the-good-shepherd*. That is what the speaker says he is. One small word
holds it together.

This is the third **ἐγώ εἰμι** you have met — the way and the truth and the
life in Lesson 5, and now this. The pronoun is doing in all of them what it
did for Aelia: putting the weight on the speaker.

::: note
Koine, and from John again. The course will move to Attic prose for most of
Part III; these three sentences were chosen because you could read them in
your first term, not because the New Testament is the centre of the course.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Greek can say *the man is wise* without any verb at all, just by where
   the adjective stands. What does that tell you about what a sentence
   needs?
2. Sabina's **ὁ κακὸς υἱός** was grammatically correct and Theodoros said
   so. Was he right to say so?
3. **καλός** means beautiful, fine, noble and well-made at once. Is a
   language with one word there seeing something English has lost, or
   failing to make a distinction English has gained?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
The list was returned to Sabina at the end of term, folded, with one
addition in Theodoros's hand at the bottom.

> **ἡ σοφὴ Σαβίνα.**

Inside the article group. A description, not a judgement — and therefore,
as she pointed out to him immediately, not something he had actually
committed himself to.

He said that was why he had chosen it.
:::
