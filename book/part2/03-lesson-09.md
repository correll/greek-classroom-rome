# The Dative and the Recipient

::: story
Felix had been composing for some minutes, which was unusual, and smiling
while he did it, which was worse.

"I have written you a message," he announced.

"In Greek?"

"In Greek."

Theodoros put down what he was holding. "Read it to the class."

Felix stood, held up his tablet, and read with enormous care:

> **πέμπω τὸν διδάσκαλον.**

He waited. The pause went on slightly too long for his liking.

"What did you intend?"

"*I send a letter to the teacher.*" Felix looked at his tablet again.
"Which is what it says."

"It is not what it says."

"It says **πέμπω** — *I send*. And **τὸν διδάσκαλον** — the teacher. I send,
to the teacher."

"**τὸν διδάσκαλον** is accusative," said Julia, who had got there some
seconds earlier and had been visibly holding it in. "It is the thing being
sent."

Felix read his sentence again.

"*I send the teacher*," said Livia.

"Where?" said Quintus. "Where are you sending him?"

"I am not sending him anywhere —"

"You have not said where," said Theodoros. "That is the only mercy in it.
You have announced to this room that you are dispatching me, as one
dispatches a parcel, to an unspecified destination."

Felix sat down. He appeared to be weighing something.

"It is not even rude," he said at last, with real disappointment. "I would
not have minded if it were rude."

"No," said Theodoros. "It is merely peculiar, and that is worse, and it
happened because the teacher is not the *object* of your sending. He is the
one it goes **to**. Greek has a case for that, and you have not learned it
until this afternoon, so sit still and be peculiar for ten more minutes."
:::

::: newgreek
## The one it goes to

The **dative** is the fourth and last case you need. Its central job is the
one Felix wanted: it marks the person something is given, sent, said, or
shown **to**.

> **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.** — I send the letter to the teacher.

Three nouns, three different jobs, and each one says which job it has:

- **ἐγώ**, hidden inside **πέμπω** — the one doing it *(nominative)*
- **τὴν ἐπιστολήν** — the thing sent *(accusative)*
- **τῷ διδασκάλῳ** — the one it goes to *(dative)*

English has to use word order and the word *to*. Greek uses an ending, and
can then put the three nouns in whatever order it likes.

## The forms

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **dat. sing.** | **τῷ** | **τῇ** | **τῷ** |
| **dat. plur.** | **τοῖς** | **ταῖς** | **τοῖς** |

| | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **ἡ οἰκία** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **dat. sing.** | **τῷ λόγῳ** | **τῇ ψυχῇ** | **τῇ οἰκίᾳ** | **τῷ δώρῳ** |
| **dat. plur.** | **τοῖς λόγοις** | **ταῖς ψυχαῖς** | **ταῖς οἰκίαις** | **τοῖς δώροις** |

Look at the singular endings and you will see the same small mark under
every one of them: **-ῳ**, **-ῃ**, **-ᾳ**. That is an **iota subscript** —
an iota written underneath, because it stopped being pronounced long before
it stopped being written.

It is easy to miss, it is easy to leave out, and leaving it out turns a
dative into something else. Write it.

::: note
**You now have all four cases.** Here is the whole of it, in one line each:

- **nominative** — the one doing it
- **accusative** — the one it is done to
- **genitive** — whose it is, or where it came from
- **dative** — the one it goes to

Every noun inside a sentence is in one of these four, and tells you which.
(The vocative, for calling to someone, stands outside the sentence; you had
it in Lesson 4.) There is nothing more to come. The rest of Part II is
practice.
:::

## Two more things the dative does

**By means of.** With no preposition at all, the dative can say what
something was done *with*:

> **γράφω τοῖς γράμμασιν.** — I write with letters. *(**γράμμασιν** is the
> third-declension dative plural of **τὰ γράμματα**; take it whole.)*

**In, and with.** Two prepositions take the dative and only the dative:

- **ἐν** — in, among
- **σύν** — together with

> **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — in the house
>
> **σὺν τῷ υἱῷ** — together with the son

Note the difference between the two *withs*. **σύν** is *in company with* —
the son is walking beside you. The bare dative is *by means of* — the
letters are the tool. English uses one word for both and leaves you to
guess; Greek makes you decide which you meant.

::: latinbridge
Latin splits this work between two cases. *To the teacher* is dative —
*magistro* — exactly as in Greek. But *with the son* and *by means of
letters* are **ablative** in Latin, and Greek has no ablative.

So Greek's dative is doing the work of two Latin cases, and when you are
translating out of Latin you will have to stop and ask which one you are
looking at. That is not a difficulty to be got around. It is the question
the Greek is asking you.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **δίδωμι** — I give *(third person **δίδωσι** — he, she, or it gives)*
- **πέμπω** — I send
- **ἡ ἐπιστολή** — letter, message
- **ἐν** — in, among *(with the dative)*
- **σύν** — together with *(with the dative)*
- **ὁ ἄγγελος** — messenger
- **ἡ φωνή** — voice, sound *(Lesson 1)*
- **ἡ εἰρήνη** — peace *(Lesson 2)*
- **ἡ θύρα** — door *(Lesson 3)*
- **τρέχω** — I run
- **φεύγω** — I flee, run away *(Lesson 5)*
- **ἐνταῦθα** — here; there, at that point *(Lesson 6)*

::: note
**δίδωμι** does not end in **-ω** like the verbs you know, and it does not
conjugate like them. It belongs to a small, very old, very common family
whose full set of endings comes in Lesson 36. Learn **δίδωμι** and **δίδωσι** as two
whole words for now and do not try to build the rest.

**ἡ ἐπιστολή** goes like **ἡ ψυχή** throughout: **τῆς ἐπιστολῆς**, **τῇ
ἐπιστολῇ**, **τὴν ἐπιστολήν**. It is the ancestor of *epistle*, and the
verb behind it means simply *to send to*.
:::

7. Make the subject of **ὁ διδάσκαλος γράφει τὰ γράμματα** plural, and

8. Translate **περὶ τῶν ἔργων**.

10. Translate **μόνον**.

::: answers
1. Genitive singular.
2. *Son.*
3. Second person plural of **εἰμί**: *you (all) are*.
4. **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.**
5. **ἐξ ἀρχῆς** — a vowel follows.
6. Feminine, genitive, plural; the circumflex has gone to the last
   syllable, which every first-declension genitive plural does whatever
   the nominative's accent.
7. **οἱ διδάσκαλοι γράφουσι τὰ γράμματα.** — the verb must move too; a
   singular verb with a plural subject is the commonest slip.
8. *About the deeds.*
9. **ὁ διδάσκαλος**, because it has the article and **φίλος** does not.
10. *Only* — the neuter of **μόνος**, used as an adverb.

::: {.exercise title="Which case, and why?"}
For every noun phrase other than the subject, give the case and say what
job it is doing.

1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ κριτῇ.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος δίδωσι τὰ δῶρα τοῖς ἀνθρώποις.**
3. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ γράφομεν.**
4. **τὸ βιβλίον τοῦ υἱοῦ ἐστιν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
5. **ὁ ἄγγελος πέμπει τὰς ἐπιστολὰς τοῖς κριταῖς.**
6. **σὺν τοῖς τέκνοις τρέχει ὁ δοῦλος ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.**
7. **γράφομεν τοῖς γράμμασιν ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ.**
8. **ἡ θυγάτηρ τοῦ κριτοῦ δίδωσι τὸν ἵππον τῷ υἱῷ τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
9. **ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις τῆς εἰρήνης οἱ ἄνθρωποι πέμπουσιν ἐπιστολὰς τοῖς
   φίλοις.**
10. **τῇ φωνῇ λέγει ὁ μάρτυς τὴν ἀλήθειαν τῷ κριτῇ.**
:::

::: answers
1. **τὴν ἐπιστολήν** accusative, the thing sent; **τῷ κριτῇ** dative, the
   one it goes to.
2. **τὰ δῶρα** accusative, the things given; **τοῖς ἀνθρώποις** dative, the
   recipients.
3. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** dative, after **ἐν**; the place.
4. **τοῦ υἱοῦ** genitive, whose book it is; **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** dative, where
   it is. Three of the four cases appear in this sentence; ask the class
   to find the nominative, and then to say which case is missing and why
   (**ἐστιν** takes no object).
5. **τὰς ἐπιστολάς** accusative, the things sent; **τοῖς κριταῖς** dative
   plural, the recipients. **κριτής** takes **-αῖς** like **ψυχαῖς**.
6. **σὺν τοῖς τέκνοις** dative, after **σύν** — company, the children run
   too; **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας** genitive, after **ἐκ**.
7. **τοῖς γράμμασιν** dative with no preposition — the means, *with
   letters*; **ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ** dative after **ἐν** — the place. Two datives,
   two jobs, in five words.
8. **τοῦ κριτοῦ** genitive, whose daughter; **τὸν ἵππον** accusative, the
   thing given; **τῷ υἱῷ** dative, the recipient; **τοῦ διδασκάλου**
   genitive, whose son. All four cases; the nominative is **ἡ θυγάτηρ**.
9. **ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις** dative after **ἐν** — here the *time* rather than
   the place, which **ἐν** also does; **τῆς εἰρήνης** genitive, what days
   they are; **ἐπιστολάς** accusative, the things sent; **τοῖς φίλοις**
   dative, the recipients.
10. **τῇ φωνῇ** dative of means — *with his voice*, no preposition; **τὴν
    ἀλήθειαν** accusative; **τῷ κριτῇ** dative, the one told. A pupil who
    takes **τῇ φωνῇ** as *to the voice* has met the dative's two jobs and
    chosen the wrong one; ask whether a voice can receive anything.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **δίδωσι τὰ γράμματα τῷ υἱῷ.**
2. **σὺν τῷ φίλῳ βλέπομεν τὴν οἰκίαν.**
3. **οὐ πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τοῖς κριταῖς.**
4. **ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις τοῦ χρόνου ἔχομεν τὰ ἔργα.**
5. **ὁ ἄγγελος τρέχει πρὸς τὴν θύραν σὺν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ.**
6. **ἐν τῇ δέλτῳ γράφει ὁ μαθητὴς τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ θεοῦ.**
7. **οἱ δοῦλοι φεύγουσιν ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας, ἀλλὰ τὰ τέκνα ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ μένει.**
8. **εἰρήνη τοῖς ἀνθρώποις.**
9. **ὁ κριτὴς οὐ δίδωσι τὴν δέλτον τοῖς μαθηταῖς, ἀλλὰ τῷ διδασκάλῳ.**
10. **τῇ φωνῇ γιγνώσκομεν τὸν φίλον.**
:::

::: answers
1. *He gives the letters to the son.*
2. *Together with the friend we see the house.*
3. *I do not send the letter to the judges.*
4. *In the days of time we have the work.* Deliberately slightly odd in
   English; the pupils should be able to account for all four noun phrases
   even though the sentence is not a thing anyone would say.
5. *The messenger runs to the door with the letter.* — **πρός** with the
   accusative for the direction, **σύν** with the dative for what comes
   along.
6. *On the tablet the pupil writes the name of God.* — **ἐν** here is *on*
   as much as *in*; English chooses, Greek does not have to.
7. *The slaves flee out of the house, but the children stay in the house.*
   — **τὰ τέκνα … μένει**: a neuter plural subject takes a singular verb.
   The reading of Lesson 7 showed it; a pupil who "corrects" it to
   **μένουσι** has produced acceptable Greek and should be told that the
   singular is the ordinary form.
8. *Peace to men*, or *peace to people.* — No verb; the dative alone
   carries *to*. Compare the Luke 2:14 line below.
9. *The judge does not give the tablet to the pupils, but to the teacher.*
10. *By the voice we recognise the friend.* — dative of means with no
    preposition. A pupil who writes *to the voice* should be sent back to
    *Which case, and why?*, item 10.
:::

1. I send the letter.
2. I send the letter to the teacher.
3. He gives the gift to the son.
4. In the house.
5. Together with the friends.
6. We do not write to the judge.
7. The messenger runs to the door.
8. We write with letters.
9. Peace in the house.
10. The son gives the gifts to the friends of the judge.
:::

::: answers
1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
2. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.**
3. **δίδωσι τὸ δῶρον τῷ υἱῷ.**
4. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
5. **σὺν τοῖς φίλοις.**
6. **οὐ γράφομεν τῷ κριτῇ.**
7. **ὁ ἄγγελος τρέχει πρὸς τὴν θύραν.** — **πρός** and the accusative,
   because this is *towards*, not *to* as a recipient. A pupil who writes
   **τῇ θύρᾳ** has given the door a letter.
8. **γράφομεν τοῖς γράμμασιν.** — the dative of means, no preposition.
   **σὺν τοῖς γράμμασιν** would mean the letters came along for company.
9. **εἰρήνη ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
10. **ὁ υἱὸς δίδωσι τὰ δῶρα τοῖς φίλοις τοῦ κριτοῦ.** — four noun phrases,
    four jobs; ask for every case before marking it.

The iota subscript is the whole examination here. **τῷ διδασκάλω** without
it is not a dative and not anything else either. Mark it as wrong every
time, early, while it is still cheap to fix.

In 6, accept **τῷ κριτῇ** only; a pupil who writes **τὸν κριτήν** has made
Felix's mistake, and should be shown the two side by side rather than
simply corrected.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Felix's message"}
Felix wrote **πέμπω τὸν διδάσκαλον** and meant *I send a letter to the
teacher*.

1. Write what he should have written.
2. Explain, in one sentence, what his version actually claims.
3. Felix said he would not have minded if it had been rude. Why was the
   peculiar version worse than a rude one would have been?
4. Felix's corrected message said nothing except that it was being sent.
   Write one sentence he could have added, in which the teacher is given
   something.
5. Put Felix's corrected sentence into the plural: *we send the letters to
   the teachers*.
6. Quintus asked *where?* Finish Felix's original sentence so that it
   answers him: *I send the teacher to the house.* Which case, and which
   small word, does *to the house* need here?
7. Which mark, on which two words, shows that **τῷ διδασκάλῳ** is a
   dative?
8. Write *the teacher sends the pupil* — the sentence Felix wrote, the
   other way round.
9. Write Felix's corrected sentence twice more, once with **τῷ διδασκάλῳ**
   first and once with **τὴν ἐπιστολήν** first, and say what each order
   puts the weight on.
10. Felix wrote **τὸν διδάσκαλον** where he needed **τῷ διδασκάλῳ**. How
    many letters differ, and what does that tell you about where to look
    when a Greek sentence seems to say something strange?
:::

::: answers
1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.** The letter must be supplied — his
   original did not contain it — and the teacher must move into the dative.
2. That he is dispatching Theodoros somewhere, as a parcel.
3. This is a question about precision rather than grammar, and worth two
   minutes. A rude sentence would at least have been the sentence he
   intended. The peculiar one was produced by somebody who believed he was
   saying something else, which is the position this whole course is
   designed to get you out of.
4. Anything correct; e.g. **δίδωμι τὸ δῶρον τῷ διδασκάλῳ** or **δίδωμι
   τῷ διδασκάλῳ τὴν δέλτον**. Check for the dative on the teacher and the
   accusative on the thing.
5. **πέμπομεν τὰς ἐπιστολὰς τοῖς διδασκάλοις.** — three plurals and all
   three endings change.
6. **πέμπω τὸν διδάσκαλον πρὸς τὴν οἰκίαν.** — accusative after **πρός**,
   because the house is a destination, not a recipient. A pupil who writes
   **τῇ οἰκίᾳ** has given the house a teacher in the post; accept it only
   with a straight face.
7. The iota subscript, under the **ω** of **τῷ** and under the **ω** of
   **διδασκάλῳ**. Two of them, and both must be there.
8. **ὁ διδάσκαλος πέμπει τὸν μαθητήν.**
9. **τῷ διδασκάλῳ πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολήν** — the weight is on the teacher:
   it is *to him* that it goes. **τὴν ἐπιστολὴν πέμπω τῷ διδασκάλῳ** — the
   weight is on the letter: *a letter* is what I am sending. Neither
   changes who gets what; that was settled by the endings (Lesson 4).
10. The article changes entirely (**τόν** → **τῷ**) and the noun's last
    syllable changes (**-ον** → **-ῳ**). When Greek seems to be saying
    something strange, look at the ends of the words before you look at
    the dictionary: Felix had every word right and every ending wrong on
    one of them.
:::
:::

::: {.voice source="Luke 2:14"}
A line you may know in English, and whose structure is carried by three
datives.

**δόξα ἐν ὑψίστοις θεῷ καὶ ἐπὶ γῆς εἰρήνη ἐν ἀνθρώποις εὐδοκίας.**

- **δόξα** — glory *(nominative)*
- **ἐν ὑψίστοις** — in the highest places *(dative plural, after **ἐν**)*
- **θεῷ** — to God *(dative — the recipient, with no preposition at all)*
- **καί** — and
- **ἐπὶ γῆς** — on earth *(genitive, after **ἐπί**)*
- **εἰρήνη** — peace *(nominative)*
- **ἐν ἀνθρώποις** — among men *(dative plural of your word from Lesson 1)*
- **εὐδοκίας** — of good pleasure, of favour *(genitive)*

*Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among men of good will.*

Two datives doing two different jobs, in the same half-line. **ἐν ὑψίστοις**
is **ἐν** plus the dative, giving place. **θεῷ** is a bare dative with no
preposition whatever, giving the recipient — exactly the ending Felix
needed and did not have.

There is no verb in the whole line. Greek is content to say *glory to God*
and leave the *be* to be understood, which English can also just about
manage.

::: note
**One letter, and a very old argument.** The last word appears in some
manuscripts as **εὐδοκίας**, genitive, and in others as **εὐδοκία**,
nominative. On the first reading the peace is for *men of good will* — the
people God favours. On the second it is *peace on earth, good will toward
men* — which is the version that reached English hymns.

The whole difference is a final **ς**. You are not in a position to judge
which reading is right, and neither are most adults. But you are already in
a position to see that the two readings are different, and why, and that is
further than the argument usually gets.
:::
:::

The seventh tablet concerned a letter, and Felix read it aloud himself,
having been given no choice in the matter.

ὁ Φῆλιξ γράφει ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ. ἐν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ λέγει· ὦ
διδάσκαλε, ἐγὼ γράφω, ὁ Μᾶρκος οὐ γράφει.

ὁ αἴλουρος ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ ἐστὶ σὺν τοῖς μαθηταῖς. ὁ Φῆλιξ οὐ δίδωσι
τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ, ἀλλὰ τῷ αἰλούρῳ. ὦ αἴλουρε, λέγει, ἄγγελος
εἶ. ὁ αἴλουρος γιγνώσκει τὴν φωνήν, ἀλλὰ οὐ τὰ γράμματα.

ὁ αἴλουρος λαμβάνει τὴν ἐπιστολὴν καὶ τρέχει πρὸς τὴν θύραν καὶ φεύγει
ἐκ τοῦ διδασκαλείου. οἱ μαθηταὶ ἐνταῦθα, ὁ αἴλουρος ἐκεῖ· ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ
καθεύδει σὺν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ. ὁ ἄγγελος δίδωσι τὰς ἐπιστολὰς τοῖς
ἀνθρώποις· ὁ αἴλουρος οὐδὲν δίδωσιν οὐδενί.

ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγει τοῖς μαθηταῖς· οὐκ ἄγγελός ἐστιν ὁ αἴλουρος, ἀλλὰ
ζῷον. εἰρήνη ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ οὐκ ἔστιν· ὁ Φῆλιξ γράφει ἐπιστολὴν τῷ
αἰλούρῳ.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Φῆλιξ, ὁ Μᾶρκος** — Felix, Marcus
- **ὁ αἴλουρος** — the cat; **τῷ αἰλούρῳ** is its dative and **ὦ αἴλουρε**
  its vocative, both by the ordinary rules
- **τὸ διδασκαλεῖον** — the schoolroom
- **καθεύδει** — sleeps
- **οὐδὲν … οὐδενί** — nothing … to anyone *(two forms of one word,
  **οὐδείς**, *no one*; Greek lets its negatives reinforce one another
  rather than cancel)*
- **διδασκαλείῳ ἐστί, ἄγγελός ἐστιν, οὐκ ἔστιν** — take the accents as
  printed; **ἐστί** leans on the word before it
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything.

1. To whom does Felix write the letter, and to whom does he give it? Which
   case marks both, and which word keeps the two apart?
2. What does Felix write in the letter? Why is **ἐγώ** there?
3. Where is the cat at the start, and in whose company? Two datives: say
   which preposition governs each, and what each one means.
4. What does Felix call the cat? What in the Greek tells you he is speaking
   *to* it rather than *about* it?
5. What does the cat know, and what does it not know?
6. Follow the cat out. List its three verbs, from taking the letter to
   running off, and the two
   prepositional phrases that go with them, each with its case.
7. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ καθεύδει σὺν τῇ ἐπιστολῇ** — two datives. Why does each
   have the preposition it has?
8. What does a messenger do that the cat does not? Translate the sentence
   with **οὐδέν** in it.
9. **οὐκ ἄγγελός ἐστιν ὁ αἴλουρος, ἀλλὰ ζῷον** — which noun is the
   subject, and how do you know?
10. Translate the last paragraph. Judging from its last sentence, what has
    Felix learned?
:::

::: answers
1. He writes to the teacher, **τῷ διδασκάλῳ**, and gives it to the cat,
   **τῷ αἰλούρῳ**. Both are datives — the one it goes to — and **ἀλλά**
   is what keeps them apart: *not to the teacher, but to the cat*.
2. *Teacher, I am writing; Marcus is not writing.* **ἐγώ** sets Felix
   against Marcus; without the contrast it would not be there (Lesson 5).
3. In the schoolroom, **ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ**, together with the pupils,
   **σὺν τοῖς μαθηταῖς**. **ἐν** gives the place, **σύν** the company.
4. A messenger: **ἄγγελος εἶ**, *you are a messenger*. The vocative **ὦ
   αἴλουρε**, with its **-ε** and its **ὦ**, says he is addressing it; and
   **εἶ** is second person.
5. The voice, **τὴν φωνήν**; not the letters, **οὐ τὰ γράμματα** — it
   knows Felix's voice and cannot read.
6. **λαμβάνει** (takes), **τρέχει** (runs), **φεύγει** (flees); **πρὸς
   τὴν θύραν**, accusative after **πρός** — the direction; **ἐκ τοῦ
   διδασκαλείου**, genitive after **ἐκ** — the place it leaves.
7. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — *in the house*, the place, so **ἐν**. **σὺν τῇ
   ἐπιστολῇ** — *with the letter*, which is keeping it company, so
   **σύν**. A bare dative **τῇ ἐπιστολῇ** would have meant the cat was
   sleeping *by means of* the letter, which even this cat does not do.
8. Gives letters to people: **ὁ ἄγγελος δίδωσι τὰς ἐπιστολὰς τοῖς
   ἀνθρώποις**. *The cat gives nothing to anyone* — **οὐδὲν δίδωσιν
   οὐδενί**, two negatives and no cancelling.
9. **ὁ αἴλουρος**, because it has the article; **ἄγγελος** and **ζῷον**
   are bare, and are the two things said and unsaid about it. The order
   puts **ἄγγελος** first for emphasis — *a messenger is what the cat is
   not* — and a pupil who makes the messenger the subject has read the
   order (Lesson 6).
10. *The teacher says to the pupils: the cat is not a messenger, but an
    animal. There is no peace in the schoolroom: Felix is writing a letter
    to the cat.* He has learned the dative perfectly and nothing else.
:::
:::

::: question
1. The dative covers *to*, *for*, *with*, *by*, and *in*. Is that one case
   doing many jobs, or several cases that happened to collapse into one
   shape?
2. Greek distinguishes *with the son* from *with a pen*. English does not.
   Has English lost something, or shed something?
3. A single **ς** decides who the peace in Luke 2:14 is for. Should a
   translator tell the reader when a choice like that has been made, or get
   on with it?
:::

::: story
Felix delivered the corrected message at the end of the hour, folded, with
some ceremony.

It read:

> **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.**

Theodoros turned it over. There was nothing else written on it.

"It is the message," said Felix. "The message is that I am sending it. I
have not sent anything else."

"No," Theodoros agreed. "But the case is correct, and the iota is under the
omega where it belongs, and you will notice that I am still here."
:::
