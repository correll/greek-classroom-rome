# The Dative and the Recipient

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Felix had been composing for some minutes, which was unusual, and smiling
while he did it, which was worse.

"I have written you a message," he announced.

"In Greek?"

"In Greek."

Theodoros put down what he was holding. "Read it to the class."

Felix stood, held up his tablet, and read with enormous care:

> **πέμπω τὸν διδάσκαλον.**

He waited. The pause went on slightly too long for his liking.

"What did you intend?"

"*I send a letter to the teacher.*" Felix looked at his tablet again.
"Which is what it says."

"It is not what it says."

"It says **πέμπω** — *I send*. And **τὸν διδάσκαλον** — the teacher. I send,
to the teacher."

"**τὸν διδάσκαλον** is accusative," said Julia, who had got there some
seconds earlier and had been visibly holding it in. "It is the thing being
sent."

Felix read his sentence again.

"*I send the teacher*," said Livia.

"Where?" said Quintus. "Where are you sending him?"

"I am not sending him anywhere —"

"You have not said where," said Theodoros. "That is the only mercy in it.
You have announced to this room that you are dispatching me, as one
dispatches a parcel, to an unspecified destination."

Felix sat down. He appeared to be weighing something.

"It is not even rude," he said at last, with real disappointment. "I would
not have minded if it were rude."

"No," said Theodoros. "It is merely peculiar, and that is worse, and it
happened because the teacher is not the *object* of your sending. He is the
one it goes **to**. Greek has a case for that, and you have not learned it
until this afternoon, so sit still and be peculiar for ten more minutes."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The one it goes to

The **dative** is the fourth and last case you need. Its central job is the
one Felix wanted: it marks the person something is given, sent, said, or
shown **to**.

> **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.** — I send the letter to the teacher.

Three nouns, three different jobs, and each one says which job it has:

- **ἐγώ**, hidden inside **πέμπω** — the one doing it *(nominative)*
- **τὴν ἐπιστολήν** — the thing sent *(accusative)*
- **τῷ διδασκάλῳ** — the one it goes to *(dative)*

English has to use word order and the word *to*. Greek uses an ending, and
can then put the three nouns in whatever order it likes.

## The forms

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **dat. sing.** | **τῷ** | **τῇ** | **τῷ** |
| **dat. plur.** | **τοῖς** | **ταῖς** | **τοῖς** |

| | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **ἡ οἰκία** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **dat. sing.** | **τῷ λόγῳ** | **τῇ ψυχῇ** | **τῇ οἰκίᾳ** | **τῷ δώρῳ** |
| **dat. plur.** | **τοῖς λόγοις** | **ταῖς ψυχαῖς** | **ταῖς οἰκίαις** | **τοῖς δώροις** |

Look at the singular endings and you will see the same small mark under
every one of them: **-ῳ**, **-ῃ**, **-ᾳ**. That is an **iota subscript** —
an iota written underneath, because it stopped being pronounced long before
it stopped being written.

It is easy to miss, it is easy to leave out, and leaving it out turns a
dative into something else. Write it.

::: note
**You now have all four cases.** Here is the whole of it, in one line each:

- **nominative** — the one doing it
- **accusative** — the one it is done to
- **genitive** — whose it is, or where it came from
- **dative** — the one it goes to

Every noun inside a sentence is in one of these four, and tells you which.
(The vocative, for calling to someone, stands outside the sentence; you had
it in Lesson 4.) There is nothing more to come. The rest of Part II is
practice.
:::

## Two more things the dative does

**By means of.** With no preposition at all, the dative can say what
something was done *with*:

> **γράφω τοῖς γράμμασιν.** — I write with letters.

**In, and with.** Two prepositions take the dative and only the dative:

- **ἐν** — in, among
- **σύν** — together with

> **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — in the house
>
> **σὺν τῷ υἱῷ** — together with the son

Note the difference between the two *withs*. **σύν** is *in company with* —
the son is walking beside you. The bare dative is *by means of* — the
letters are the tool. English uses one word for both and leaves you to
guess; Greek makes you decide which you meant.

::: latinbridge
Latin splits this work between two cases. *To the teacher* is dative —
*magistro* — exactly as in Greek. But *with the son* and *by means of
letters* are **ablative** in Latin, and Greek has no ablative.

So Greek's dative is doing the work of two Latin cases, and when you are
translating out of Latin you will have to stop and ask which one you are
looking at. That is not a difficulty to be got around. It is the question
the Greek is asking you.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **δίδωμι** — I give *(third person **δίδωσι** — he, she, or it gives)*
- **πέμπω** — I send
- **ἡ ἐπιστολή** — letter, message
- **ἐν** — in, among *(with the dative)*
- **σύν** — together with *(with the dative)*

::: note
**δίδωμι** does not end in **-ω** like the verbs you know, and it does not
conjugate like them. It belongs to a small, very old, very common family
whose full set of endings this course does not teach. Learn **δίδωμι** and **δίδωσι** as two
whole words for now and do not try to build the rest.

**ἡ ἐπιστολή** goes like **ἡ ψυχή** throughout: **τῆς ἐπιστολῆς**, **τῇ
ἐπιστολῇ**, **τὴν ἐπιστολήν**. It is the ancestor of *epistle*, and the
verb behind it means simply *to send to*.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Felix was permitted to rewrite his message, on the condition that he read
the corrected version aloud as well.

::: {.exercise title="Which case, and why?"}
For each underlined phrase, give the case and say what job it is doing.

1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ κριτῇ.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος δίδωσι τὰ δῶρα τοῖς ἀνθρώποις.**
3. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ γράφομεν.**
4. **τὸ βιβλίον τοῦ υἱοῦ ἐστιν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
:::

::: answers
1. **τὴν ἐπιστολήν** accusative, the thing sent; **τῷ κριτῇ** dative, the
   one it goes to.
2. **τὰ δῶρα** accusative, the things given; **τοῖς ἀνθρώποις** dative, the
   recipients.
3. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** dative, after **ἐν**; the place.
4. **τοῦ υἱοῦ** genitive, whose book it is; **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** dative, where
   it is. All four cases appear in this sentence; ask the class to find the
   nominative.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **δίδωσι τὰ γράμματα τῷ υἱῷ.**
2. **σὺν τῷ φίλῳ βλέπομεν τὴν οἰκίαν.**
3. **οὐ πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τοῖς κριταῖς.**
4. **ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις τοῦ χρόνου ἔχομεν τὰ ἔργα.**
:::

::: answers
1. *He gives the letters to the son.*
2. *Together with the friend we see the house.*
3. *I do not send the letter to the judges.*
4. *In the days of time we have the work.* Deliberately slightly odd in
   English; the pupils should be able to account for all four noun phrases
   even though the sentence is not a thing anyone would say.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I send the letter.
2. I send the letter to the teacher.
3. He gives the gift to the son.
4. In the house.
5. Together with the friends.
6. We do not write to the judge.
:::

::: answers
1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
2. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.**
3. **δίδωσι τὸ δῶρον τῷ υἱῷ.**
4. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
5. **σὺν τοῖς φίλοις.**
6. **οὐ γράφομεν τῷ κριτῇ.**

The iota subscript is the whole examination here. **τῷ διδασκάλω** without
it is not a dative and not anything else either. Mark it as wrong every
time, early, while it is still cheap to fix.

In 6, accept **τῷ κριτῇ** only; a pupil who writes **τὸν κριτήν** has made
Felix's mistake, and should be shown the two side by side rather than
simply corrected.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Felix's message"}
Felix wrote **πέμπω τὸν διδάσκαλον** and meant *I send a letter to the
teacher*.

1. Write what he should have written.
2. Explain, in one sentence, what his version actually claims.
3. Felix said he would not have minded if it had been rude. Why was the
   peculiar version worse than a rude one would have been?
:::

::: answers
1. **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.** The letter must be supplied — his
   original did not contain it — and the teacher must move into the dative.
2. That he is dispatching Theodoros somewhere, as a parcel.
3. This is a question about precision rather than grammar, and worth two
   minutes. A rude sentence would at least have been the sentence he
   intended. The peculiar one was produced by somebody who believed he was
   saying something else, which is the position this whole course is
   designed to get you out of.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Luke 2:14"}
A line you may know in English, and which is built almost entirely out of
datives.

**δόξα ἐν ὑψίστοις θεῷ καὶ ἐπὶ γῆς εἰρήνη ἐν ἀνθρώποις εὐδοκίας.**

- **δόξα** — glory *(nominative)*
- **ἐν ὑψίστοις** — in the highest places *(dative plural, after **ἐν**)*
- **θεῷ** — to God *(dative — the recipient, with no preposition at all)*
- **καί** — and
- **ἐπὶ γῆς** — on earth *(genitive, after **ἐπί**)*
- **εἰρήνη** — peace *(nominative)*
- **ἐν ἀνθρώποις** — among men *(dative plural of your word from Lesson 1)*
- **εὐδοκίας** — of good pleasure, of favour *(genitive)*

*Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among men of good will.*

Two datives doing two different jobs, in the same half-line. **ἐν ὑψίστοις**
is **ἐν** plus the dative, giving place. **θεῷ** is a bare dative with no
preposition whatever, giving the recipient — exactly the ending Felix
needed and did not have.

There is no verb in the whole line. Greek is content to say *glory to God*
and leave the *be* to be understood, which English can also just about
manage.

::: note
**One letter, and a very old argument.** The last word appears in some
manuscripts as **εὐδοκίας**, genitive, and in others as **εὐδοκία**,
nominative. On the first reading the peace is for *men of good will* — the
people God favours. On the second it is *peace on earth, good will toward
men* — which is the version that reached English hymns.

The whole difference is a final **ς**. You are not in a position to judge
which reading is right, and neither are most adults. But you are already in
a position to see that the two readings are different, and why, and that is
further than the argument usually gets.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. The dative covers *to*, *for*, *with*, *by*, and *in*. Is that one case
   doing many jobs, or several cases that happened to collapse into one
   shape?
2. Greek distinguishes *with the son* from *with a pen*. English does not.
   Has English lost something, or shed something?
3. A single **ς** decides who the peace in Luke 2:14 is for. Should a
   translator tell the reader when a choice like that has been made, or get
   on with it?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Felix delivered the corrected message at the end of the hour, folded, with
some ceremony.

It read:

> **πέμπω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ διδασκάλῳ.**

Theodoros turned it over. There was nothing else written on it.

"It is the message," said Felix. "The message is that I am sending it. I
have not sent anything else."

"No," Theodoros agreed. "But the case is correct, and the iota is under the
omega where it belongs, and you will notice that I am still here."
:::
