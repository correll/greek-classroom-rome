# The Genitive Mystery

::: {.story time="5 min"}
A tablet goes missing and reappears in the wrong satchel. Two pupils claim it. The only evidence is a Greek phrase scratched on the back, and the case turns entirely on one ending.

> **To be written.** The scene above is the plan; the prose is not yet
> drafted. See the course notes in `README.md` for the drafting order.
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
**Grammar for this lesson.** The genitive singular and plural in both declensions; possession; the genitive after certain prepositions; **ἐκ**, **ἀπό**.

> **To be written.** Present one main grammatical idea with a paradigm
> table, two or three worked examples, and a **From Latin** box wherever
> the Latin parallel either helps or misleads.
:::

::: vocab
**ὁ υἱός** son · **ἡ θυγάτηρ** daughter · **τὸ ὄνομα** name · **ἐκ / ἐξ** out of · **ἀπό** from · **περί** about, concerning

> **To be checked.** Accentuation, vowel length, and the article for every
> noun must be verified against a lexicon before this lesson is taught.
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min"}
**Text for this lesson.** A short genealogical or dedicatory formula from a real inscription — *X son of Y dedicated this* — where the genitive does the whole job.

> **To be selected and verified.** The passage must be quoted exactly from
> a public-domain edition and cited. Vocabulary beyond the course list must
> be glossed in full. Do not present a composed sentence as a quotation.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
Greek marks possession with an ending; English uses an apostrophe or the word *of*. Which is clearer? Which is shorter? Are those the same question?
:::
