# The Genitive Mystery

::: {.story time="5 min"}
The wax tablet turned up on the third day, in Quintus's satchel, which
Quintus said proved nothing and Felix said proved everything.

"It is mine," said Felix. "It was given to me."

"It was in my bag."

"Bags are not arguments."

Sabina, who had appointed herself to these matters since the business of the
lamp, took the tablet, turned it over, and found something scratched into
the back of the frame in small careful letters.

> **ΤΟΥ ΔΙΔΑΣΚΑΛΟΥ**

"There," said Felix immediately. "*The teacher.* He gave it to me. It says
so."

"It does not say *the teacher*," said Julia.

"It says **ΔΙΔΑΣΚΑΛΟΥ**. That is the teacher. I know that word, I have known
it since the second week —"

"You know the word. You do not know the ending." Julia took the tablet.
"**ὁ διδάσκαλος** is *the teacher*. This is **τοῦ διδασκάλου**. It is a
different ending and it means something different."

"What does it mean?"

"*Of the teacher*," said Livia.

There was a pause while the room worked out what that did to both claims.

"Then it is not mine," said Felix slowly.

"No."

"And it is not *his*."

"No."

Theodoros held out his hand, and after a moment Felix put the tablet into
it.

"One ending," said Theodoros, "settled a three-day argument that two
witnesses, one accusation and a search of three satchels could not. Sit
down and I will show you how it works."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## A third case, and what it is for

You have the **nominative**, for the one doing, and the **accusative**, for
the one it is done to. The third is the **genitive**, and its central job is
the one on Felix's tablet: it says that one thing belongs to, comes from, or
is somehow *of* another.

> **τὸ βιβλίον τοῦ διδασκάλου** — the book of the teacher; the teacher's
> book

Two nouns, each with its own article, standing next to each other. The one
in the genitive is the owner. Nothing else is needed — no word for *of*, no
apostrophe, no preposition. The ending is the whole machinery.

## The forms

The article first, since it is the signpost:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **gen. sing.** | **τοῦ** | **τῆς** | **τοῦ** |
| **gen. plur.** | **τῶν** | **τῶν** | **τῶν** |

**τῶν** is the same for all three genders. This is the first piece of good
news the Greek noun has given you.

And the nouns:

| | **ὁ λόγος** | **ἡ ψυχή** | **ἡ οἰκία** | **τὸ δῶρον** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **gen. sing.** | **τοῦ λόγου** | **τῆς ψυχῆς** | **τῆς οἰκίας** | **τοῦ δώρου** |
| **gen. plur.** | **τῶν λόγων** | **τῶν ψυχῶν** | **τῶν οἰκιῶν** | **τῶν δώρων** |

Note **τοῦ δώρου**. The nominative is **τὸ δῶρον**, with a circumflex; the
genitive has an acute. That is the accent rule from last lesson doing its
work: a circumflex cannot sit on the penultimate syllable when the last
syllable is long, and **-ου** is long. The word has not changed its mind
about anything. It is obeying the rule about weight.

::: note
**The first declension genitive plural always ends in -ῶν, with a
circumflex, whatever the accent of the nominative.** **ἡ οἰκία** is
accented on its second syllable; **τῶν οἰκιῶν** is accented on its last.
**ἡ ἡμέρα** gives **τῶν ἡμερῶν**.

This is the one place in the first declension where the accent does
something you cannot predict from the nominative, so it is the one place
worth memorising deliberately.
:::

## Three prepositions that demand it

Some prepositions insist on a particular case. Three common ones take the
genitive, and all three have to do with *coming away from* something:

- **ἐκ** (**ἐξ** before a vowel) — out of
- **ἀπό** — from, away from
- **περί** — about, concerning

> **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας** — out of the house
>
> **ἀπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου** — from the teacher
>
> **περὶ τῶν ἔργων** — about the deeds

**ἐκ** gives English *ex-*: an *exit* is the way out, an *exodus* is a road
out. **περί** gives *peri-*: a *perimeter* is the measure around.

::: latinbridge
Latin's genitive does the same central job — *liber magistri*, the
teacher's book — and if you have met it, the idea transfers whole.

The warning is about the prepositions. Latin *de*, *ex* and *ab* take the
**ablative**, a case Greek does not have. Greek handed the ablative's work
to the genitive and the dative and kept four cases where Latin has five.
So when a Greek preposition wants the genitive, do not look for an ablative
ending. There is not one.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ υἱός** — son
- **ἡ θυγάτηρ** — daughter
- **τὸ ὄνομα** — name
- **ἐκ**, **ἐξ** — out of *(with the genitive)*
- **ἀπό** — from, away from *(with the genitive)*
- **περί** — about, concerning *(with the genitive)*

::: note
**ὁ υἱός** behaves like **ὁ λόγος**: **τοῦ υἱοῦ**, **τῶν υἱῶν**. Use it
freely.

**ἡ θυγάτηρ** and **τὸ ὄνομα** belong to the third declension and their
genitives are not formed like anything above. Recognise them in the
nominative for now; Part III gives you their endings. A course that teaches
you a form you cannot yet use has wasted your afternoon.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros returned the tablet to its owner, who was himself, and set the
class to work.

::: {.exercise title="Whose?"}
Translate, and say which word is in the genitive and what tells you.

1. **ἡ οἰκία τοῦ κριτοῦ**
2. **τὰ γράμματα τῶν ἀνθρώπων**
3. **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ διδασκάλου**
4. **αἱ ἡμέραι τοῦ χρόνου**
5. **τὸ ἔργον τῶν ψυχῶν**
:::

::: answers
1. *The house of the judge.* **τοῦ κριτοῦ** — **τοῦ** and **-οῦ**.
2. *The letters of the men.* **τῶν ἀνθρώπων** — **τῶν** and **-ων**.
3. *The son of the teacher.* **τοῦ διδασκάλου**.
4. *The days of time.* **τοῦ χρόνου**.
5. *The work of the souls.* **τῶν ψυχῶν** — note the circumflex on the
   ultima, which is where every first-declension genitive plural puts it.

In every one of these the genitive is the second noun, which is the normal
order and not a rule. Pupils should be warned that it can come first.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Out of, from, about"}
Translate.

1. **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας**
2. **ἀπὸ τοῦ υἱοῦ**
3. **περὶ τῶν ἔργων γράφομεν.**
4. **ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας βλέπει τὰ γράμματα.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Out of the house.*
2. *From the son.*
3. *We write about the deeds.*
4. *The man out of the house sees the letters.* — or *from out of the
   house*. The prepositional phrase sits between the subject and its verb,
   which English finds awkward and Greek does not.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The book of the teacher.
2. The sons of the men.
3. Out of the house.
4. We write about the work.
5. The judge's house.  *(same as 1 in form — say why)*
:::

::: answers
1. **τὸ βιβλίον τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
2. **οἱ υἱοὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
3. **ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.**
4. **περὶ τοῦ ἔργου γράφομεν.**
5. **ἡ οἰκία τοῦ κριτοῦ.** — English has two ways of saying this, *the
   house of the judge* and *the judge's house*, and they are the same
   thing. Greek has one, and it is the genitive. A pupil who hunts for a
   second construction to match the second English phrasing is being misled
   by their own language.

The commonest error here is leaving the article off the genitive: **τὸ
βιβλίον διδασκάλου**. It is not meaningless — it says *a book of a teacher*
— but it is not what was asked for.
:::

::: {.exercise title="One ending"}
Felix read **ΤΟΥ ΔΙΔΑΣΚΑΛΟΥ** as *the teacher* and concluded that the
teacher had given him the tablet.

1. What would the Greek have been if the tablet had meant what Felix
   thought?
2. What would it have been if the tablet had meant *the teacher's son*?
3. Felix's reading and the true reading differ by how many letters? What
   does that tell you about reading Greek quickly?
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ διδάσκαλος** — nominative, and it would have been a strange thing to
   scratch on a tablet.
2. **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ διδασκάλου**, or **τοῦ διδασκάλου ὁ υἱός**.
3. Two: **-ος** against **-ου**. The point is not that Felix was careless.
   It is that in Greek the difference between *the teacher* and *of the
   teacher* is carried entirely by the last two letters of the word, so the
   last two letters are not a detail you check afterwards. They are the
   sentence.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 1:1"}
Here is a sentence that is almost nothing but genitives. It is the first
line of the New Testament.

**Βίβλος γενέσεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ υἱοῦ Δαυὶδ υἱοῦ Ἀβραάμ.**

- **βίβλος** — book, record *(nominative — the only one in the sentence)*
- **γενέσεως** — of the origin, of the descent *(genitive; third
  declension, which you meet in Part III)*
- **Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ** — of Jesus Christ *(genitive)*
- **υἱοῦ** — of the son *(genitive — your word, from this lesson)*
- **Δαυίδ** — David
- **Ἀβραάμ** — Abraham

*A record of the descent of Jesus Christ, son of David, son of Abraham.*

There is one nominative, at the front, and then the sentence simply chains
genitives: a record **of** the descent **of** Jesus **of** David **of**
Abraham. Each genitive hangs on the word before it. English needs the word
*of* four times to do what Greek does with four endings.

::: note
**Δαυίδ** and **Ἀβραάμ** do not change at all. They are Hebrew names, taken
into Greek whole, and Greek declined to give them endings — which is why
**υἱοῦ Δαυίδ** has a genitive **υἱοῦ** doing the grammatical work for both
words. When you meet a name that refuses to inflect, it is usually a
foreign one, and that is itself a piece of evidence about where a text came
from.
:::

There is no verb. There is no *is*. The line is a title, in the way that
*The Life of Agricola* is a title, and Greek is entirely comfortable
building one out of a noun and a column of genitives.

Felix's tablet said **ΤΟΥ ΔΙΔΑΣΚΑΛΟΥ** and settled who owned it. This line
uses the same ending to settle who someone is.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Greek marks possession with an ending; English uses an apostrophe or the
   word *of*. Which is clearer? Which is shorter? Are those the same
   question?
2. The genealogy runs backwards — son, then father, then father's father.
   Why might you build a line that way rather than forwards?
3. **ἡ θυγάτηρ** appears in your vocabulary but not in the genealogy. Open
   the question of why, and see how far the class gets before the bell.
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
The tablet went back on Theodoros's shelf, where it had been for eleven
years before Felix borrowed it without asking.

Some time later Julia noticed that the scratched letters were not in
Theodoros's hand. They were small and careful and slightly crooked, the way
a person writes who is learning, and they had been there a long time.

She did not ask whose they were. She had begun to understand that some of
the things in that room had been owned by somebody before.
:::
