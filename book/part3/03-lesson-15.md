# The Words of Homer

::: {.story time="5 min"}
The scroll Theodoros brought out had been repaired three times and smelled

The class knew what it was before he said anything. They had been promised
it since the first week, in the way that a thing is promised to children

Felix actually applauded.

"The first?"

"This is the most important poem in the history of the

wants to settle an argument he quotes this. When a soldier scratches a line
on a wall at the edge of the world, it is usually this."

"And the second thing?"

Theodoros turned the scroll towards them, and the class leaned in, and
there was a silence of about four seconds.

"That is not Greek," said Marcus.

"It is Greek."

"There is no **ὁ**." Julia had found it first, as usual, and sounded
personally affronted. "There is no article anywhere. You said the article

"I did."

"Then where is it?"

"Four hundred years in the future," said Theodoros. "Homer is older than the

you are going to find out that it is a dialect, which is a thing nobody can
be told. It has to happen to you."

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Greek is not one language

The Greek you have been learning is **Attic** — the speech of Athens in the
fifth and fourth centuries BC, and the standard for prose ever afterwards.

Homer is **epic** Greek: older, Ionic in its base, and never anybody's
everyday speech even when it was new. It is a poetic dialect, assembled over
centuries of oral performance, and it kept old forms because the verse
needed them.

So the differences you are about to meet are not mistakes, and they are not
sloppiness. They are a different stage and a different variety of the same
language.

## Four differences, and why they exist

**One. The article is mostly absent.** Homer has **ὁ, ἡ, τό**, but they are
not yet articles; they are demonstratives, meaning *that one*, *he*, *she*.
The word you have learned to lean on had not finished becoming itself.

This means Homeric Greek cannot do the trick of Lesson 10. There is no
attributive position, because there is no article to be inside or outside
of. Position and sense have to do the work.

**Two. Vowels that Attic contracted are left open.** Attic **ᾄδω**, *I
sing*, is a contraction. Homer has **ἀείδω**, with both vowels still
sounding, and the imperative **ἄειδε**. Attic squeezed; Homer had not yet.

**Three. Endings that Attic simplified survive.** Genitives in **-οιο** and
**-εω**, datives in **-οισι** and **-ῃσι**, where Attic has **-ου**, **-ου**,
**-οις** and **-αις**. They are longer, and in verse that is often exactly why they
were kept.

**Four. Word order is far freer, because the metre is in charge.** Homer is
in hexameters, and a poet composing aloud to a rhythm will put a word where
the rhythm wants it. You cannot read Homer by expecting Attic prose order.

::: note
**This is why the course taught you Attic first.** Homer is not harder than
Attic in its grammar; it is harder because it is *different*, and you cannot
see a difference without a standard to see it against.

You now have the standard. That is what the last fourteen lessons were for.
:::

## The third declension, at last

Homer's nouns, and most of this lesson's, belong to the declension you have
been told to wait for since Lesson 4. Here it is, on **ὁ ἀνήρ**, *man*.

| | singular | plural |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **nominative** | **ὁ ἀνήρ** | **οἱ ἄνδρες** |
| **accusative** | **τὸν ἄνδρα** | **τοὺς ἄνδρας** |
| **genitive** | **τοῦ ἀνδρός** | **τῶν ἀνδρῶν** |
| **dative** | **τῷ ἀνδρί** | **τοῖς ἀνδράσι(ν)** |

The endings are the thing to learn, because they are the same for most
masculine and feminine nouns of this declension (the ones whose stem ends
in a consonant): **-ς** or nothing, **-α**,
**-ος**, **-ι**; then **-ες**, **-ας**, **-ων**, **-σι(ν)**. Neuters, as
always, have nominative and accusative alike, and **-α** in the plural
(**τὸ ὄνομα**, **τὰ ὀνόματα**).

What makes this declension awkward is not the endings but the **stem**:
**ἀνήρ** hides it, **ἀνδρ-** shows it. The nominative is often the one form
that looks nothing like the rest. That is why a dictionary gives you the
genitive alongside — **ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός** — and why, from here on, this book
does the same for every third-declension word it introduces. Learn the two
forms together and the other six follow.

You have already met several of these without their endings: **ὁ μάρτυς,
μάρτυρος** (Lesson 4), **ἡ θυγάτηρ, θυγατρός** and **τὸ ὄνομα, ὀνόματος**
(Lesson 8). The article in front of each has been telling you the case all
along; now the noun can too.

## Formulas

Homer is built out of repeated phrases — **πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς**, *Odysseus
of many wiles*; *swift-footed Achilles*; *the wine-dark sea* — and these are
not laziness.
They are the technique of a poet composing in performance, with a fixed
rhythm to fill, who needs a phrase of exactly the right shape to hand.

When you meet the same two or three words about the same man for the twentieth
time, you are seeing the machinery of oral poetry, not a shortage of
imagination.

::: latinbridge
Latin has nothing quite like this, because Latin literature begins in
writing and in imitation of Greek. Virgil opens *Arma virumque cano* — *arms
and the man I sing* — and that first line is a deliberate echo of the two
you are about to read.

If you have met the *Aeneid*, you have met Homer at one remove, in a poet
who expected you to notice.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ὁ ἀνήρ** — man *(as against a woman; third declension)*
- **ἡ ναῦς** — ship *(irregular; recognise it)*
- **ὁ οἶκος** — house, home; **οἶκόνδε** — homeward
- **πολύτροπος** — of many turns; much-travelled; resourceful
- **ἄειδε** — sing! *(imperative; Attic would contract this)*
- **ἡ μῆνις** — wrath, anger *(of a god or a hero; not ordinary temper)*
- **ὁ ἑταῖρος** — companion, comrade
- **ἡ πόλις** — city *(third declension, irregular: genitive **πόλεως**,
  accusative plural **πόλεις**; recognise it)*
- **πᾶς, πᾶσα, πᾶν** — all, every; the whole *(third declension; genitive
  **παντός**; plural **πάντες, πάντα**)*
- **ὁ θυμός** — spirit, heart; the seat of anger and courage
- **ὁ ἥλιος** — sun; **ὁ Ἥλιος** — the Sun, as a god
- **ἐσθίω** — I eat *(imperfect **ἤσθιον**)*
- **εἶδον** — I saw *(aorist; its present, **ὁράω**, is a verb of a kind
  you meet in Part VI — use **βλέπω** for *I see* until then)*
- **ἔπαθον** — I suffered, I experienced *(aorist of **πάσχω**)*

::: note
**ὁ ἀνήρ** is not **ὁ ἄνθρωπος**. **ἄνθρωπος** is a human being;
**ἀνήρ** is a man as against a woman, and a man of a certain kind — a
warrior, a husband, somebody acting like one. The *Odyssey* opens with
**ἄνδρα**, not **ἄνθρωπον**, and the choice is the first thing the poem
tells you.

**πολύτροπος** is **πολύ-** (much) + **τρόπος** (turn, way). *Of many
turns.* It can mean widely travelled, and it can mean devious, and the
*Odyssey* spends twenty-four books declining to say which it meant.
:::
:::

::: {.exercise title="Attic or Homeric?"}
For each pair, say which form is Attic and which is Homeric, and what
changed.

1. **ᾄδω** / **ἀείδω**
2. **τοῦ λόγου** / **τοῖο λόγοιο**
3. **τοῖς ἀνθρώποις** / **ἀνθρώποισι**
4. **ὁ ἀνήρ** / **ἀνήρ** *(in the middle of a sentence, meaning "the man")*
5. **ἦμαρ** / **ἡμέρα**
6. **ἴδεν** / **εἶδεν**
7. **Ἠελίοιο** / **Ἡλίου**
8. **πάθεν** / **ἔπαθεν**
9. **ἀτασθαλίῃσιν** / **ἀτασθαλίαις** *(the word means "acts of folly")*
10. **Τροίης** / **Τροίας**
:::

::: answers
1. **ᾄδω** Attic, **ἀείδω** Homeric. Attic contracted the two vowels; Homer
   left them open.
2. **τοῦ λόγου** Attic; **τοῖο λόγοιο** Homeric, with the older genitive in
   **-οιο**. Longer, and useful in a hexameter.
3. **τοῖς ἀνθρώποις** Attic; **ἀνθρώποισι** Homeric, with the older dative
   plural and no article.
4. The Attic needs **ὁ** to mean *the man*; Homer does not have the article
   in that use, so bare **ἀνήρ** can carry it. A pupil who says "the
   Homeric one is missing a word" should be asked whether a language can be
   missing a word it has not invented yet.
5. **ἦμαρ** Homeric, **ἡμέρα** Attic. This time it is not an ending but a
   whole different word for *day*; epic kept an old noun that Attic prose
   dropped.
6. **ἴδεν** Homeric, **εἶδεν** Attic. The augment is optional in Homer, and
   here it is missing. A pupil who says *the Homeric one is not past* should
   be told that it is, and that the metre decided whether the augment
   appeared.
7. **Ἠελίοιο** Homeric: uncontracted **Ἠελι-** and the genitive **-οιο**.
   **Ἡλίου** Attic, contracted and shortened. Two of the four differences in
   one word.
8. **πάθεν** Homeric, **ἔπαθεν** Attic. The same point as 6: the augment
   left off.
9. **ἀτασθαλίῃσιν** Homeric, with the old dative plural **-ῃσι(ν)** and
   **η** where Attic keeps **α** after **ι**; **ἀτασθαλίαις** is how Attic
   would spell it. (Attic would not in fact say it — it is a poetic word —
   but the ending is the point.)
10. **Τροίης** Homeric, **Τροίας** Attic. Ionic turned every long **α** into
    **η**; Attic kept **α** after **ε, ι, ρ**, which is why **ἡ οἰκία** has
    the genitive **οἰκίας** and not **οἰκίης**. A pupil who has wondered
    since Lesson 8 why there are two patterns of first declension has just
    been handed the answer.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
Attic sentences, using this lesson's vocabulary.

1. **ὁ ἀνὴρ ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
2. **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνὴρ εἶπε τοὺς λόγους τῆς ἀληθείας.**
3. **ἡ μῆνις τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἦν μεγάλη.** *(**τοῦ ἀνδρός** = genitive of
   **ἀνήρ**)*
4. **οἱ ἑταῖροι τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἤσθιον τοὺς βοῦς τοῦ θεοῦ.** *(**τοὺς βοῦς** =
   the cattle)*
5. **πολλὰ ἔπαθεν ὁ ἀνὴρ ἐν τῷ θυμῷ.**
6. **εἶδον τὰς πόλεις πολλῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
7. **πάντες οἱ ἄνδρες ἦλθον εἰς τὴν πόλιν.**
8. **ἡ ναῦς ἦν μεγάλη, ἀλλὰ οἱ ἄνδρες ἦσαν μικροί.**
9. **ὁ ἥλιος ἔβλεπε πάντα τὰ ἔργα τῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
10. **ἡ θυγάτηρ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἔφερε τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ θεοῦ.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The man came into the house.*
2. *The man of many turns said the words of truth.* — **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνήρ**
   is attributive: inside the article group, describing.
3. *The wrath of the man was great.* — note **μεγάλη**, the feminine of
   **μέγας**, agreeing with **μῆνις**.
4. *The man's companions were eating the god's cattle.* — imperfect: they
   kept at it, which in the poem is the whole trouble.
5. *The man suffered many things in his heart.* — **πολλά** is neuter
   plural used as a noun, *many things*; aorist, because the suffering is
   summed up as one whole. English wants *much*.
6. *I saw the cities of many men.* Or *they saw* — **εἶδον** is an **-ον**
   form and ambiguous like the rest. **τὰς πόλεις** is the irregular
   accusative plural from the vocabulary box; the article tells the case
   even if the ending is strange.
7. *All the men came into the city.* — **πάντες** agrees with **οἱ
   ἄνδρες**; **πόλιν** is accusative singular, regular **-ν** on a stem in
   **-ι**.
8. *The ship was big, but the men were small.* — two imperfects of
   **εἰμί**, two states; **μικροί** masculine plural for **ἄνδρες**.
9. *The sun was watching all the deeds of men.* — **πάντα τὰ ἔργα**, neuter
   plural, accusative because **ὁ ἥλιος** has the nominative.
10. *The man's daughter bore the god's name.* — **θυγάτηρ** and **ὄνομα**
    are the two third-declension nouns you have had since Lesson 8 without
    the table; now you can see **ἀνδρός** beside them.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
In Attic, not in Homeric.

1. The man came home.
2. The wrath of the man was great.
3. The men were always singing.
4. The man saw the city.
5. All the companions were eating.
6. The men suffered many bad things.
7. The wrath of the god was in the heart of the man.
8. The companions of the man were not wise.
9. The sun sees all men.
10. The man of many turns came to the house of his companions.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ ἀνὴρ ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον.**
2. **μεγάλη ἦν ἡ μῆνις τοῦ ἀνδρός.** — or **ἡ μῆνις τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἦν
   μεγάλη**. Predicate position either way.
3. **οἱ ἄνδρες ἀεὶ ᾖδον.** — accept **οἱ ἄνθρωποι** with a comment; the
   whole point of the vocabulary note is that the two are not the same word.
4. **ὁ ἀνὴρ εἶδε τὴν πόλιν.** — watch for **ἔβλεψε**, which is not wrong
   but is not the word the lesson gave; and for **εἶδον**, which is the
   wrong person.
5. **πάντες οἱ ἑταῖροι ἤσθιον.** — imperfect, for *were eating*; the
   augment on **ἐσθίω** is **ἠ-**, as the vocabulary shows.
6. **οἱ ἄνδρες πολλὰ κακὰ ἔπαθον.** — **πολλὰ κακά**, neuter plural, *many
   bad things*. Aorist: the sufferings as one sum.
7. **ἡ μῆνις τοῦ θεοῦ ἦν ἐν τῷ θυμῷ τοῦ ἀνδρός.** — two genitives, one
   dative; a pupil who puts **θυμός** in the accusative after **ἐν** has
   confused it with **εἰς**.
8. **οἱ ἑταῖροι τοῦ ἀνδρὸς οὐκ ἦσαν σοφοί.** — **σοφοί** plural, to agree.
9. **ὁ ἥλιος βλέπει πάντας τοὺς ἄνδρας.** — **πάντας** and **ἄνδρας** carry
   the same third-declension accusative plural **-ας**, which is the thing
   to point out: the adjective and the noun are in the same declension for
   once and show it.
10. **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνὴρ ἦλθεν εἰς τὸν οἶκον τῶν ἑταίρων.** — Greek has no
    word for *his* here and does not need one.

Ask why they were told to write Attic. The answer is that they cannot yet
write Homeric and should not pretend to: imitating a dialect you cannot
read is how bad editions get made.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Julia's objection"}
Julia said that Homer had no article and that Theodoros had told them the
article was the load-bearing wall.

1. Was she right about Homer?
2. Was Theodoros wrong in Lesson 7?
3. How can both be true at once?
4. Homer does have the words **ὁ, ἡ, τό**. In line 9 of the Reading below
   (**αὐτὰρ ὁ τοῖσιν ἀφείλετο**), what does **ὁ** mean?
5. Find one noun in the original lines of the Reading that is accusative
   without any article, and say what told you its case.
6. Theodoros said the article was "four hundred years in the future".
   Roughly when was Homer composed, and when was Attic prose written?
7. Julia wrote the rule down in Lesson 7. Is a rule written down in Lesson
   7 wrong if Lesson 15 finds a text it does not fit? Answer in one
   sentence.
8. Which other rule from Part II can Homer not use, because it depends on
   the article?
9. Does Homeric Greek have *more* grammar than Attic, or *less*?
10. Write one Attic sentence of your own, then write it again with every
    article taken out. Can it still be understood? What was lost?
:::

::: answers
1. Essentially yes. Homer has the forms but uses them as demonstratives, not
   as the article of Attic prose.
2. No — he was describing Attic, which is what they were learning.
3. Because a language changes, and a rule about one stage of it is not a
   rule about all of them. This is the single most important idea in Part
   III and it is worth taking the full five minutes. A pupil who concludes
   that the grammar they learned was *wrong* should be asked whether a map
   of Rome is wrong because it does not show Athens.
4. *He* — it is a pronoun standing on its own, with no noun after it. In
   Attic prose **ὁ** cannot do that. This is what "not yet an article"
   looks like on the page.
5. **ἄνδρα**, **νόστον**, **ψυχήν**, **βοῦς**, **ἦμαρ** — any one. The
   ending told you (**-α**, **-ον**, **-ην**), or in the case of **ἦμαρ**,
   nothing did except the sense; which is itself the lesson.
6. Homer: about 700 BC, give or take a century, from much older material.
   Attic prose: the fifth and fourth centuries BC. Three to four hundred
   years, as Theodoros said; about the gap between Shakespeare and us.
7. No — a rule describes the texts it was made from, and Lesson 7's rule
   was made from Attic. Accept any sentence that distinguishes *wrong* from
   *limited*.
8. The attributive and predicate positions of Lesson 10. With no article
   there is no *inside the group* and *outside the group*, and Homer must
   show the difference another way, or not show it. (The New Greek says
   this; the question checks that they read it.)
9. Neither — different. More endings (the **-οιο** and **-οισι** forms, the
   missing augments) and fewer articles. A pupil who says *more* because
   there are more forms, or *less* because there is no article, has half
   the answer and should hear the other half from someone else.
10. Answers will vary. Usually it can still be understood, because the
    endings survive; what is lost is definiteness (*the* against *a*), the
    marking of the subject when two nominatives stand together, and the
    attributive–predicate distinction. Have them say which of the three
    their sentence lost. That is the inventory of what the article does.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Homer, *Odyssey* 1.1 and *Iliad* 1.1"}
Two openings. Between them they begin European literature, and they are
built on the same frame.

**ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον**

- **ἄνδρα** — the man *(accusative — the object, and the first word)*
- **μοι** — to me *(dative)*
- **ἔννεπε** — tell! *(imperative)*
- **Μοῦσα** — Muse *(vocative — Lesson 4)*
- **πολύτροπον** — of many turns *(accusative, agreeing with **ἄνδρα**)*

*Tell me of the man of many turns, Muse.*

**μῆνιν ἄειδε, θεά, Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος**

- **μῆνιν** — the wrath *(accusative — the object, and the first word)*
- **ἄειδε** — sing! *(imperative, uncontracted)*
- **θεά** — goddess *(vocative)*
- **Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος** — of Peleus' son Achilles *(genitive, Homeric)*

*Sing, goddess, of the wrath of Achilles, son of Peleus.*

**The same shape, twice.** Object first. Then a command to a goddess. Then
the thing described. Both poems announce their subject in the first word and
then ask somebody else to supply it — the poet does not claim to be the one
who knows.

**Now count the articles.** There are none. Not one, in either line. The
word you have built fourteen lessons on is simply not there, and the lines are
not ambiguous, because **ἄνδρα** and **μῆνιν** are accusative and say so
with their endings.

**And look at Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος.** Two genitives, neither of them Attic:
Attic would have **Πηλείδου Ἀχιλλέως**. These are older forms, kept because
the hexameter wanted their shape. You can see they are genitives — the
sense and the **-ος** are enough — without being able to produce them.

That is the right relationship to have with Homer at this stage. Read, with
help. Do not imitate.

**Homer, *Odyssey* 1.1–10**

ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ  
πλάγχθη, ἐπεὶ Τροίης ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον ἔπερσεν·  
πολλῶν δ᾽ ἀνθρώπων ἴδεν ἄστεα καὶ νόον ἔγνω,  
πολλὰ δ᾽ ὅ γ᾽ ἐν πόντῳ πάθεν ἄλγεα ὃν κατὰ θυμόν,  
ἀρνύμενος ἥν τε ψυχὴν καὶ νόστον ἑταίρων.  
ἀλλ᾽ οὐδ᾽ ὣς ἑτάρους ἐρρύσατο, ἱέμενός περ·  
αὐτῶν γὰρ σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο,  
νήπιοι, οἳ κατὰ βοῦς Ὑπερίονος Ἠελίοιο  
ἤσθιον· αὐτὰρ ὁ τοῖσιν ἀφείλετο νόστιμον ἦμαρ.  
τῶν ἁμόθεν γε, θεά, θύγατερ Διός, εἰπὲ καὶ ἡμῖν.

::: gloss
- **ἡ Μοῦσα** — the Muse *(vocative **Μοῦσα** in Homer)*
- **ἡ Τροία** — Troy *(Homeric genitive **Τροίης**)*
- **ὁ νόστος** — homecoming *(next lesson's word)*
- **ἡ θεά** — goddess *(vocative **θεά**)*
- **τοὺς βοῦς** — the cattle *(accusative plural of **ὁ βοῦς**)*
- **ἔννεπε** — tell! *(as in the Voice)*
- **ὅς** — who *(the relative pronoun; Lesson 17)*
- **μάλα πολλά** — very much, very far
- **πλάγχθη** — was driven to wander *(aorist; take it whole)*
- **ἐπεί** — when, after
- **ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον** — the holy city *(**πτολίεθρον** is epic for
  **πόλις**)*
- **ἔπερσεν** — he sacked *(aorist of **πέρθω**)*
- **δ᾽** — **δέ**, and, but *(never first in its clause; Lesson 17)*
- **ἴδεν** — he saw *(= **εἶδεν**, without the augment)*
- **ἄστεα** — cities *(neuter plural of **τὸ ἄστυ**)*
- **νόον** — mind, way of thinking *(accusative; Attic **νοῦν**)*
- **ἔγνω** — he came to know *(aorist of **γιγνώσκω**; irregular)*
- **ὅ γ᾽** — he *(**ὁ** as a pronoun, with **γε** for emphasis)*
- **ἐν πόντῳ** — on the sea
- **πάθεν** — he suffered *(= **ἔπαθεν**, without the augment)*
- **ἄλγεα** — pains, griefs *(neuter plural; Attic **ἄλγη**)*
- **ὃν κατὰ θυμόν** — in his heart *(**ὅν** here = his own)*
- **ἀρνύμενος** — striving to win *(a participle, Part IV; take it whole)*
- **ἥν τε** — and his own *(**ἥν** agrees with **ψυχήν**; **τε** = and)*
- **ἑταίρων, ἑτάρους** — of his companions, his companions *(the shorter
  stem is Homeric)*
- **οὐδ᾽ ὥς** — not even so
- **ἐρρύσατο** — he saved *(aorist; take it whole)*
- **ἱέμενός περ** — though he longed to *(a participle; take it whole)*
- **αὐτῶν … σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν** — by their own reckless folly
  *(dative plural, Homeric **-ῃσιν**)*
- **γάρ** — for *(never first in its clause; Lesson 17)*
- **ὄλοντο** — they perished *(aorist; take it whole)*
- **νήπιοι** — fools, children
- **οἵ** — who *(plural of **ὅς**)*
- **κατὰ … ἤσθιον** — were eating up *(**κατά** belongs with the verb;
  **ἤσθιον** is the imperfect of **ἐσθίω**)*
- **Ὑπερίονος Ἠελίοιο** — of Hyperion the Sun *(two Homeric genitives;
  Attic **Ἡλίου**)*
- **αὐτάρ** — but, and then
- **ὁ** — he *(the article as a pronoun)*
- **τοῖσιν** — from them *(dative plural; Attic **τοῖς**)*
- **ἀφείλετο** — took away *(aorist; take it whole)*
- **νόστιμον ἦμαρ** — the day of homecoming *(**ἦμαρ** is epic for
  **ἡμέρα**)*
- **τῶν ἁμόθεν γε** — of these things, from some point or other
- **θύγατερ Διός** — daughter of Zeus *(vocative of **θυγάτηρ**; **Διός**
  is the genitive of **Ζεύς**)*
- **εἰπέ** — tell! *(aorist imperative of **εἶπον**; Lesson 26)*
- **καὶ ἡμῖν** — to us too *(dative of **ἡμεῖς**, we)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted version aloud, then the Homer, slowly. Then answer.

1. In the adapted version, who tells the story — the poet or the Muse?
   Find the verb and its subject. In the original, who is asked to tell
   it, and by whom?
2. **πολλὰ ἔπαθεν** — which tense, and why not an imperfect?
3. The adapted version says **ἐγίγνωσκε τὰς ψυχὰς τῶν ἀνθρώπων**; Homer
   says **νόον ἔγνω**. What is different about the tense, and what does
   each one claim?
4. Why did the companions not get home? Give the Greek sentence of the
   adapted version that says so.
5. Find the one imperfect verb in the Homer. What is its present, and what
   does the imperfect tell you about the eating?
6. Find the two genitives in the Homer that an Attic writer would spell
   differently, and give the Attic spelling.
7. **αὐτὰρ ὁ τοῖσιν ἀφείλετο** — what does **ὁ** mean here? What tells you
   it is not an article?
8. Count the articles in each version.
9. **θύγατερ Διός** — what case is **θύγατερ**, and what word from Lesson 8
   is it?
10. The adaptation made the Muse the subject of a statement; Homer makes
    her the object of a command. What has the adaptation lost? Is there
    anything it has gained?
:::

::: answers
1. In the adapted version, the Muse: **ἡ Μοῦσα λέγει**. In Homer, the Muse
   is *asked* to tell it — **ἔννεπε**, a command — by the poet, who says
   **μοι**, *to me*, and never *I*.
2. Aorist. The sufferings of ten years are summed up as one thing that
   happened to him, looked at whole. An imperfect would have us watch him
   suffering; the aorist puts it on the record and moves on. (Homer makes
   the same choice: **πάθεν**.)
3. The adaptation has an imperfect, *he was getting to know* — a process
   that went on as he travelled. Homer has an aorist, **ἔγνω**, *he came
   to know*: the knowing as an achievement. Both are defensible; they say
   different things about the man, and the Attic sentence was written
   knowing the Homeric one is sharper.
4. They ate the cattle of the Sun: **οἱ ἑταῖροι ἤσθιον τοὺς βοῦς τοῦ
   Ἡλίου**, and so **ὁ Ἥλιος ἔλαβε τὴν ἡμέραν τοῦ νόστου** — the Sun took
   away the day of their homecoming.
5. **ἤσθιον**, line 9; present **ἐσθίω**. They *kept* eating — it was not
   one meal but a habit, which is why it was a crime and not a mistake. A
   pupil who hears that the one imperfect in ten lines is the one that
   dooms the companions has heard Homer.
6. **Τροίης** (Attic **Τροίας**) and **Ἠελίοιο** (**Ἡλίου**). The other
   genitives — **πολλῶν ἀνθρώπων**, **ἑταίρων**, **αὐτῶν**, **τῶν**,
   **Ὑπερίονος**, **Διός** — are spelt as Attic would spell them; a pupil
   who offers **Ὑπερίονος** has found a Homeric word in an Attic form,
   which is worth saying. **Πηληϊάδεω** from the Voice is a third, if
   anyone remembers it.
7. *He* — the Sun. It stands alone, with no noun following, and is the
   subject of **ἀφείλετο**. An Attic article cannot do that; a Homeric one
   can, because it is still a pronoun.
8. Adapted: twenty-seven. Homer: none — **ὁ** in line 9 and **ὅ γ᾽** in line
   4 are pronouns, and a pupil who counts them should be asked what noun
   each one goes with. The exact count of the adapted version matters less
   than the contrast.
9. Vocative — she is being addressed. It is **ἡ θυγάτηρ**, *daughter*, from
   Lesson 8, with the vocative's own short form; you have now seen a third
   declension noun in every case.
10. Lost: the command, and with it the poet's refusal to be the one who
    knows; the first word **ἄνδρα**, which Homer puts before anything else
    and the Attic buries in the middle; the relative **ὅς** that chains
    the man to everything he did; the reason (**γάρ**) and the pity
    (**νήπιοι**). Gained: nothing, except that you could read it — which
    was the point, and is why it was labelled.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Homer asks a goddess to tell the story. Virgil, seven hundred years later,
   writes *I sing*. What changed?
2. The *Iliad* begins with wrath and the *Odyssey* with a man. Does a poem's
   first word commit it to anything?
3. You have learned a dialect and have just been shown that it is one. Does
   that make the last fourteen lessons worth less, or more?

Sabina asked whether she could learn Homeric Greek instead.

Theodoros said she could learn it afterwards, in about two years, and that
people who tried to start there generally ended up able to recite forty
lines and unable to read a letter.

She said that forty lines sounded quite good.

He said that it did, and that this was precisely the trouble with it.
