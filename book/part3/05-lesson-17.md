# What Makes a Just Person?

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Quintus had been asking for Plato since Lesson 5.

He had asked in the way that children ask for things they have decided are
owed to them: at intervals, with increasing legal precision, and always just
after being told something else was more important first. Theodoros had put
him off six times.

On the seventh he did not.

"Sit down," he said. "You have been promised a philosopher and you are about
to get one, and I want to tell you what is going to happen before it
happens, because otherwise you will conclude that you are stupid, and you are
not stupid, and I would rather not spend the afternoon on it."

Quintus sat down with the expression of somebody who has been handed a sword
and warned about the handle.

"You will know every word," said Theodoros. "That is the first thing. There
is no vocabulary in this sentence you have not had. **ὁ** you have had.
**βίος** is new and I will give it to you. **ἄνθρωπος** you have had since
the first week."

"Then where is the difficulty?"

"In the small words."

He wrote one sentence on the board. It was seven words long.

Quintus looked at it for some time.

"I can translate all of it," he said at last, "and I do not know what it
means."

"No."

"I know what every word means."

"Yes."

"That is *worse*," said Quintus, with feeling. "That is much worse than not
knowing."

Julia had been reading it from the back of the room. "It starts with
**δέ**," she said. "The second word is **δέ**."

"It does."

"So it is answering something. You have given us half of an argument."

"I have given you half of an argument," Theodoros agreed, "and I did it on
purpose, and the half I have withheld is the half every translation into
Latin also withholds, which is why educated men have been quoting this
sentence wrongly for four hundred years." He put the chalk down. "Philosophy
in Greek is not difficult because the words are long. It is difficult because
the words that carry the argument are two or three letters each, and nobody thinks
they matter, and they are the only things that do."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The words that hold an argument together

Greek prose is *chained*. In a page of Plato, almost every sentence after the
first begins with a small word tying it to the one before — *and*, *but*,
*for*, *therefore*. A Greek sentence that begins with no connector at all is
doing something deliberate and unusual, and a reader notices it.

English is not like this. English can set two sentences side by side and
leave the reader to work out the join. Greek nearly always says what the join
is.

| Word | What it does | Usually English |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **δέ** | and, and so; the next step, often with a mild contrast | *but*, *and*, or nothing |
| **μέν … δέ** | balances two things against each other | *on the one hand … on the other* — or just a comma |
| **γάρ** | gives the *reason* for what was just said | *for*, *because* |
| **οὖν** | draws a *conclusion* from what was just said | *so*, *therefore* |
| **ἀλλά** | sharp contrast; cancels what came before | *but* |

Learn them as a set of road signs. **γάρ** means *here comes the evidence*.
**οὖν** means *here comes the conclusion*. **δέ** means *next*. If you can see
those three coming, you can follow an argument in Greek before you can
translate it.

## They do not come first

Here is the thing that will trip you for a month.

**δέ**, **γάρ**, **οὖν** and **μέν** are **postpositive**: they cannot stand
first in their clause. They take second place, behind whatever word the
writer wants to emphasise.

- **ὁ δὲ ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *And the man speaks.*
- **ὁ γὰρ ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *For the man speaks.*
- **ὁ οὖν ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *So the man speaks.*

In each case the English connector goes at the front and the Greek one does
not. When you translate, the word you pick up second you will put down first.

**ἀλλά** is the exception. It does stand first, and it is the strongest of
the five: it does not advance an argument, it overturns one.

## μέν … δέ

**μέν** is a promise. It says: *there is a second half coming*. The second
half arrives with **δέ**.

**ὁ μὲν διδάσκαλος λέγει, οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι ἀκούουσιν.**

*The teacher speaks; the people listen.*

You can translate it as *on the one hand … on the other*, and for a first
pass you should, because it forces you to see the structure. Then stop,
because no English speaker has ever said it that way about anything. A comma
usually does the work. Sometimes *while*. Sometimes a full stop and a slight
change of tone.

::: note
**μέν is almost untranslatable, and it is not meaningless.** It tells a
reader that the sentence is not finished being balanced. English carries that
information in the voice — in the lift you put on *the teacher* when you know
*the pupils* are coming. Greek writes it down.
:::

## The pronoun that is the article in disguise

Two more small words, and then the sentence.

The **relative pronoun** — *who*, *which*, *that* — is **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ**. Look at
it beside the article you learned in Lesson 3.

| | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Nominative sg.** | **ὅς** | **ἥ** | **ὅ** |
| **Accusative sg.** | **ὅν** | **ἥν** | **ὅ** |
| **Genitive sg.** | **οὗ** | **ἧς** | **οὗ** |
| **Dative sg.** | **ᾧ** | **ᾗ** | **ᾧ** |
| **Nominative pl.** | **οἵ** | **αἵ** | **ἅ** |
| **Accusative pl.** | **οὕς** | **ἅς** | **ἅ** |
| **Genitive pl.** | **ὧν** | **ὧν** | **ὧν** |
| **Dative pl.** | **οἷς** | **αἷς** | **οἷς** |

It is the article with the **τ** knocked off and a rough breathing put on
instead. **τόν** becomes **ὅν**. **τῶν** becomes **ὧν**. **τοῖς** becomes
**οἷς**. You have known this paradigm since Lesson 3 without knowing you knew
it.

**ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν δίκαιός ἐστιν.** — *The man who speaks the
truth is just.*

The **demonstrative** is **οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο** — *this*, *this one*. You
chiefly need three forms now: **οὗτος** (*this man*), **τοῦτο** (*this
thing*), and the neuter plural **ταῦτα** (*these things*), which is one of
the commonest words in Greek philosophy because it is how a philosopher says
*all of the above*.

::: latinbridge
**Latin does this too, and does it less.** *Enim* (for), *igitur*
(therefore) and *autem* (however) are postpositive in exactly the same way,
so the habit of picking a word up second and putting it down first is one you
may already have.

*Quī, quae, quod* matches **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ** closely enough that you can usually
construe a Greek relative clause by Latin instinct alone.

**Where Latin misleads:** Latin is comfortable with sentences that simply
begin. Caesar writes whole paragraphs with no connective at all. If you read
Greek expecting that freedom you will skate over the **γάρ** and the **οὖν**
as though they were noise, and you will lose the argument while translating
every word of it correctly.

And Latin has nothing for **μέν**. There is no Latin word to look up. You
have to hear it.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **δίκαιος** — just, righteous
- **ἄδικος** — unjust
- **ἡ δικαιοσύνη** — justice
- **ὁ βίος** — life, a life as it is lived
- **μέν … δέ** — on the one hand … on the other *(both postpositive)*
- **γάρ** — for, because *(postpositive)*
- **οὖν** — therefore, so *(postpositive)*
- **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ** — who, which

::: note
**ἄδικος** is **δίκαιος** with the **ἀ-** of Lesson 4 stuck on the front:
*not-just*. The same prefix you met in **ἀλήθεια**.

**ὁ βίος is not ἡ ζωή.** You met **ζωή** in Lesson 5: life as against death,
the bare fact of being alive. A plant has **ζωή**.

**βίος** is a life as somebody lives it — a career, a manner of living, the
shape of the years. It is what a biography is a writing of. Only **βίος**
can be examined, and that is why Socrates uses it and not the other word.

**ἡ δικαιοσύνη** is built out of **δίκαιος** with the ending **-σύνη**, which
turns an adjective into the name of the quality. English does the same thing
with *-ness*: *just* becomes *justice*, *kind* becomes *kindness*. Greek did
it first and more tidily.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros made them read each sentence aloud before translating it, and stop
at the connector.

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ μὲν δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ δὲ ἄδικος ψεύδεται.**
2. **οὐ γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν· οὐ γὰρ ἀκούομεν.**
3. **ὁ ἄδικος οὖν οὐ δύναται φίλος εἶναι.**
4. **τὸ βιβλίον ὃ ἔχω καλόν ἐστιν.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The just man speaks the truth, but the unjust man lies.* — or *the just
   man … the unjust one …*, with a comma doing the work of **μέν … δέ**.
   Accept *on the one hand … on the other* on a first pass and ask for a
   second.
2. *We do not know the truth; for we do not listen.* — **γάρ** is second
   word in its clause and gives the reason. A pupil who translates *for we
   do not listen* has it; a pupil who leaves **γάρ** out has lost the whole
   point of the sentence, which is that the second clause explains the
   first.
3. *The unjust man, therefore, is not able to be a friend.* — **οὖν** is
   second word and draws the conclusion. **εἶναι** is the infinitive from
   Lesson 16, completing **δύναται**.
4. *The book which I have is good.* — **ὅ** is neuter because **βιβλίον** is
   neuter, and accusative because it is the object of **ἔχω**. Both facts
   come from the same two letters.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
These three sentences use the same eight words. Only the small word changes.

- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ γὰρ παῖς λέγει.**
- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ δὲ παῖς λέγει.**
- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ οὖν παῖς λέγει.**

*(**ὁ παῖς** = the boy.)*

1. Translate all three.
2. For each one, say what relationship the second clause has to the first —
   evidence, next step, or conclusion.
3. Which of the three is the only one that could not be true?
:::

::: answers
1. *The man is not speaking; for the boy is speaking.* / *The man is not
   speaking, but the boy is.* / *The man is not speaking; so the boy is
   speaking.*
2. **γάρ** = evidence: the boy's speaking is the reason we know the man is
   not. **δέ** = next step, with a contrast. **οὖν** = conclusion: because
   the man is not speaking, the boy must be.
3. The third. **οὖν** claims that the boy's speaking *follows from* the man's
   silence, and it does not; somebody else could be speaking, or nobody.
   The first two are fine.

This is the exercise to spend time on. The words are identical, the grammar
is identical, and the logic is three different shapes. A class that can hear
that difference is ready for Plato; a class that cannot will translate Plato
accurately and understand none of him.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The just man speaks the truth.
2. The teacher speaks; the people listen.
3. Therefore justice was a gift of god.
4. We know that the unjust man lies.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — accept **ὁ δίκαιος ἄνθρωπος**; the
   adjective can stand on its own as a noun, which is worth saying aloud.
2. **ὁ μὲν διδάσκαλος λέγει, οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι ἀκούουσιν.** — a version without
   **μέν … δέ** is not wrong, but ask why the Greek would want them. The
   answer is that the sentence is a balance and Greek likes to say so.
3. **ἡ δικαιοσύνη οὖν δῶρον ἦν τοῦ θεοῦ.** — **οὖν** must be second. A
   sentence beginning **οὖν ἡ δικαιοσύνη** is the single most predictable
   error in this lesson and should be corrected every time, kindly, until it
   stops.
4. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν ἄδικον ψεύδεσθαι.** — accusative and infinitive, from
   Lesson 16. Watch for **ὁ ἄδικος**, which is the mistake of somebody
   translating word by word out of English.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Plato, *Apology* 38a"}
Socrates is on trial for his life. He has been convicted. He is explaining
why he will not accept exile and silence in place of death.

**ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.**

- **ὁ** — the
- **δέ** — and, but *(postpositive; second word, as always)*
- **ἀνεξέταστος** — unexamined *(**ἀ-** not + **ἐξετάζω** I examine)*
- **βίος** — life
- **οὐ** — not
- **βιωτός** — livable, worth living *(an adjective built straight off the
  verb **βιόω**, I live)*
- **ἀνθρώπῳ** — for a human being *(dative, and with no article: for anyone
  who is human)*

*But the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being.*

**There is no verb.** Not one. Greek does not need **ἐστίν** here, exactly as
it did not need it in Lesson 3: the article tells you which word is the
subject, and the rest follows. Seven words, no verb, and the most quoted
sentence in Greek philosophy.

**Now the second word.**

It is **δέ**. The sentence does not begin; it *continues*. Socrates is
answering something he has just said, and the **δέ** is the hinge. Strip it
out and you have a slogan — a thing to put on a wall. Leave it in and you
have the second half of an argument, which is what Socrates was actually
making.

Almost every translation drops it. "The unexamined life is not worth living"
is how the sentence lives in English, and that sentence is missing the one
word that tells you it is a reply.

**And look at ἀνθρώπῳ.** Dative, no article — *for a human being*. Not for
Socrates, not for philosophers, not for Athenians. He has been told he could
keep his life if he would stop asking questions, and his answer is that the
thing he is being offered is not a human life. The whole weight of the
sentence sits on a word in the dative at the very end.

**That is Greek.** The vocabulary was easy. The argument was carried by
**δέ**, by a missing verb, and by the absence of an article.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** Check the line against your text of the
*Apology*; the wording here is given from memory of the standard text and the
punctuation of the surrounding passage varies between editions.

**On what precedes it.** Socrates has just said that the greatest good for a
human being is to spend each day discussing virtue and examining himself and
others — and that his jury will believe this even less than what he has
already told them. The **δέ** picks up that sentence. You do not need to
teach the context in Greek; it is enough that the class knows the sentence is
an answer, because the grammar has already told them so.

**If a pupil asks whether the standard English is a mistranslation**, the
honest answer is that it is not wrong about the words and is wrong about the
shape — which is exactly the distinction Lesson 18 is about, and is worth
saving for it.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Quintus could translate every word and did not know what the sentence
   meant. Is that a failure of his Greek, or has he just discovered what
   reading is?
2. **μέν** cannot be translated and is not meaningless. Name something in
   English that works the same way — something a reader hears that the words
   do not say.
3. Socrates says an unexamined life is not worth living *for a human being*.
   He does not say it is a bad life. Is that a different claim?

Afterwards Quintus stayed behind, which he never did.

"The sentence before it," he said. "The one you did not give us. What does it
say?"

"It says that the greatest good for a man is to spend every day talking about
goodness and examining himself and other people." Theodoros was rolling the
scroll. "And then it says that the jury will find that even harder to believe
than everything else he has told them."

"So he knew."

"He knew."

"He knew they would not believe him, and he said it anyway, and then he said
the famous part, and then they killed him."

"That is the order of events."

Quintus thought about this for a while.

"Sir," he said, "I would like to go on with the philosophers."

"I thought you might," said Theodoros.
