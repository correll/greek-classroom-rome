# What Makes a Just Person?

::: story
Quintus had been asking for Plato since Lesson 1.

He had asked in the way that children ask for things they have decided are
owed to them: at intervals, with increasing legal precision, and always just
after being told something else was more important first. Theodoros had put
him off six times.

On the seventh he did not.

"Sit down," he said. "You have been promised a philosopher and you are about
to get one, and I want to tell you what is going to happen before it
happens, because otherwise you will conclude that you are stupid, and you are
not stupid, and I would rather not spend the lesson on it."

Quintus sat down with the expression of somebody who has been handed a sword
and warned about the handle.

"You will know nearly every word," said Theodoros. "That is the first
thing. There are three words in this sentence you have not had, and I will
give you all three. **ὁ** you have had. **βίος** is one of the three.
**ἄνθρωπος** you have had since Lesson 1."

"Then where is the difficulty?"

"In the small words."

He wrote one sentence on the board. It was seven words long.

Quintus looked at it for some time.

"I can translate all of it," he said at last, "and I do not know what it
means."

"No."

"I know what every word means."

"Yes."

"That is *worse*," said Quintus, with feeling. "That is much worse than not
knowing."

Julia had been reading it from the back of the room. "It starts with
**δέ**," she said. "The second word is **δέ**."

"It does."

"So it is answering something. You have given us half of an argument."

"I have given you half of an argument," Theodoros agreed, "and I did it on
purpose, and the half I have withheld is the half every translation into
Latin also withholds, which is why educated men have been quoting this
sentence wrongly for four hundred years." He put the charcoal down. "Philosophy
in Greek is not difficult because the words are long. It is difficult because
the words that carry the argument are two or three letters each, and nobody thinks
they matter, and they are the only things that do."
:::

::: newgreek
## The words that hold an argument together

Greek prose is *chained*. In a page of Plato, almost every sentence after the
first begins with a small word tying it to the one before — *and*, *but*,
*for*, *therefore*. A Greek sentence that begins with no connector at all is
doing something deliberate and unusual, and a reader notices it.

English is not like this. English can set two sentences side by side and
leave the reader to work out the join. Greek nearly always says what the join
is.

| Word | What it does | Usually English |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **δέ** | and, and so; the next step, often with a mild contrast | *but*, *and*, or nothing |
| **μέν … δέ** | balances two things against each other | *on the one hand … on the other* — or just a comma |
| **γάρ** | gives the *reason* for what was just said | *for*, *because* |
| **οὖν** | draws a *conclusion* from what was just said | *so*, *therefore* |
| **ἀλλά** | sharp contrast; cancels what came before | *but* |

Learn them as a set of road signs. **γάρ** means *here comes the evidence*.
**οὖν** means *here comes the conclusion*. **δέ** means *next*. If you can see
those three coming, you can follow an argument in Greek before you can
translate it.

## They do not come first

Here is the thing that will trip you again and again.

**δέ**, **γάρ**, **οὖν** and **μέν** are **postpositive**: they cannot stand
first in their clause. They take second place, behind whatever word the
writer wants to emphasise.

- **ὁ δὲ ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *And the man speaks.*
- **ὁ γὰρ ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *For the man speaks.*
- **ὁ οὖν ἄνθρωπος λέγει.** — *So the man speaks.*

In each case the English connector goes at the front and the Greek one does
not. When you translate, the word you pick up second you will put down first.

**ἀλλά** is the exception. It does stand first, and it is the strongest of
the five: it does not advance an argument, it overturns one.

## μέν … δέ

**μέν** is a promise. It says: *there is a second half coming*. The second
half arrives with **δέ**.

**ὁ μὲν διδάσκαλος λέγει, οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι ἀκούουσιν.**

*The teacher speaks; the people listen.*

You can translate it as *on the one hand … on the other*, and for a first
pass you should, because it forces you to see the structure. Then stop,
because no English speaker has ever said it that way about anything. A comma
usually does the work. Sometimes *while*. Sometimes a full stop and a slight
change of tone.

::: note
**μέν is almost untranslatable, and it is not meaningless.** It tells a
reader that the sentence is not finished being balanced. English carries that
information in the voice — in the lift you put on *the teacher* when you know
*the pupils* are coming. Greek writes it down.
:::

## The pronoun that is the article in disguise

Two more small words, and then the sentence.

The **relative pronoun** — *who*, *which*, *that* — is **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ**. Look at
it beside the article you learned in Lesson 3.

| | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Nominative sg.** | **ὅς** | **ἥ** | **ὅ** |
| **Accusative sg.** | **ὅν** | **ἥν** | **ὅ** |
| **Genitive sg.** | **οὗ** | **ἧς** | **οὗ** |
| **Dative sg.** | **ᾧ** | **ᾗ** | **ᾧ** |
| **Nominative pl.** | **οἵ** | **αἵ** | **ἅ** |
| **Accusative pl.** | **οὕς** | **ἅς** | **ἅ** |
| **Genitive pl.** | **ὧν** | **ὧν** | **ὧν** |
| **Dative pl.** | **οἷς** | **αἷς** | **οἷς** |

It is the article with the **τ** knocked off and a rough breathing put on
instead. **τόν** becomes **ὅν**. **τῶν** becomes **ὧν**. **τοῖς** becomes
**οἷς**. You have known this paradigm since Lesson 3 without knowing you knew
it.

**ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν δίκαιός ἐστιν.** — *The man who speaks the
truth is just.*

The **demonstrative** is **οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο** — *this*, *this one*. You
chiefly need three forms now: **οὗτος** (*this man*), **τοῦτο** (*this
thing*), and the neuter plural **ταῦτα** (*these things*), which is one of
the commonest words in Greek philosophy because it is how a philosopher says
*all of the above*.

::: latinbridge
**Latin does this too, and does it less.** *Enim* (for), *igitur*
(therefore) and *autem* (however) are postpositive in exactly the same way,
so the habit of picking a word up second and putting it down first is one you
may already have.

*Quī, quae, quod* matches **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ** closely enough that you can usually
construe a Greek relative clause by Latin instinct alone.

**Where Latin misleads:** Latin is comfortable with sentences that simply
begin. Caesar writes whole paragraphs with no connective at all. If you read
Greek expecting that freedom you will skate over the **γάρ** and the **οὖν**
as though they were noise, and you will lose the argument while translating
every word of it correctly.

And Latin has only *quidem* for **μέν**, which does a fraction of the work.
There is no word to look up that will hand you the sense. You have to hear
it.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **δίκαιος** — just, righteous
- **ἄδικος** — unjust
- **ἡ δικαιοσύνη** — justice
- **ὁ βίος** — life, a life as it is lived
- **ὁ παῖς** — child, boy *(third declension; genitive **παιδός**)*
- **μέν … δέ** — on the one hand … on the other *(both postpositive)*
- **γάρ** — for, because *(postpositive)*
- **οὖν** — therefore, so *(postpositive)*
- **ὅς, ἥ, ὅ** — who, which
- **οὗτος, αὕτη, τοῦτο** — this, this one; **ταῦτα** — these things *(it stands
  outside the article group: **οὗτος ὁ παῖς**, this boy)*
- **ὅτι** — that *(introducing what somebody says or knows — the second way
  of Lesson 16)*; because
- **ὑμεῖς** — you *(plural; the dative **ὑμῖν** was in Lesson 11)*
- **ὡς** — as, like; *(with a verb of saying)* how, that

::: note
**ἄδικος** is **δίκη**, *justice, right*, with the **ἀ-** of Lesson 4 stuck
on the front: *not-just*. The same root gives **δίκαιος**. The same prefix you met in **ἀλήθεια**.

**ὁ βίος is not ἡ ζωή.** You met **ζωή** in Lesson 5: life as against death,
the bare fact of being alive. A plant has **ζωή**.

**βίος** is a life as somebody lives it — a career, a manner of living, the
shape of the years. It is what a biography is a writing of. Only **βίος**
can be examined, and that is why Socrates uses it and not the other word.

**ἡ δικαιοσύνη** is built out of **δίκαιος** with the ending **-σύνη**, which
turns an adjective into the name of the quality. English does the same thing
with *-ness*: *just* becomes *justice*, *kind* becomes *kindness*. Greek did
it first and more tidily.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory. Nothing here is from this lesson; the imperfects
and aorists are mixed on purpose.

1. **ἔπιεν** — tense; what told you?
2. **ἐδίδασκον** — tense and person.
3. Translate **ὁ νόστος**.
4. **τοῖς ἀνδράσι** — case and number, and the nominative.
5. Turn into the imperfect: **ὁ ξένος πίνει τὸν οἶνον.**
6. **βούλεται μανθάνειν** — translate, and say which job the infinitive is
   doing.
7. **ἦμεν** — translate.
8. Which is Homeric, **Τροίης** or **Τροίας**?
9. **λέγει τὸν ξένον ἐλθεῖν** — translate.
10. **ἔδωκε** — translate. What is its present?
:::

::: answers
1. Aorist — the stem **πι-** is not the present **πιν-**. (Lesson 16.) A
   pupil who says *imperfect because of the ending* has looked at the
   wrong end again.
2. Imperfect, 1st singular or 3rd plural — *I was teaching* or *they were
   teaching*. Present stem, augment. (Lesson 13.) **ἐδίδαξα** would be the
   aorist.
3. *Homecoming.* (Lesson 16.)
4. Dative plural of **ὁ ἀνήρ** — *to the men*. (Lesson 15.) The article
   gives it away before the ending does.
5. **ὁ ξένος ἔπινε τὸν οἶνον.** (Lessons 13 and 16.) **ἔπιε** is the aorist
   and the predictable error.
6. *He wants to learn.* Job one — the infinitive completes **βούλεται**
   and carries no time. (Lesson 16.)
7. *We were.* (Lesson 13.) Not *they were*, which is **ἦσαν**.
8. **Τροίης** — Ionic **η** for Attic **α**. (Lesson 15.)
9. *He says that the stranger came.* Job two: accusative and aorist
   infinitive, so the coming is in the past. (Lesson 16.) *Is coming*
   would need **ἔρχεσθαι**.
10. *He gave.* Aorist of **δίδωμι**. (Lesson 16.) A pupil who offers
    *he gives* has the right verb and the wrong tense.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ μὲν δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ δὲ ἄδικος ψεύδεται.**
2. **οὐ γιγνώσκομεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν· οὐ γὰρ ἀκούομεν.**
3. **ὁ ἄδικος οὖν οὐ δύναται φίλος εἶναι.**
4. **τὸ βιβλίον ὃ ἔχω καλόν ἐστιν.**
5. **ὁ παῖς ὃν ὁ διδάσκαλος ἐδίδασκε νῦν σοφός ἐστιν.**
6. **οὗτος ὁ ἀνὴρ δίκαιος ἦν· τὴν γὰρ ἀλήθειαν ἀεὶ ἔλεγεν.**
7. **ἡ μὲν δικαιοσύνη καλή ἐστιν, ὁ δὲ βίος τοῦ ἀδίκου κακός.**
8. **οἱ παῖδες οὖν ἦλθον εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν τοῦ δικαίου.**
9. **ταῦτα λέγω· ὑμεῖς δὲ οὐκ ἀκούετε.**
10. **ὁ Σωκράτης λέγει ὅτι ὁ βίος ὃν οὐκ ἐξετάζει ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐκ ἀγαθός
    ἐστιν.** *(**ὁ Σωκράτης** = Socrates; **ἐξετάζει** = he examines)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The just man speaks the truth, but the unjust man lies.* — or *the just
   man … the unjust one …*, with a comma doing the work of **μέν … δέ**.
   Accept *on the one hand … on the other* on a first pass and ask for a
   second.
2. *We do not know the truth; for we do not listen.* — **γάρ** is second
   word in its clause and gives the reason. A pupil who translates *for we
   do not listen* has it; a pupil who leaves **γάρ** out has lost the whole
   point of the sentence, which is that the second clause explains the
   first.
3. *The unjust man, therefore, is not able to be a friend.* — **οὖν** is
   second word and draws the conclusion. **εἶναι** is the infinitive from
   Lesson 16, completing **δύναται**.
4. *The book which I have is good.* — **ὅ** is neuter because **βιβλίον** is
   neuter, and accusative because it is the object of **ἔχω**. Both facts
   come from the same single letter.
5. *The boy whom the teacher was teaching is now wise.* — **ὅν** is
   masculine for **παῖς** and accusative as the object of **ἐδίδασκε**; the
   imperfect says the teaching went on for some time, and **νῦν** marks
   where it stopped. A pupil who makes the boy the subject of
   **ἐδίδασκε** has not asked what case **ὅν** is.
6. *This man was just; for he always spoke the truth.* — **οὗτος** outside
   the article group, as the vocabulary says; **γάρ** second in its clause,
   behind **τήν**, giving the evidence. Imperfect with **ἀεί**, as in
   Lesson 13.
7. *Justice is beautiful, but the life of the unjust man is bad.* — **μέν …
   δέ** balancing two clauses; **ἐστιν** is left out of the second half,
   which Greek does whenever the first half has supplied it.
8. *So the children went to the house of the just man.* — **οὖν** second;
   **τοῦ δικαίου**, the adjective standing as a noun, Lesson 10's trick.
   **οἱ παῖδες** is the third-declension plural from the vocabulary.
9. *I say these things; but you do not listen.* — **ταῦτα**, neuter plural,
   *these things*; **δέ** with a contrast, and **ὑμεῖς** written out
   because the contrast is between *I* and *you*. Socrates's complaint, in
   six words.
10. *Socrates says that the life which a man does not examine is not good.*
    — **ὅτι** reports the words (the second way of Lesson 16), and inside
    the report there is a relative clause: **ὅν** accusative, object of
    **ἐξετάζει**. Two small words, two jobs, and the sentence of the Voice
    rebuilt out of pieces the class has.
:::

::: {.exercise title="What tells you that?"}
These three sentences use the same eight words. Only the small word changes.

- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ γὰρ παῖς λέγει.**
- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ δὲ παῖς λέγει.**
- **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ οὖν παῖς λέγει.**

*(**ὁ παῖς** = the boy.)*

1. Translate all three.
2. For each one, say what relationship the second clause has to the first —
   evidence, next step, or conclusion.
3. Which of the three is the only one that could not be true?
4. In the second sentence, could **δέ** be translated *and* rather than
   *but*? What decides?
5. Rewrite the first sentence with **γάρ** as the first word of its clause.
   What is wrong with it?
6. **ὁ παῖς ὃς λέγει** / **ὁ παῖς ὃν βλέπω** — the relative has changed
   case. What changed it?
7. Now begin the sentence **ὁ μὲν ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει …**. What does a reader
   expect next, and which of the three second clauses can supply it?
8. **τοῦτο λέγω** / **ταῦτα λέγω** — translate both. What is the
   difference, and where is it carried?
9. **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ἀλλὰ ὁ παῖς λέγει.** — **ἀλλά** stands first.
   What does this version claim that the **δέ** version does not?
10. Write a fourth version with **οὖν** that *could* be true — a conclusion
    that does follow from the man's not speaking. You will need to change
    the verb of the second clause.
:::

::: answers
1. *The man is not speaking; for the boy is speaking.* / *The man is not
   speaking, but the boy is.* / *The man is not speaking; so the boy is
   speaking.*
2. **γάρ** = evidence: the boy's speaking is the reason we know the man is
   not. **δέ** = next step, with a contrast. **οὖν** = conclusion: because
   the man is not speaking, the boy must be.
3. The third. **οὖν** claims that the boy's speaking *follows from* the man's
   silence, and it does not; somebody else could be speaking, or nobody.
   The first two are fine.
4. Yes. **δέ** is the mildest of the connectors and often means no more than
   *and the next thing is*. What decides is the content: here the two
   clauses are opposites (*not speaking* / *speaking*), so the contrast is
   in the facts and English wants *but*. A pupil who insists **δέ** always
   means *but* will mistranslate half of Plato.
5. **γὰρ ὁ παῖς λέγει** — and it is not Greek. **γάρ** cannot stand first;
   it needs a word to lean on. This is the error to correct every time,
   kindly, until it stops.
6. Its job in its own clause. In the first, the boy is the subject of
   **λέγει**, so **ὅς** is nominative; in the second he is the object of
   **βλέπω**, so **ὅν** is accusative. **ὁ παῖς** is nominative in both,
   and that is the point: the relative takes its case from its own clause,
   not from the word it refers back to. Gender and number come from the
   antecedent; case does not.
7. A second half with **δέ**. **μέν** is the promise, and only the **δέ**
   sentence keeps it: **ὁ μὲν ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει, ὁ δὲ παῖς λέγει**. The
   **γάρ** and **οὖν** versions leave the promise unkept, and a Greek
   reader would wait for the second half and never get it.
8. *I say this* / *I say these things*. Singular against plural, carried
   by **-ο** against **-α** (and the stem's **ου** against **αυ**) — the neuter endings of Lesson 7. A
   philosopher's **ταῦτα** is *all of the above*; **τοῦτο** is one point.
9. That the boy's speaking *cancels* or *corrects* the man's silence — as
   if somebody expected the man to speak and the boy spoke instead. **δέ**
   only moves on; **ἀλλά** overturns. A pupil who hears no difference
   should say both aloud with the stress English puts on *but*.
10. **ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐ λέγει· ὁ οὖν παῖς οὐκ ἀκούει.** — *The man is not
    speaking; so the boy is not listening.* That follows: there is nothing
    to hear. Accept **οὐ μανθάνει**, *is not learning*, on the same
    reasoning. A pupil who leaves **λέγει** and merely moves **οὖν** has
    not understood what question 3 was asking.

This is the exercise that matters most. The words are identical, the grammar
is identical, and the logic is three different shapes. A class that can hear
that difference is ready for Plato; a class that cannot will translate Plato
accurately and understand none of him.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. The just man speaks the truth.
2. The teacher speaks; the people listen.
3. Therefore justice was a gift of god.
4. We know that the unjust man lies.
5. This boy is just.
6. The life of the unjust man is not good; for he always lies.
7. The teacher, who was teaching the children, said these things.
8. We do not listen; therefore we do not know the truth.
9. The judge says that this man is unjust. *(use **ὅτι**)*
10. The man whom we see is a stranger.
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ δίκαιος λέγει τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — accept **ὁ δίκαιος ἄνθρωπος**; the
   adjective can stand on its own as a noun, which is worth saying aloud.
2. **ὁ μὲν διδάσκαλος λέγει, οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι ἀκούουσιν.** — a version without
   **μέν … δέ** is not wrong, but ask why the Greek would want them. The
   answer is that the sentence is a balance and Greek likes to say so.
3. **ἡ δικαιοσύνη οὖν δῶρον ἦν τοῦ θεοῦ.** — **οὖν** must be second. A
   sentence beginning **οὖν ἡ δικαιοσύνη** is the single most predictable
   error in this lesson and should be corrected every time, kindly, until it
   stops.
4. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν ἄδικον ψεύδεσθαι.** — accusative and infinitive, from
   Lesson 16. Watch for **ὁ ἄδικος**, which is the mistake of somebody
   translating word by word out of English.
5. **οὗτος ὁ παῖς δίκαιός ἐστιν.** — **οὗτος** stands outside the article
   group, never **ὁ οὗτος παῖς**. Say this once, plainly: it is simply how
   the demonstrative behaves, and it will look like a breach of Lesson 10
   until it is said.
6. **ὁ βίος τοῦ ἀδίκου οὐκ ἀγαθός ἐστιν· ἀεὶ γὰρ ψεύδεται.** — **γάρ**
   second, behind **ἀεί**. Accept **ψεύδεται γὰρ ἀεί** with **γάρ** still
   second; do not accept it first.
7. **ὁ διδάσκαλος, ὃς τοὺς παῖδας ἐδίδασκε, ταῦτα εἶπεν.** — **ὅς**
   nominative, the subject of its own clause; **τοὺς παῖδας**, the
   third-declension accusative plural; **ἐδίδασκε** imperfect for *was
   teaching* and **εἶπεν** aorist for *said*. Two past tenses, each where
   it belongs.
8. **οὐκ ἀκούομεν· τὴν οὖν ἀλήθειαν οὐ γιγνώσκομεν.** — **οὖν** second,
   behind **τήν**. Watch for **οὐκ οὖν γιγνώσκομεν**: it puts **οὖν**
   second, and it is also, to a Greek ear, a different word — **οὐκοῦν**,
   *so then …?* — which is why the object was moved to the front. Do not
   teach **οὐκοῦν**; just steer round it.
9. **ὁ κριτὴς λέγει ὅτι οὗτος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἄδικός ἐστιν.** — the **ὅτι**
   way, because the infinitive way needs the accusative of **οὗτος**,
   **τοῦτον**, which the class has not had. A pupil who guesses **τοῦτον
   τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἄδικον εἶναι** has guessed right and should be told so.
10. **ὁ ἀνὴρ ὃν βλέπομεν ξένος ἐστίν.** — **ὅν** accusative as the object
    of **βλέπομεν**, masculine for **ἀνήρ**. Watch for **ὅς**, from a pupil
    who gave the relative the case of its antecedent; this is the error
    question 6 of the last exercise was written to catch.
:::

::: {.voice source="Plato, *Apology* 38a"}
Socrates is on trial for his life. He has been convicted. He is explaining
why he will not accept exile and silence in place of death.

**ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.**

- **ὁ** — the
- **δέ** — and, but *(postpositive; second word, as always)*
- **ἀνεξέταστος** — unexamined *(**ἀ-** not + **ἐξετάζω** I examine)*
- **βίος** — life
- **οὐ** — not
- **βιωτός** — livable, worth living *(an adjective built straight off the
  verb **βιόω**, I live)*
- **ἀνθρώπῳ** — for a human being *(dative, and with no article: for anyone
  who is human)*

*But the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being.*

**There is no verb.** Not one. Greek does not need **ἐστίν** here, exactly as
it did not need it in Lesson 3: the article tells you which word is the
subject, and the rest follows. Seven words, no verb, and the most quoted
sentence in Greek philosophy.

**Now the second word.**

It is **δέ**. The sentence does not begin; it *continues*. Socrates is
answering something he has just said, and the **δέ** is the hinge. Strip it
out and you have a slogan — a thing to put on a wall. Leave it in and you
have the second half of an argument, which is what Socrates was actually
making.

Almost every translation drops it. "The unexamined life is not worth living"
is how the sentence lives in English, and that sentence is missing the one
word that tells you it is a reply.

**And look at ἀνθρώπῳ.** Dative, no article — *for a human being*. Not for
Socrates, not for philosophers, not for Athenians. He has been told he could
keep his life if he would stop asking questions, and his answer is that the
thing he is being offered is not a human life. The whole weight of the
sentence sits on a word in the dative at the very end.

**That is Greek.** The vocabulary was easy. The argument was carried by
**δέ**, by a missing verb, and by the absence of an article.

**Verify before teaching.** Check the line against your text of the
*Apology*; the wording here follows the standard text (see *Sources*), and
the punctuation of the surrounding passage varies between editions.

**On what precedes it.** Socrates has just said that the greatest good for a
human being is to spend each day discussing virtue and examining himself and
others. The **δέ** picks up that sentence. He then adds — after the famous
words, not before them — that his jury will believe this even less than
what he has already told them. You do not need to
teach the context in Greek; it is enough that the class knows the sentence is
an answer, because the grammar has already told them so.

**If a pupil asks whether the standard English is a mistranslation**, the
honest answer is that it is not wrong about the words and is wrong about the
shape — which is exactly the distinction Lesson 18 is about, and is worth
saving for it.
:::

::: {.reading title="The half Theodoros withheld" source="Plato, *Apology* 37e–38a (Burnet's Oxford text), with an adapted version"}
The Voice gave you seven words. Here is the sentence they sit in, and the
one that follows it — twice. The first version was written for you, in
Attic, with the words and forms you have; it is **adapted**, and labelled
so. Socrates is speaking to the five hundred men of his jury, and he calls
them what every Athenian speaker called a jury. The second is Plato.

**Adapted**

ὦ ἄνδρες, ἐγὼ μὲν λέγω ὅτι ὁ θεὸς βούλεταί με λέγειν καὶ ὅτι οὐ δύναμαι οὖν
ἡσυχίαν ἔχειν· ὑμεῖς δὲ λέγετε ὅτι ψεύδομαι.

λέγω δὲ καὶ τοῦτο· μέγα ἀγαθόν ἐστι τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ πᾶσαν ἡμέραν περὶ τῆς ἀρετῆς
λέγειν καὶ περὶ τῶν ἄλλων ἃ ἀεὶ ἀκούετε. ἐγὼ γὰρ ἀεὶ ἐξετάζω καὶ ἐμαυτὸν
καὶ τοὺς ἄλλους. ὁ δὲ βίος ὃν οὐκ ἐξετάζει ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὐκ ἔστι βίος
ἀνθρώπῳ. ταῦτα δὲ λέγω, ὑμεῖς δὲ ἔτι οὐκ ἀκούετε.

οὕτως οὖν ἔχει, ὦ ἄνδρες, ὡς ἐγὼ λέγω· χαλεπὸν δέ ἐστι τοὺς ἀνθρώπους
διδάσκειν.

**Plato, *Apology* 38a**

ἐάντε γὰρ λέγω ὅτι τῷ θεῷ ἀπειθεῖν τοῦτ᾽ ἐστὶν καὶ διὰ τοῦτ᾽ ἀδύνατον
ἡσυχίαν ἄγειν, οὐ πείσεσθέ μοι ὡς εἰρωνευομένῳ· ἐάντ᾽ αὖ λέγω ὅτι καὶ
τυγχάνει μέγιστον ἀγαθὸν ὂν ἀνθρώπῳ τοῦτο, ἑκάστης ἡμέρας περὶ ἀρετῆς τοὺς
λόγους ποιεῖσθαι καὶ τῶν ἄλλων περὶ ὧν ὑμεῖς ἐμοῦ ἀκούετε διαλεγομένου καὶ
ἐμαυτὸν καὶ ἄλλους ἐξετάζοντος, ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ,
ταῦτα δ᾽ ἔτι ἧττον πείσεσθέ μοι λέγοντι. τὰ δὲ ἔχει μὲν οὕτως, ὡς ἐγώ φημι,
ὦ ἄνδρες, πείθειν δὲ οὐ ῥᾴδιον.

::: gloss
- **ὦ ἄνδρες** — gentlemen *(vocative plural of **ἀνήρ**; how a speaker
  addressed an Athenian jury)*
- **με, μοι, ἐμοῦ** — me, to me, of me *(the accusative, dative and
  genitive of **ἐγώ**; the first two are enclitic)*
- **ἡ ἡσυχία** — quiet, stillness; **ἡσυχίαν ἔχειν** / **ἄγειν** — to keep
  quiet, to live quietly
- **ἡ ἀρετή** — excellence, virtue *(Lesson 2)*
- **ἐξετάζω** — I examine, I cross-question *(the verb inside
  **ἀνεξέταστος**)*
- **ἐμαυτόν** — myself *(accusative)*
- **ἔχει οὕτως** — it is so *(**ἔχω** with an adverb means *to be in a
  state*: **καλῶς ἔχει**, it is well)*
- **ἐάντε … ἐάντ᾽ αὖ** — both if … and if again *(**ἐάν**, *if*, is
  followed by a verb in the subjunctive, Part VI; here **λέγω** is one and
  happens to look like the present)*
- **τῷ θεῷ ἀπειθεῖν** — to disobey the god *(**ἀ-** + **πείθομαι**, I obey;
  infinitive, with the dative)*
- **τοῦτ᾽ ἐστίν** — this is *(**τοῦτο** with its last vowel cut off)*
- **διὰ τοῦτο** — because of this *(**διά** with the accusative)*
- **ἀδύνατον** — impossible *(**ἀ-** + **δύναμαι**)*
- **πείσεσθέ μοι** — you will believe me *(a future of **πείθομαι**; take
  it whole)*
- **ὡς εἰρωνευομένῳ** — as a man not meaning what he says *(a participle
  agreeing with **μοι**; take it whole — the word gave English *irony*)*
- **τυγχάνει … ὄν** — really is, happens to be *(**τυγχάνω** with a
  participle; take it whole)*
- **μέγιστον** — greatest *(superlative of **μέγας**)*
- **ἑκάστης ἡμέρας** — each day *(genitive, for the time within which)*
- **τοὺς λόγους ποιεῖσθαι** — to hold conversations *(a middle infinitive
  of **ποιέω**, I make; take it whole)*
- **περὶ ὧν** — about which *(genitive plural of **ὅς**)*
- **ἀκούετε διαλεγομένου** — you hear me conversing *(**ἀκούω** takes the
  genitive of the person heard; **διαλεγομένου** is a participle agreeing
  with **ἐμοῦ**; take it whole)*
- **ἐξετάζοντος** — examining *(a participle, agreeing with **ἐμοῦ**; take
  it whole)*
- **ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ** — the Voice's sentence
- **ἔτι ἧττον** — even less *(**ἧττον**, less)*
- **λέγοντι** — when I say it *(a participle agreeing with **μοι**; take it
  whole)*
- **τὰ δέ** — but these things *(**τά** as a pronoun, as Homer's **ὁ** in
  Lesson 15; Attic keeps the old use in **ὁ μέν … ὁ δέ** phrases)*
- **φημί** — I say *(a **-μι** verb, Part VI; **ὡς ἐγώ φημι**, as I say)*
- **πείθειν** — to persuade *(**πείθω**, Lesson 26)*
- **ῥᾴδιον** — easy *(supply **ἐστίν**)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted version aloud, then the Plato, slowly. Then answer.

1. **ὦ ἄνδρες** — who is being addressed, and in what case? Find the same
   two words in Plato.
2. Find every **δέ**, **γάρ** and **οὖν** in the adapted version. For each,
   say where it stands in its clause and which road sign it is — next
   step, evidence, or conclusion.
3. **ἐγὼ μὲν λέγω … ὑμεῖς δὲ λέγετε** — what two things are being
   balanced, and why are **ἐγώ** and **ὑμεῖς** written out when the verb
   endings already say *I* and *you*?
4. **ὁ δὲ βίος ὃν οὐκ ἐξετάζει ὁ ἄνθρωπος** — what case is **ὅν**, and why?
   Which single word of Plato's does the whole relative clause replace?
5. **οὐκ ἔστι βίος ἀνθρώπῳ** — why is there no article on **ἀνθρώπῳ**?
   (The Voice has the answer.)
6. Plato's sentence is two *if*-clauses: *if I say this … and if again I
   say that …*. The adaptation turned them into plain statements. Say what
   was lost.
7. Count the forms in the gloss list marked *take it whole* that are
   participles — the ones ending in **-μενος**, **-ων**, **-οντος**,
   **-οντι**. Why could the adaptation not use them, and what did it put
   in place of **ἐμοῦ ἀκούετε διαλεγομένου**?
8. Plato has **οὐ πείσεσθέ μοι**, *you will not believe me*; the
   adaptation has **ὑμεῖς δὲ λέγετε ὅτι ψεύδομαι**. Are these the same
   claim?
9. **τὰ δὲ ἔχει μὲν οὕτως … πείθειν δὲ οὐ ῥᾴδιον** — find the **μέν … δέ**
   and say what two things it balances. Then find the adaptation's
   version of the same balance.
10. Quintus could translate every word of the famous sentence and did not
    know what it meant. Having read the whole, say in one English sentence
    what Socrates is arguing — and name the small word that carries it.
:::

::: answers
1. The jury — the five hundred Athenian men trying him. Vocative plural of
   **ἀνήρ**, from the Lesson 15 table. In Plato: **ὦ ἄνδρες**, in the last
   sentence, between **φημι** and **πείθειν**.
2. **μέν … δέ** in the first paragraph (second word each time: **ἐγὼ μέν**,
   **ὑμεῖς δέ**) — balance. **οὖν** in **οὐ δύναμαι οὖν** — conclusion:
   because the god wants me to speak, I cannot be quiet. **δέ** in **λέγω
   δὲ καὶ τοῦτο** — next step. **γάρ** in **ἐγὼ γὰρ ἀεὶ ἐξετάζω** —
   evidence for the claim just made. **δέ** in **ὁ δὲ βίος** — the next
   step, the famous one. **δέ … δέ** in **ταῦτα δὲ λέγω, ὑμεῖς δὲ** —
   next step and contrast. **οὖν** in **οὕτως οὖν ἔχει** — conclusion.
   **δέ** in **χαλεπὸν δέ ἐστι** — contrast. Every one except **οὖν** in
   **οὐ δύναμαι οὖν**, which follows the negative and its verb as a unit,
   is second in its clause. A pupil who finds them all has read the paragraph the way a
   Greek did.
3. What *I* say against what *you* say — Socrates's account of himself
   against the jury's. The pronouns are written out for the contrast, as
   Lesson 5 and Lesson 11 said they would be: Greek adds **ἐγώ** and
   **ὑμεῖς** only when it wants them heard.
4. Accusative, as the object of **ἐξετάζει** inside its own clause,
   although **ὁ βίος** is nominative. It replaces **ἀνεξέταστος** — one
   adjective, *unexamined*, built out of **ἀ-** and **ἐξετάζω**, which the
   class has not had, so the adaptation unpacked it into a clause. Plato's
   version is one word where the adaptation needs five, and far heavier.
5. Because it means *for a human being as such*, any human being, not *for
   the man*. The absence is the point of the sentence, and the adaptation
   kept it on purpose.
6. The whole shape of the argument: Socrates is listing the things he might
   say and predicting, for each, that the jury will not believe him. The
   famous sentence is not asserted; it is quoted, inside a condition, as
   an example of a thing he could say and would not be believed. Turned
   into statements it becomes a speech; in Plato it is a man explaining
   why speaking is useless and speaking anyway.
7. Five: **εἰρωνευομένῳ**, **ὄν**, **διαλεγομένου**, **ἐξετάζοντος**,
   **λέγοντι**. Participles are Lesson 21's business. In place of *you
   hear me conversing* the adaptation has **περὶ τῶν ἄλλων ἃ ἀεὶ
   ἀκούετε**, *about the other things which you always hear* — a relative
   clause, which the class does have, doing the participle's work less
   neatly.
8. Not quite. *You will not believe me* says the jury will take him for a
   liar or a joker — **ὡς εἰρωνευομένῳ** adds that they will think he is
   being ironic. *You say that I am lying* is the nearest the class can
   get without the future and without the participle, and it is sharper
   and ruder than Plato. Accept either answer provided the pupil has
   noticed the future tense is missing from the adaptation.
9. **ἔχει μὲν οὕτως** — *this is how things are* — against **πείθειν δὲ οὐ
   ῥᾴδιον** — *but persuading people of it is not easy*. The truth on one
   side, the difficulty of making anyone hear it on the other. The
   adaptation: **οὕτως οὖν ἔχει … χαλεπὸν δέ ἐστι τοὺς ἀνθρώπους
   διδάσκειν**, with **οὖν** and **δέ** instead of **μέν … δέ**, and
   *teach* for *persuade*.
10. Something like: *I cannot stop questioning people, because the god
    requires it and because a life without questioning is not a human
    life — and I know you will not believe me.* The word is **δέ** at the
    head of the famous sentence, which makes it the second half of
    something; or, for a pupil who has read to the end, the **δέ** of
    **πείθειν δὲ οὐ ῥᾴδιον**, which admits the whole speech has failed.
    Either is a good answer. A pupil who gives *the unexamined life is not
    worth living* as the argument has given the slogan, which is what the
    Story said would happen.
:::

::: question
1. Quintus could translate every word and did not know what the sentence
   meant. Is that a failure of his Greek, or has he just discovered what
   reading is?
2. **μέν** cannot be translated and is not meaningless. Name something in
   English that works the same way — something a reader hears that the words
   do not say.
3. Socrates says an unexamined life is not worth living *for a human being*.
   He does not say it is a bad life. Is that a different claim?
:::

::: story
Afterwards Quintus stayed behind, which he never did.

"The sentence before it," he said. "The one you did not give us. What does it
say?"

"It says that the greatest good for a man is to spend every day talking about
goodness and examining himself and other people." Theodoros was rolling the
scroll. "And after the famous part it says that the jury will find all of
that even harder to believe than everything else he has told them."

"So he knew."

"He knew."

"He said the famous part, and then he said he knew they would not believe
it, and then they killed him."

"That is the order of events."

Quintus thought about this for a while.

"Sir," he said, "I would like to go on with the philosophers."

"I thought you might," said Theodoros.
