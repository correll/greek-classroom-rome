# The Aorist Arrives

::: story
"One past," said Marcus, "ought to be enough for anyone."

"Should it."

"It is a past. The thing happened. It is behind us. What more is there to
say about it?" He was enjoying himself. "Latin has three and that is already
two too many. Greek will no doubt have four."

"Greek has rather more than four," said Theodoros, "but for now you get one,
and I will trade you for it." He wrote on the board:

> **ἔλεγε τὸν λόγον.**
>
> **εἶπε τὸν λόγον.**

"Translate both into Latin. If you can do it without losing anything, you
may have your one past and I shall say no more about it."

Marcus looked at them for a moment. "*Dicebat verbum. Dixit verbum.* It was
saying, it said. That is easy."

"It is," Theodoros agreed. "You are quite right, and Latin does that
perfectly well." He rubbed both out. "Now these."

> **ἐθέλω λέγειν.**
>
> **ἐθέλω εἰπεῖν.**

Marcus started confidently and stopped after two words.

"*Volo dicere*," he said. And then, after a longer pause: "*...Volo
dicere.*"

"Twice."

"They are the same in Latin."

"They are the same in Latin, and in English, and they are not the same in
Greek, and nothing in the difference has anything whatever to do with the
past." Theodoros sat down. "That is what you are getting now. Not another
past tense. A second question Greek asks about every verb, which Latin asks
only sometimes and English asks in a different place."
:::

::: newgreek
## The aorist: an action as a single whole

The **aorist** is the tense Greek uses for an action looked at as **one
complete thing** — it happened, that is all, no comment on how long it took
or whether it was still going on.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἔλεγε** *(imperfect)* | he was saying, he kept saying, he used to say |
| **εἶπε** *(aorist)* | he said |

Both are past. Neither is "more past" than the other. The difference is not
**when** but **how it is being looked at**, and that is called **aspect**.

The name says so. **ἀόριστος** means *undefined*, *without boundaries* — the
tense that declines to tell you anything about the shape of the action.

## Two ways to build one

**The first aorist** adds **-σα-** between stem and ending, and takes the
augment like the imperfect:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἔλυσα** — I loosed |
| 2 sing. | **ἔλυσας** |
| 3 sing. | **ἔλυσε(ν)** |
| 1 plur. | **ἐλύσαμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἐλύσατε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἔλυσαν** |

When the stem ends in a consonant, that consonant and the **σ** fuse:
**γράφω → ἔγραψα** (**φ + σ = ψ**), **διδάσκω → ἐδίδαξα** (**κ + σ = ξ**).

**The second aorist** has no **-σα-** at all. It uses a *different stem*,
with the plain endings you already know from the imperfect:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **λέγω** | **εἶπον** — I said |
| **ἔρχομαι** *(I come)* | **ἦλθον** — I came |
| **φέρω** | **ἤνεγκον** — I carried |

These must be learned as pairs, like English *go / went*. There is no rule
to deduce; **εἶπον** is simply the aorist of **λέγω**, as *went* is simply
the past of *go*, and for the same historical reason.

::: note
**How to tell a second aorist from an imperfect**, since the endings are
identical: look at the **stem**. **ἔλεγον** has the stem of **λέγω**;
**εἶπον** does not. If the stem is the one you know from the present, it is
an imperfect. If it is a stem you have to learn separately, it is an aorist.

This is why vocabulary lists give you the aorist. It is not decoration.
:::

## Why Marcus lost

In the indicative, aspect and time travel together, and Latin can keep up:
*dicebat* and *dixit* do the work of **ἔλεγε** and **εἶπε**.

But Greek's aspect is not attached to the past. It survives into forms that
have no time in them at all — and the infinitive is one. **λέγειν** is *to
say, to be saying, to go on saying*; **εἰπεῖν** is *to say, once*. Both are
timeless; only the aspect differs, and Latin has one word for both.

You meet infinitives properly in Lesson 16. For now it is enough to know
that the thing you learned in this lesson is not a tense. It is a second question,
and Greek asks it everywhere.

::: latinbridge
Marcus was right about the indicative and wrong about the language.

Latin's *dixit* does double duty: it serves both as *he said* (a single
event — the Greek aorist) and as *he has said* (a thing done, with a result
standing — the Greek **perfect**, which you meet in Lesson 33). Greek keeps
those two apart with different tenses.

So the score is roughly even in the past and lopsided everywhere else.
Greek asks about aspect in the infinitive, the imperative, the participle
and the subjunctive; Latin mostly does not; English does so only with
clumsy extra words. Marcus will find this out slowly and with visible
irritation.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἔγραψα** — I wrote *(aorist of **γράφω**)*
- **εἶπον** — I said *(aorist of **λέγω**)*
- **ἦλθον** — I came, I went *(aorist)*
- **ἐγένετο** — it happened, it became, it came to be
- **εὐθύς** — at once, immediately
- **τέλος** — finally, in the end
- **εἰς** — into, to *(with the accusative)*
- **ἔπειτα** — then, next, thereupon
- **οὐκέτι** — no longer
- **ἔλαβον** — I took *(aorist of **λαμβάνω**, I take)*
- **ἔπεσον** — I fell *(aorist of **πίπτω**, I fall)*
- **ἔδραμον** — I ran *(aorist of **τρέχω**, I run)*
- **ἔμαθον** — I learned *(aorist of **μανθάνω**)*

::: note
**Four second aorists at once.** **ἔλαβον**, **ἔπεσον**, **ἔδραμον** and
**ἔμαθον** all have the plain endings of the imperfect and a stem that is
not the present stem. Learn each with its present, as a pair, and notice
that the aorist stem is usually the *shorter* one: **λαμβάν-** against
**λαβ-**, **μανθάν-** against **μαθ-**. The present had things added to it;
the aorist is the verb stripped down.

**ἐγένετο** is a third person singular aorist of a verb whose endings you
have not met — the middle voice, which you meet in full in Lesson 31. Take it
whole. It
is one of the commonest words in Greek narrative and it means *came to be*,
in the strong sense: something that was not, now is.

**εὐθύς** belongs with the aorist the way **ἀεί** belonged with the
imperfect. When you see it, expect a single event.

**τέλος** here is adverbial, *finally*. It is also a noun, **τὸ τέλος** —
*end, purpose, completion* — of the third declension, which arrives in
Lesson 15. English *teleology* is built on it: the study of what things are
**for**.

6. Put into the plural: **ὁ μαθητὴς μανθάνει.**
7. **νῦν** and **τότε** — which one belongs with the present tense?
8. **ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ** — name the case and say what demanded it.
9. **φέρουσι** — person and number, and translate.
10. Turn into the imperfect: **ἀκούω τὴν φωνήν.**

::: answers
1. Feminine — **ἡ ψυχή**, **ἡ ὁδός**, **ἡ οἰκία**, **ἡ ἡμέρα**, any of them.
   (Lesson 10.) A pupil who offers **ὁ λόγος** has matched the sound and
   not the gender.
2. *I am willing, I want.* (Lesson 11.)
3. Imperfect, 2nd singular — *you were teaching*. (Lesson 13.)
4. The first is a phrase (*the great house*); the second a sentence (*the
   house is great*). (Lesson 10.)
5. *We were.* (Lesson 13.) Watch for *they were*, which is **ἦσαν**.
6. **οἱ μαθηταὶ μανθάνουσιν.** Three things change: article, noun ending,
   verb ending. (Lessons 7, 11, 13.)
7. **νῦν**. **τότε** points to a time that is not now. (Lessons 11, 13.)
8. Dative — **ἐν** takes it. (Lesson 9.)
9. Third plural — *they carry*. (Lesson 11.) Not *we carry*, which is
   **φέρομεν**.
10. **ἤκουον τὴν φωνήν.** The augment lengthens **ἀ-** to **ἠ-**; the
    commonest error is **ἐάκουον**, with the augment stuck on the front as
    if the verb began with a consonant. (Lesson 13.)

Say whether each is imperfect or aorist, and what told you.

1. **ἔγραφε**
2. **ἔγραψε**
3. **ἤκουον**
4. **εἶπον**
5. **ἦλθες**
6. **ἐδίδασκον**
7. **ἔπεσε**
8. **ἔμενον**
9. **ἐπέμψαμεν**
10. **ἔλαβες**

::: answers
1. Imperfect — the stem **γραφ-** is the present stem, and there is no
   **-σα-**.
2. Aorist — the **-ψ-** is **φ + σ**.
3. Imperfect — present stem **ἀκου-**, lengthened augment.
4. Aorist — the stem **εἰπ-** is not the present stem of **λέγω**. (Also
   ambiguous between 1st singular and 3rd plural, like every **-ον**.)
5. Aorist — stem **ἐλθ-**, which is not the present stem of anything they
   know.
6. Imperfect — present stem **διδασκ-**. Compare the aorist **ἐδίδαξα**.
7. Aorist — stem **πεσ-** is not the present stem **πιπτ-**. A second
   aorist, with no **-σα-**, so the stem alone decides.
8. Imperfect — present stem **μεν-**, and nothing else to go on. Ambiguous
   between *I was staying* and *they were staying*.
9. Aorist — **-ψα-** is **π + σα**; the first aorist of **πέμπω**.
10. Aorist — stem **λαβ-** against the present **λαμβαν-**. A pupil who
    calls this an imperfect has not learned the pair; send them to the
    vocabulary box.

1 and 2 differ by one letter and are the whole lesson. 7, 8 and 10 are the
reason the lesson's note says to look at the stem: with no **-σα-**, the
ending tells you nothing.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **τότε ἔλεγε τοὺς λόγους· τέλος δὲ εἶπε τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
2. **εὐθὺς ἦλθον εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν.** *(**εἰς** = into, with the accusative)*
3. **ἀεὶ ἔγραφε, ἀλλὰ οὐκ ἔγραψε τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
4. **ἐγένετο ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ πρὸς τοὺς ἀνθρώπους.**
5. **ἔπειτα ὁ μαθητὴς ἔλαβε τὸ βιβλίον καὶ ἔδραμεν ἐκ τῆς οἰκίας.**
6. **ὁ ἵππος ἔπεσεν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ· οὐκέτι ἔτρεχεν.**
7. **ἐμάνθανον ἀεί, ἀλλὰ τέλος ἔμαθον.**
8. **εὐθὺς ἔγραψεν ἡ Ἰουλία· ὁ Μᾶρκος οὐκέτι ἔγραφεν.**
9. **ἦλθες εἰς τὸ διδασκαλεῖον, ἀλλὰ οὐκ ἤκουσας.** *(**τὸ διδασκαλεῖον**
   = the school room)*
10. **τότε ἐγένετο μεγάλη φωνὴ ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Then he kept saying the words; but finally he said the truth.* The
   imperfect for the repeated attempts, the aorist for the one that landed.
   This is the characteristic Greek narrative shape.
2. *At once I came into the house.* Or *they came* — **ἦλθον** is ambiguous.
3. *He was always writing, but he did not write the letter.* The joke is in
   the aspect: endless writing, nothing finished. Greek can say this in seven
   words.
4. *The word of God came to the men.*
5. *Then the pupil took the book and ran out of the house.* — two aorists,
   two events, in order. **ἐκ** with the genitive, from Lesson 8.
6. *The horse fell on the road; it was no longer running.* — aorist for the
   fall, imperfect for the running that had been going on until then.
   **ἔτρεχεν** is the imperfect of **τρέχω**, which the vocabulary gives you
   as the present of **ἔδραμον**; a pupil who calls it an aorist has not
   checked the stem.
7. *I kept learning always, but finally I learned it.* Or *they* — both
   verbs are **-ον** forms and ambiguous together. The sentence is sentence
   3 turned the other way up: this time the finished act happens.
8. *Julia wrote at once; Marcus was no longer writing.* — **εὐθύς** with the
   aorist, **οὐκέτι** with the imperfect, each where the lesson's note says
   to expect it.
9. *You came into the school room, but you did not listen.* — **ἤκουσας** is
   the first aorist of **ἀκούω**: lengthened augment, **-σα-**, and the
   second person ending. Nobody has shown them this form; they can build it
   from the table.
10. *Then there was a loud voice in the house* — more literally, *a great
    voice came about*. No article on **φωνή**, so *a* voice; **ἐγένετο**
    for a sound that happened once and was over. Accept *a loud sound*.
:::

   for some time is a different and stranger story.
7. **τὸ βιβλίον ἔπεσεν.**
8. **εὐθὺς ἐλάβομεν τὰ δῶρα.** — the augment goes on the aorist stem

9. **οὐκέτι ἐμάνθανεν.** — imperfect: the learning was a state that

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's trade"}
Marcus offered to give up his position if he could translate **ἐθέλω
λέγειν** and **ἐθέλω εἰπεῖν** into Latin without loss.

1. Why could he not?
2. Give an English pair that gets closest to the difference.
3. Marcus said a past is a past. In the indicative he was nearly right.
   Where was he wrong?
4. Give the Latin for **ἔγραφε** and **ἔγραψε**.
5. Latin *dixit* — which Greek form does it match: **ἔλεγε**, **εἶπε**, or
   something else as well?
6. Is **ἐγένετο** imperfect or aorist? How can you tell, given that you
   have not been shown its endings?
7. **εὐθύς** and **ἀεί** — which goes with which tense, and why?
8. Write one Greek sentence containing an imperfect and an aorist, in
   which the imperfect is the background and the aorist the event.
9. Marcus's heading was *things Latin cannot do*. Name one thing in this
   lesson that Latin does exactly as well as Greek.
10. Theodoros said that what Marcus was getting was "not another past
    tense" but "a second question". Say in one sentence what the question
    is.
:::

::: answers
1. Because both are *volo dicere*. The Greek pair differs in aspect alone,
   and the infinitive has no time in it for Latin's tense system to hold on
   to.
2. *I want to be saying* against *I want to say* — thin, and the best
   English manages. Accept *I want to speak* against *I want to say my
   piece* if a pupil can defend it.
3. He was wrong about the rest of the language. Aspect in Greek is not a
   fact about the past; it is a fact about verbs, and it survives wherever
   verbs go.
4. *Scribebat* and *scripsit*. The Latin imperfect and perfect carry the
   indicative distinction perfectly well, which is the half of the argument
   Marcus won.
5. **εἶπε** — and also the Greek perfect, *he has said*, which this course
   has not reached. *Dixit* does both jobs; Greek keeps them apart. This is
   the point of the Latin bridge above.
6. Aorist. The vocabulary note says so, and the stem **γεν-** is not a
   present stem the class knows. A pupil who says *imperfect, because it
   has an augment* has forgotten that the aorist has one too.
7. **εὐθύς** with the aorist, **ἀεί** with the imperfect. *At once* names a
   moment; *always* names a stretch. The adverbs are not grammar, but they
   are a reliable tell, and a reader uses them.
8. For example **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἔλεγε, καὶ ὁ Φῆλιξ εὐθὺς ἦλθεν.** Check that
   the imperfect is the thing going on and the aorist the thing that
   happens inside it, not the other way round. Sentences with both tenses
   but no such relation (*he was writing and he said*) are grammatical and
   miss the point; ask what the imperfect is the background *to*.
9. Telling *he was saying* from *he said* in a plain past statement:
   *dicebat* / *dixit*. Marcus's trade was fair in the indicative.
10. *Not when did it happen, but what shape does it have* — one whole act,
    or something going on. Any wording that separates time from shape is
    right.
:::

::: {.voice source="John 1:1 and 1:14"}
The same author, the same subject, the same chapter — and two different
tenses, thirteen verses apart.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

*In the beginning was the Word.*

**καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο.**

- **καί** — and
- **ὁ λόγος** — the Word *(nominative, the subject)*
- **σάρξ** — flesh *(third declension; a predicate, with no article)*
- **ἐγένετο** — became, came to be *(aorist)*

*And the Word became flesh.*

**ἦν** is imperfect: *was, and went on being*, with no beginning reported
and no end. **ἐγένετο** is aorist: a single thing that happened, at a point,
complete.

So the two lines are not saying the same kind of thing twice. The first
describes a state with no edges. The second reports an event with edges on
both sides. Had the author written **ἦν σάρξ** he would have said the Word
*was* flesh all along; had he written **ἐγένετο ἐν ἀρχῇ** he would have had
the Word begin.

He wrote neither, and the whole of the difference is carried by two verb
forms you learned in Lessons 13 and 14.

Notice also **σάρξ**: no article, standing outside the group, which by
Lesson 10 makes it a predicate. The construction is exactly that of
**θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** in verse 1. Same shape, different tense, and the
difference between them is the point.

::: note
Marcus wanted one past. Here are two, about one subject, in one paragraph,
doing two jobs that no single tense could do at once. He conceded the
argument and then asked whether Latin could manage it, which it can, and
then asked about the infinitives again, which it cannot.
:::

Read it aloud first. Then go through it a second time marking every verb
**I** (imperfect) or **A** (aorist) before you answer.

2. What is the first aorist in the tablet? What happens at that moment?
3. What did Felix say, and to whom? What case is **διδάσκαλε**, and which

4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος οὐκέτι ἔλεγεν.** Why is this an imperfect and not an

5. What woke the cat? Give the Greek.
6. Where did the cat run from, and where to? Name the two prepositions and
   the case each one takes.
7. What fell, and what was written on it?
8. Who picked up the tablet, and what did he say? Which tablet is he
   talking about — this one, or the one from Lesson 13?
9. Felix says **ἐγὼ οὐκ ἦλθον**. Is it true? Quote the words of the tablet
   that contradict him, and say what tense they are.
10. List every aorist in the fourth paragraph, then every imperfect. Which
    tense carries the events, and which the states?

::: answers
1. All imperfect — **ἔλεγε, ἤκουον, ἐκάθευδεν** — and all with **ἔτι**.
   They are the background: three things still going on, into which the
   events are about to arrive. Nothing has happened yet.
2. **ἦλθεν** — Felix came in. The story has been standing still for three
   verbs and now moves.
3. *Hello, teacher* — **χαῖρε, ὦ διδάσκαλε** — to the teacher. Vocative,
   from Lesson 4, with **ὦ** and the **-ε** ending.
4. Because it describes a state — *he was no longer speaking* — and not an
   act. The stopping is not narrated as a thing he did; the speaking is
   simply reported as not going on. **οὐκέτι** with the imperfect is the
   natural pair. A pupil who expected an aorist (*he stopped*) has noticed
   that Greek has chosen to look at the silence, not the stop.
5. The voice: **ἤκουσε τὴν φωνήν** — Felix's greeting. A pupil may say
   *Felix*; ask what the cat *heard*.
6. **ἐκ τῶν βιβλίων**, out of the books — genitive; **εἰς τὴν τράπεζαν**,
   onto the table — accusative. Lesson 8 and this lesson.
7. The teacher's tablet, **ἡ δέλτος τοῦ διδασκάλου**, and on it **ἡ γραφὴ
   περὶ τῶν μαθητῶν** — the writing about the pupils: the earlier tablet's
   story, as question 8 shows.
8. Marcus; **ἐγὼ οὐκ ἔλεγον περὶ τῶν ἵππων** — *I was not talking about
   horses*. He means the Lesson 13 tablet, which said he was; he has read
   the accusation and is answering it. The teacher's one-word reply,
   **ἔλεγες**, is the imperfect of Lesson 13 doing all the work.
9. No. **ὁ Φῆλιξ ἦλθεν εἰς τὸ διδασκαλεῖον** — aorist, at the start of the
   second paragraph, in the tablet he is holding. He is denying an event
   the story has already recorded. (Felix's denial is itself in the
   aorist, which is the right tense for it; he is simply wrong.)
10. Aorists: **ἔλαβε, εἶπεν, εἶπεν, ἔγραψε, εἶπεν, ἦλθον** — six, counting
    the one inside Felix's words. Imperfects: **ἔβλεπε, ἔλεγεν, ἔλεγον,
    ἔλεγες** — four (and **γράφει** is present, which a careful pupil will
    flag). The aorists are the things that
    happen one after another; the imperfects are what people were doing,
    or claim to have been doing, meanwhile.
:::

::: question
1. **ἦν** has no beginning in it and **ἐγένετο** does. If a language forces
   you to choose, is it making you more precise or making the choice for
   you?
2. English says *was* for both. How does an English translator decide, and
   what should they do when they cannot?
3. **ἀόριστος** means *undefined*. Why would a language name a tense after
   what it refuses to tell you?
:::

::: story
Marcus wrote both sentences out, at the back of his tablet, under a heading
of his own.

The heading read: **things Latin cannot do**.

It had one entry to begin with. By the end of Part V it had grown to a
column, and he had stopped writing it in a tone of complaint some time
around the fourth.
