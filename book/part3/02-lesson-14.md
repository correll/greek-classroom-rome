# The Aorist Arrives

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"One past," said Marcus, "ought to be enough for anyone."

"Should it."

"It is a past. The thing happened. It is behind us. What more is there to
say about it?" He was enjoying himself. "Latin has two and that is already
one too many. Greek will no doubt have four."

"Greek has rather more than four," said Theodoros, "but today you get one,
and I will trade you for it." He wrote on the board:

> **ἔλεγε τὸν λόγον.**
>
> **εἶπε τὸν λόγον.**

"Translate both into Latin. If you can do it without losing anything, you
may have your one past and I shall say no more about it."

Marcus looked at them for a moment. "*Dicebat verbum. Dixit verbum.* It was
saying, it said. That is easy."

"It is," Theodoros agreed. "You are quite right, and Latin does that
perfectly well." He rubbed both out. "Now these."

> **ἐθέλω λέγειν.**
>
> **ἐθέλω εἰπεῖν.**

Marcus started confidently and stopped after two words.

"*Volo dicere*," he said. And then, after a longer pause: "*...Volo
dicere.*"

"Twice."

"They are the same in Latin."

"They are the same in Latin, and in English, and they are not the same in
Greek, and nothing in the difference has anything whatever to do with the
past." Theodoros sat down. "That is what you are getting today. Not another
past tense. A second question Greek asks about every verb, which Latin asks
only sometimes and English hardly asks at all."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The aorist: an action as a single whole

The **aorist** is the tense Greek uses for an action looked at as **one
complete thing** — it happened, that is all, no comment on how long it took
or whether it was still going on.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **ἔλεγε** *(imperfect)* | he was saying, he kept saying, he used to say |
| **εἶπε** *(aorist)* | he said |

Both are past. Neither is "more past" than the other. The difference is not
**when** but **how it is being looked at**, and that is called **aspect**.

The name says so. **ἀόριστος** means *undefined*, *without boundaries* — the
tense that declines to tell you anything about the shape of the action.

## Two ways to build one

**The first aorist** adds **-σα-** between stem and ending, and takes the
augment like the imperfect:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἔλυσα** — I loosed |
| 2 sing. | **ἔλυσας** |
| 3 sing. | **ἔλυσε(ν)** |
| 1 plur. | **ἐλύσαμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἐλύσατε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἔλυσαν** |

When the stem ends in a consonant, that consonant and the **σ** fuse:
**γράφω → ἔγραψα** (**φ + σ = ψ**), **διδάσκω → ἐδίδαξα** (**κ + σ = ξ**).

**The second aorist** has no **-σα-** at all. It uses a *different stem*,
with the plain endings you already know from the imperfect:

| | |
|:--|:--|
| **λέγω** | **εἶπον** — I said |
| **ἔρχομαι** *(I come)* | **ἦλθον** — I came |
| **φέρω** | **ἤνεγκον** — I carried |

These must be learned as pairs, like English *go / went*. There is no rule
to deduce; **εἶπον** is simply the aorist of **λέγω**, as *went* is simply
the past of *go*, and for the same historical reason.

::: note
**How to tell a second aorist from an imperfect**, since the endings are
identical: look at the **stem**. **ἔλεγον** has the stem of **λέγω**;
**εἶπον** does not. If the stem is the one you know from the present, it is
an imperfect. If it is a stem you have to learn separately, it is an aorist.

This is why vocabulary lists give you the aorist. It is not decoration.
:::

## Why Marcus lost

In the indicative, aspect and time travel together, and Latin can keep up:
*dicebat* and *dixit* do the work of **ἔλεγε** and **εἶπε**.

But Greek's aspect is not attached to the past. It survives into forms that
have no time in them at all — and the infinitive is one. **λέγειν** is *to
say, to be saying, to go on saying*; **εἰπεῖν** is *to say, once*. Both are
timeless; only the aspect differs, and Latin has one word for both.

You meet infinitives properly in Lesson 16. For now it is enough to know
that the thing you learned today is not a tense. It is a second question,
and Greek asks it everywhere.

::: latinbridge
Marcus was right about the indicative and wrong about the language.

Latin's *dixit* does double duty: it serves both as *he said* (a single
event — the Greek aorist) and as *he has said* (a thing done, with a result
standing — the Greek **perfect**, which this course leaves for later). Greek keeps
those two apart with different tenses.

So the score is roughly even in the past and lopsided everywhere else.
Greek asks about aspect in the infinitive, the imperative, the participle
and the subjunctive; Latin mostly does not; English does so only with
clumsy extra words. Marcus will find this out slowly and with visible
irritation.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἔγραψα** — I wrote *(aorist of **γράφω**)*
- **εἶπον** — I said *(aorist of **λέγω**)*
- **ἦλθον** — I came, I went *(aorist)*
- **ἐγένετο** — it happened, it became, it came to be
- **εὐθύς** — at once, immediately
- **τέλος** — finally, in the end

::: note
**ἐγένετο** is a third person singular aorist of a verb whose endings you
have not met — the middle voice, which this course does not teach in full. Take it
whole. It
is one of the commonest words in Greek narrative and it means *came to be*,
in the strong sense: something that was not, now is.

**εὐθύς** belongs with the aorist the way **ἀεί** belonged with the
imperfect. When you see it, expect a single event.

**τέλος** here is adverbial, *finally*. It is also a noun, **τὸ τέλος** —
*end, purpose, completion* — of the third declension, which arrives in
Lesson 15. English *teleology* is built on it: the study of what things are
**for**.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Marcus asked to keep the two sentences. He was given them.

::: {.exercise title="Which past, and how do you know?"}
Say whether each is imperfect or aorist, and what told you.

1. **ἔγραφε**
2. **ἔγραψε**
3. **ἤκουον**
4. **εἶπον**
5. **ἦλθες**
6. **ἐδίδασκον**
:::

::: answers
1. Imperfect — the stem **γραφ-** is the present stem, and there is no
   **-σα-**.
2. Aorist — the **-ψ-** is **φ + σ**.
3. Imperfect — present stem **ἀκου-**, lengthened augment.
4. Aorist — the stem **εἰπ-** is not the present stem of **λέγω**. (Also
   ambiguous between 1st singular and 3rd plural, like every **-ον**.)
5. Aorist — stem **ἐλθ-**, which is not the present stem of anything they
   know.
6. Imperfect — present stem **διδασκ-**. Compare the aorist **ἐδίδαξα**.

1 and 2 differ by one letter and are the whole lesson.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **τότε ἔλεγε τοὺς λόγους· τέλος δὲ εἶπε τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
2. **εὐθὺς ἦλθον εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν.** *(**εἰς** = into, with the accusative)*
3. **ἀεὶ ἔγραφε, ἀλλὰ οὐκ ἔγραψε τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
4. **ἐγένετο ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ πρὸς τοὺς ἀνθρώπους.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Then he kept saying the words; but finally he said the truth.* The
   imperfect for the repeated attempts, the aorist for the one that landed.
   This is the characteristic Greek narrative shape.
2. *At once I came into the house.* Or *they came* — **ἦλθον** is ambiguous.
3. *He was always writing, but he did not write the letter.* The joke is in
   the aspect: endless writing, nothing finished. Greek can say this in seven
   words.
4. *The word of God came to the men.*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I wrote the letter.
2. I was writing the letter.
3. He said the word.
4. They came at once.
5. Finally the teacher said the truth.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἔγραψα τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
2. **ἔγραφον τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
3. **εἶπε τὸν λόγον.**
4. **εὐθὺς ἦλθον.**
5. **τέλος εἶπε ὁ διδάσκαλος τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**

1 and 2 are the examination, and a pupil who produces the same Greek for
both has not understood the lesson however well they know the endings. Watch
also for **ἔλεγε** offered at 3: it is a past of the right verb and the
wrong aspect.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's trade"}
Marcus offered to give up his position if he could translate **ἐθέλω
λέγειν** and **ἐθέλω εἰπεῖν** into Latin without loss.

1. Why could he not?
2. Give an English pair that gets closest to the difference.
3. Marcus said a past is a past. In the indicative he was nearly right.
   Where was he wrong?
:::

::: answers
1. Because both are *volo dicere*. The Greek pair differs in aspect alone,
   and the infinitive has no time in it for Latin's tense system to hold on
   to.
2. *I want to be saying* against *I want to say* — thin, and the best
   English manages. Accept *I want to speak* against *I want to say my
   piece* if a pupil can defend it.
3. He was wrong about the rest of the language. Aspect in Greek is not a
   fact about the past; it is a fact about verbs, and it survives wherever
   verbs go.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 1:1 and 1:14"}
The same author, the same subject, the same chapter — and two different
tenses, thirteen verses apart.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

*In the beginning was the Word.*

**καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο.**

- **καί** — and
- **ὁ λόγος** — the Word *(nominative, the subject)*
- **σάρξ** — flesh *(third declension; a predicate, with no article)*
- **ἐγένετο** — became, came to be *(aorist)*

*And the Word became flesh.*

**ἦν** is imperfect: *was, and went on being*, with no beginning reported
and no end. **ἐγένετο** is aorist: a single thing that happened, at a point,
complete.

So the two lines are not saying the same kind of thing twice. The first
describes a state with no edges. The second reports an event with edges on
both sides. Had the author written **ἦν σάρξ** he would have said the Word
*was* flesh all along; had he written **ἐγένετο ἐν ἀρχῇ** he would have had
the Word begin.

He wrote neither, and the whole of the difference is carried by two verb
forms you learned this week.

Notice also **σάρξ**: no article, standing outside the group, which by
Lesson 10 makes it a predicate. The construction is exactly that of
**θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** in verse 1. Same shape, different tense, and the
difference between them is the point.

::: note
Marcus wanted one past. Here are two, about one subject, in one paragraph,
doing two jobs that no single tense could do at once. He conceded the
argument and then asked whether Latin could manage it, which it can, and
then asked about the infinitives again, which it cannot.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. **ἦν** has no beginning in it and **ἐγένετο** does. If a language forces
   you to choose, is it making you more precise or making the choice for
   you?
2. English says *was* for both. How does an English translator decide, and
   what should they do when they cannot?
3. **ἀόριστος** means *undefined*. Why would a language name a tense after
   what it refuses to tell you?
:::

Marcus wrote both sentences out, at the back of his tablet, under a heading
of his own.

The heading read: **things Latin cannot do**.

It had one entry that afternoon. By the end of Part V it had eleven, and he
had stopped writing it in a tone of complaint some time around the fourth.
