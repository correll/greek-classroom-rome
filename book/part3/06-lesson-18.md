# The Trial of a Translation

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros put six wax tablets face down on the table and said that there
would be a trial.

"Sabina's courtroom," said Felix.

"Sabina's courtroom. Six defendants, one charge." He turned the first tablet
over. "Each of you has translated the same six Greek words. I am going to
read all six translations aloud, and then you are going to decide which of
them can be defended from the Greek."

"Which is best?" said Marcus.

"No. Which can be *defended*. They are not the same question and by the end
of this afternoon you will know why."

He read them out. The room did something it had not done all year, which was
to go completely silent for most of a minute.

"They cannot all be right," said Quintus.

"No."

"They cannot all be *wrong*."

"Also no."

Marcus stood up without being asked, which was characteristic, and gave a
speech, which was also characteristic. It lasted ninety seconds. It had three
parts. It ended with the observation that he had translated the words in the
order in which they were written, which was surely the most honest thing a
translator could do, and that the others had all rearranged a sentence that
God had not asked them to rearrange.

It was the best thing anybody said that afternoon.

It was also wrong, and Julia knew exactly why, and said so, and nobody heard
her.

"Julia."

"I said — " She stopped, and started again, at the same volume. "I said the
article is on **λόγος**."

"Louder."

"The article is on **λόγος**," said Julia, "and it is not on **θεός**, and
that is not a decoration, it is the only thing in the sentence that tells us
which word is the subject. Marcus has translated the order. He has not
translated the grammar. Word order is not what carries it. We learned that in
Lesson four. It was the whole of Lesson four."

Marcus sat down.

"You are right," he said, after a moment, in a voice that cost him
something.

"I know."

"You should have said it louder the first time."

"I did say it."

"I know," said Marcus. "That is what I mean."

Theodoros let the room sit with that.

"Note what has just happened," he said, "because it will happen to you for
the rest of your lives and you will usually be on the wrong side of it. The
elegant argument was wrong. The correct argument was inaudible. The elegant
one nearly won." He picked up the first tablet. "A translation is not a
performance. It is a claim about evidence. Let us see whose evidence
survives."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## No new grammar

There is none in this lesson. Everything you need you already have, and that
is the point: this is the lesson where the parts become a method.

## Four questions

Put these to any Greek sentence, in this order, before you translate a word
of it.

**One. What do the endings say?** Case, number, gender on the nouns; person
and number on the verb. The endings assign the roles, and they do it
regardless of where the words sit. This was Parts I and II, and it was
Sabina's courtroom in Lesson 4: *the witness accused the judge* and *the
judge the witness accused* can be the same sentence in Greek, and the verdict
does not move.

**Two. Where is the article — and where is it not?** The article marks the
subject. It marks what is definite. Its position tells you whether an
adjective is describing or asserting, which was Lesson 10. And its *absence*
is evidence too: a missing article is a fact about the sentence, not a gap in
it.

**Three. What is the aspect?** Not *when* but *what shape*. Imperfect:
going on, unbounded, no end in view. Aorist: the whole thing, as one act. An
infinitive after **βούλομαι** carries shape and no time at all. Part III has
been almost entirely about this.

**Four. What are the small words doing?** **γάρ** is evidence. **οὖν** is a
conclusion. **δέ** is the next step. **μέν** is a promise of a second half.
These are the skeleton of the argument, and they are the first thing a hasty
reader drops.

## And then the fifth question, which is not grammar

Ask it of your own translation, out loud, before you defend it:

**What would have to be true in the Greek for my translation to be wrong?**

If you cannot answer that, you do not have a translation. You have a guess
that has not been tested, and it may well be a good guess, and you still have
no business defending it in front of anybody.

If you *can* answer it, then you know exactly what evidence your reading
rests on — and you will know at once whether somebody has knocked it over.

## Defensible and persuasive

These two words are going to do a lot of work for the rest of the course.

A **persuasive** translation makes the hearer agree. It can do this with
rhythm, with confidence, with a good speaking voice, with being first, with
being longer than the other one.

A **defensible** translation can name the feature of the Greek it rests on.
*The article is on this word and not on that one.* *The ending is accusative.*
*The verb is imperfect, so the action is not finished.*

The two are not opposites, and the best translation is both. But when they
come apart — and they come apart constantly — the defensible one is the one
that is actually about the Greek.

::: note
**A test you can apply to yourself.** When somebody disagrees with your
translation, listen to what you say next.

If you say *because the article is on **λόγος***, you are defending.

If you say *because it sounds better*, or *because that is how it is usually
said*, or *because my translation has a rhythm to it* — you are persuading,
and you may be right, and you have not given a reason.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin cannot help you here, and the reason is important.**

Latin has no article. None. Not definite, not indefinite. A Latin writer
cannot make the distinction that the whole of this lesson turns on, and a
Latin translator meeting it in Greek has to throw it away or replace it with
something else.

This is the first time in the course that your Latin is not merely unhelpful
but *unable*. It will not be the last. Hold on to the feeling: it is what a
translator feels when the target language has no room for what the source
language said, and it is the oldest problem in the trade.
:::
:::

::: note
Every one of these pairs is a place where English has one word and Greek has
two, or where English has two and does not keep them apart the way Greek
does. A translator who renders **ἀνήρ** and **ἄνθρωπος** with the same
English word has not made an error. They have made a *choice*, and they owe
you an account of it.

This is the whole of Lesson 19, arriving early.

::: {.exercise title="Translate, then defend"}
For each sentence, give a translation and then name one feature of the Greek
that your translation rests on — an ending, an article, an aspect, or a small
word.

1. **ὁ μὲν σοφὸς ἀνὴρ ἐθέλει μανθάνειν, ὁ δὲ κακὸς οὐ βούλεται.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος, ὃς ἔλεγε τὴν ἀλήθειαν, εἶπε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις τὸν λόγον.**
3. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν θεὸν ἀγαθὸν εἶναι· ἡ γὰρ σοφία δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The wise man is willing to learn, but the bad man does not want to.* —
   rests on **μέν … δέ**, which makes the two halves a balance, and on
   **μανθάνειν**, an infinitive completing **ἐθέλει**. A pupil may also
   point to **σοφός** inside the article group with **ἀνήρ**: attributive,
   describing, Lesson 10.
2. *The teacher, who was speaking the truth, said the word to the people.* —
   rests on **ὅς**, masculine nominative, so its antecedent is **ὁ
   διδάσκαλος** and not **τὴν ἀλήθειαν**; on **ἔλεγε**, imperfect, so the
   speaking was going on; and on **εἶπε**, aorist, so the saying was one
   act. The contrast between the two verbs is the sentence.
3. *We know that God is good; for wisdom is a gift.* — rests on **τὸν θεόν**
   accusative with **εἶναι**, which is the indirect statement of Lesson 16,
   and on **γάρ**, which marks the second clause as the reason for the
   first.

Mark the *defence*, not the translation. A pupil with a slightly clumsy
English version and a correct account of the grammar has done better work
than a pupil with a fluent version and nothing to say about it, and should
be told so in front of the class.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. In the beginning was the word.
2. The wise man wants to learn; the bad man does not.
3. We know that the word was God.
4. The teacher said the truth to the people.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.** — Lesson 6, and they should recognise it before
   they have finished writing it.
2. **ὁ μὲν σοφὸς βούλεται μανθάνειν, ὁ δὲ κακὸς οὐ βούλεται.**
3. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν λόγον θεὸν εἶναι.** — this is the afternoon's sentence
   put through Lesson 16. Note what happened to the article: it is still on
   **λόγος**, which is still the thing being talked about, and it has
   followed **λόγος** into the accusative. **θεόν** still has none. Ask the
   class what that tells them, and let them find it.

   A sharp pupil will object that the English says *was* and **εἶναι** is
   present. They are right to object, and the answer is that Greek has no
   imperfect infinitive: the present infinitive does that work too. Tell
   them so, and tell them it was a good objection.
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος εἶπε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — accept any order of
   the three groups after the verb. If somebody objects that a different
   order is a different sentence, the answer is Lesson 4, and they should be
   made to give it themselves.
:::

::: {.exercise title="The fifth question"}
Take your own answer to number 3 above — **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν λόγον θεὸν
εἶναι**.

1. What would have to be true in the Greek for your translation to be wrong?
2. Now do the same for **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**.
3. One of the two was much easier to answer. Why?
:::

::: answers
1. **τὸν λόγον** would have to be nominative, or **θεόν** would have to carry
   the article, or **εἶναι** would have to be a finite verb. Any of the three
   would break the reading.
2. Very little. There is almost nothing in it that could be otherwise:
   **ἀρχῇ** is dative after **ἐν**, **ὁ λόγος** is nominative and has the
   article, **ἦν** is third singular. The sentence is about as unambiguous
   as Greek gets.
3. Because the second sentence has fewer places where a choice is being
   made. A sentence is hard to defend exactly where it is interesting, and
   the number of ways a translation could be wrong is a fair measure of how
   much the translator is actually deciding.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 1:1c"}
Six words. You have had all six since Part I.

**καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

- **καί** — and
- **θεός** — God *(nominative — and **no article**)*
- **ἦν** — was *(imperfect, Lesson 13: was, and went on being)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — the Word *(nominative — **with** the article)*

Here is what the class handed in.

| | Translation |
|:--|:--|
| Marcus | *and God was the Word* |
| Julia | *and the Word was God* |
| Felix | *and the Word was a god* |
| Quintus | *and the Word was divine* |
| Sabina | *and what God was, the Word was* |
| Theodosius | *et Deus erat Verbum* |

**Start with the two nominatives.** Both **θεός** and **ὁ λόγος** are
nominative, so the endings alone cannot tell you which one the sentence is
about. Greek has a second device for exactly this situation, and it is the
article.

**ὁ λόγος** has it. **θεός** does not. The article marks the subject — this
is Lesson 2, and it has been load-bearing ever since. So the sentence is
about **ὁ λόγος**, and **θεός** is what is being said about it.

**Now Marcus.** His translation follows the Greek word order exactly, and
that is what is wrong with it. In English, the word in front of *was* is the
subject, so *God was the Word* tells an English reader that the sentence is
about God. The Greek says it is about the Word. Marcus has reproduced the
order and reversed the grammar, and the reason he could do it without
noticing is that English assigns roles by position and Greek does not.

That is Lesson 4, five months later, with something at stake.

**Why is θεός in front, then?** Because Greek puts a word first for emphasis,
and the article is doing the work of marking the subject, so the writer is
free to. The fronting is not an accident and it is not nothing. It is simply
not what assigns the roles.

**And Theodosius.** He wrote out the Latin, and the Latin has the same word
order as the Greek, and Latin has no article at all — so *et Deus erat
Verbum* cannot make the distinction we have just spent ten minutes making. A
Latin reader has to decide from the surrounding argument. The distinction is
not mistranslated. It is *untranslatable*, into that language, and it is gone.

**Four translations are left.** Julia's, Felix's, Quintus's and Sabina's all
make **ὁ λόγος** the subject. All four are consistent with the grammar so
far.

And here the grammar stops.

A Greek predicate noun very often drops the article even when it is entirely
definite — so Felix cannot argue straight from *no article* to *a god*; that
inference does not hold as a rule. But neither does the grammar by itself
force *God* rather than *divine*. What is left is a question about what an
anarthrous predicate noun in this position is doing, and that is a question
about usage and about the rest of the book, not about these six words.

**This is the honest end of the lesson.** The grammar eliminates one
translation outright, shows a second to be describing a distinction its
language cannot carry, and narrows the rest. It does not choose between them.
People in the city outside this room are arguing about this sentence right
now, with a heat you would find alarming, and the argument is not going to be
settled by anything in this chapter.

What the grammar has given you is the ability to tell the difference between
an argument about this sentence and a feeling about it. That is a smaller
thing than certainty, and it is worth more.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The Greek is John 1:1c and is quoted here from
memory of the standard text; check it, with the punctuation, against the
edition in use.

**On the doctrinal question.** This passage is live, and the lesson is
deliberately built to teach what the grammar does and does not determine and
to stop there. That is not evasion: the four surviving renderings genuinely
differ on grounds that are not grammatical, and a course that pretended
otherwise would be teaching pupils to dress a conviction in evidence, which
is the exact habit the whole of Part III exists to break.

If you teach in a confessional setting and wish to say where your tradition
stands, say it — and say it *after* the grammar, and mark plainly that you
are now speaking as a believer and not as a grammarian. Pupils can hold both.
What damages them is the two being run together without a seam.

**Felix's translation deserves a straight answer, not a frown.** His
reasoning — no article, therefore indefinite — is a reasonable inference from
what he has been taught, and a pupil who makes it has been paying attention.
The answer is that Greek regularly omits the article from a predicate noun
that is nonetheless definite, which is a fact about usage he has not met yet.
Tell him that, and tell him his inference was sound and his premise
incomplete.

**On the arc.** This is the lesson in which to say, if you are going to say
it, that every pupil in the room has got better at something they began badly
at — Julia at speaking up, Marcus at stopping, Felix at reading rather than
guessing. They have now watched it happen for eighteen lessons, which is why
it can be said here and could not have been said in Lesson 1. A child who
decided in Lesson 4 that they were bad at Greek has usually decided they are
bad at one of four things, and the four questions at the head of this lesson
are how you show them the other three.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Marcus's translation was wrong and convincing. Julia's was right and
   unconvincing. Which failure is worse in a reader? Which is worse in a
   teacher?
2. Latin cannot make the distinction this sentence turns on. Does that mean
   a Latin reader understands the sentence less well, or only differently?
3. The grammar narrowed six translations to four and then stopped. Is a
   method that cannot finish the job still worth having?

Afterwards Felix wanted to know who had won.

"Nobody won."

"There were six of us and a trial and you said there was a charge."

"There was a charge," said Theodoros. "The charge was defending a reading you
could not support. Four of you were acquitted. One of you wrote in Latin,
which I shall allow this once. And one of you gave the finest speech I have
heard in this room and would have carried the whole class with him if a girl
at the back had not been braver than she usually is."

Felix considered this.

"So Julia won."

"Julia was *right*," said Theodoros. "It is a smaller prize than winning and
