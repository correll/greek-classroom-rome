# The Trial of a Translation

::: story
Theodoros put eight wax tablets face down on the table and said that there
would be a trial.

"Sabina's courtroom," said Felix.

"Sabina's courtroom. Eight defendants, one charge." He turned the first tablet
over. "Each of you has translated the same five Greek words. I am going to
read all eight translations aloud, and then you are going to decide which of
them can be defended from the Greek."

"Which is best?" said Marcus.

"No. Which can be *defended*. They are not the same question and by the end
of this afternoon you will know why."

He read them out. The room did something it had not done all year, which was
to go completely silent for most of a minute.

"They cannot all be right," said Quintus.

"No."

"They cannot all be *wrong*."

"Also no."

Marcus stood up without being asked, which was characteristic, and gave a
speech, which was also characteristic. It lasted ninety seconds. It had three
parts. It ended with the observation that he had translated the words in the
order in which they were written, which was surely the most honest thing a
translator could do, and that the others had all rearranged a sentence that
God had not asked them to rearrange.

It was the best thing anybody said that afternoon.

It was also wrong, and Julia knew exactly why, and said so, and nobody heard
her.

"Julia."

"I said — " She stopped, and started again, at the same volume. "I said the
article is on **λόγος**."

"Louder."

"The article is on **λόγος**," said Julia, "and it is not on **θεός**, and
that is not a decoration, it is the only thing in the sentence that tells us
which word is the subject. Marcus has translated the order. He has not
translated the grammar. Word order is not what carries it. We learned that in
Lesson four. It was the whole of Lesson four."

Marcus sat down.

"You are right," he said, after a moment, in a voice that cost him
something.

"I know."

"You should have said it louder the first time."

"I did say it."

"I know," said Marcus. "That is what I mean."

Theodoros let the room sit with that.

"Note what has just happened," he said, "because it will happen to you for
the rest of your lives and you will usually be on the wrong side of it. The
elegant argument was wrong. The correct argument was inaudible. The elegant
one nearly won." He picked up the first tablet. "A translation is not a
performance. It is a claim about evidence. Let us see whose evidence
survives."
:::

::: newgreek
## No new grammar

There is none in this lesson. Everything you need you already have, and that
is the point: this is the lesson where the parts become a method.

## Four questions

Put these to any Greek sentence, in this order, before you translate a word
of it.

**One. What do the endings say?** Case, number, gender on the nouns; person
and number on the verb. The endings assign the roles, and they do it
regardless of where the words sit. This was Parts I and II, and it was
Sabina's courtroom in Lesson 4: *the witness accused the judge* and *the
judge the witness accused* can be the same sentence in Greek, and the verdict
does not move.

**Two. Where is the article — and where is it not?** The article marks the
subject. It marks what is definite. Its position tells you whether an
adjective is describing or asserting, which was Lesson 10. And its *absence*
is evidence too: a missing article is a fact about the sentence, not a gap in
it.

**Three. What is the aspect?** Not *when* but *what shape*. Imperfect:
going on, unbounded, no end in view. Aorist: the whole thing, as one act. An
infinitive after **βούλομαι** carries shape and no time at all. Part III has
been almost entirely about this.

**Four. What are the small words doing?** **γάρ** is evidence. **οὖν** is a
conclusion. **δέ** is the next step. **μέν** is a promise of a second half.
These are the skeleton of the argument, and they are the first thing a hasty
reader drops.

## And then the fifth question, which is not grammar

Ask it of your own translation, out loud, before you defend it:

**What would have to be true in the Greek for my translation to be wrong?**

If you cannot answer that, you do not have a translation. You have a guess
that has not been tested, and it may well be a good guess, and you still have
no business defending it in front of anybody.

If you *can* answer it, then you know exactly what evidence your reading
rests on — and you will know at once whether somebody has knocked it over.

## Defensible and persuasive

These two words are going to do a lot of work for the rest of the course.

A **persuasive** translation makes the hearer agree. It can do this with
rhythm, with confidence, with a good speaking voice, with being first, with
being longer than the other one.

A **defensible** translation can name the feature of the Greek it rests on.
*The article is on this word and not on that one.* *The ending is accusative.*
*The verb is imperfect, so the action is not finished.*

The two are not opposites, and the best translation is both. But when they
come apart — and they come apart constantly — the defensible one is the one
that is actually about the Greek.

::: note
**A test you can apply to yourself.** When somebody disagrees with your
translation, listen to what you say next.

If you say *because the article is on **λόγος***, you are defending.

If you say *because it sounds better*, or *because that is how it is usually
said*, or *because my translation has a rhythm to it* — you are persuading,
and you may be right, and you have not given a reason.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin cannot help you here, and the reason is important.**

Latin has no article. None. Not definite, not indefinite. A Latin writer
cannot make the distinction that the whole of this lesson turns on, and a
Latin translator meeting it in Greek has to throw it away or replace it with
something else.

This is the first time in the course that your Latin is not merely unhelpful
but *unable*. It will not be the last. Hold on to the feeling: it is what a
translator feels when the target language has no room for what the source
language said, and it is the oldest problem in the trade.
:::
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory, and quickly. Nothing here is from this lesson; the imperfects
and aorists are mixed on purpose.

1. **ἔλαβε** — tense; what is its present?
2. **ἐπίνομεν** — tense and person.
3. **τοῦ παιδός** — case, and the nominative.
4. Translate **ὁ βίος**. Which other word for *life* do you know, and from
   which lesson?
5. **ἐλθεῖν** — what form is this?
6. Where does **γάρ** stand in its clause?
7. **οὕς** — case, number and gender.
8. **ἔπεσε** / **ἔπιπτε** — which is the aorist, and what told you?
9. Translate **ὁ πολύτροπος ἀνήρ**.
10. **δύναται ψεύδεσθαι** — translate.
:::

::: answers
1. Aorist — stem **λαβ-**, not the present **λαμβαν-**. Present
   **λαμβάνω**. (Lesson 14.)
2. Imperfect, 1st plural — *we were drinking*. (Lessons 13 and 16.)
   **ἐπίομεν** would be the aorist.
3. Genitive singular of **ὁ παῖς**. (Lesson 17.) The article says so
   before the ending does.
4. *Life*, as it is lived — a career, a manner of living. (Lesson 17.) The
   other is **ἡ ζωή**, Lesson 5: life as against death. A pupil who gives
   the same English for both is the translator the vocabulary note is
   about.
5. The aorist infinitive of **ἦλθον** — *to come, to go*. (Lesson 16.) A
   pupil who says *imperative* has confused it with Lesson 26, which has
   not happened yet.
6. Second — never first. (Lesson 17.)
7. Accusative plural masculine of **ὅς** — *whom*. (Lesson 17.) The
   article's **τούς** with the **τ** knocked off.
8. **ἔπεσε** is the aorist: stem **πεσ-** against the present stem
   **πιπτ-** in **ἔπιπτε**, *was falling*. (Lesson 14.)
9. *The man of many turns* — or *the resourceful man*. (Lesson 15.)
10. *He is able to lie.* (Lesson 16.) Job one; no time in it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate, then defend"}
For each sentence, give a translation and then name one feature of the Greek
that your translation rests on — an ending, an article, an aspect, or a small
word.

1. **ὁ μὲν σοφὸς ἀνὴρ ἐθέλει μανθάνειν, ὁ δὲ κακὸς οὐ βούλεται.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος, ὃς ἔλεγε τὴν ἀλήθειαν, εἶπε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις τὸν λόγον.**
3. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν θεὸν ἀγαθὸν εἶναι· ἡ γὰρ σοφία δῶρόν ἐστιν.**
4. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν.**
5. **οὗτος μὲν ὁ παῖς τὴν ἀλήθειαν εἶπεν, ὁ δὲ ἄλλος ψεύδεται.**
6. **ὁ ξένος ὃν ὁ Κύκλωψ ἔβλεπεν Οὖτις ἦν· ὁ γὰρ δόλος σοφία ἦν.** *(**ὁ
   Κύκλωψ** = the Cyclops)*
7. **πάντα τὰ ἔργα τοῦ ἀνδρὸς καλὰ ἦν.**
8. **ὁ κριτὴς λέγει τοὺς μάρτυρας ψεύδεσθαι· οὐ γὰρ εἶπον τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
   *(**τοὺς μάρτυρας** = accusative plural of **ὁ μάρτυς**)*
9. **σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ.** / **ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ.** — translate both, and defend
   the difference.
10. **τότε μὲν ἔλεγεν ὁ διδάσκαλος, νῦν δὲ οἱ παῖδες λέγουσιν.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The wise man is willing to learn, but the bad man does not want to.* —
   rests on **μέν … δέ**, which makes the two halves a balance, and on
   **μανθάνειν**, an infinitive completing **ἐθέλει**. A pupil may also
   point to **σοφός** inside the article group with **ἀνήρ**: attributive,
   describing, Lesson 10.
2. *The teacher, who was speaking the truth, said the word to the people.* —
   rests on **ὅς**, masculine nominative, so its antecedent is **ὁ
   διδάσκαλος** and not **τὴν ἀλήθειαν**; on **ἔλεγε**, imperfect, so the
   speaking was going on; and on **εἶπε**, aorist, so the saying was one
   act. The contrast between the two verbs is the sentence.
3. *We know that God is good; for wisdom is a gift.* — rests on **τὸν θεόν**
   accusative with **εἶναι**, which is the indirect statement of Lesson 16,
   and on **γάρ**, which marks the second clause as the reason for the
   first.
4. *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God.* — rests on
   **ἀρχῇ** dative after **ἐν**; on **ὁ λόγος** nominative with the article,
   the subject both times; on **πρός** with the accusative, *towards, in
   the presence of*, Lesson 6; and on **ἦν**, imperfect — a state, not an
   event. They should recognise it as the afternoon's sentence before
   they finish reading it.
5. *This boy told the truth, but the other one is lying.* — rests on
   **μέν … δέ** for the balance; on **εἶπεν**, aorist, one act in the
   past, against **ψεύδεται**, present, still going on; and on **οὗτος**
   outside the article group, Lesson 17.
6. *The stranger whom the Cyclops was watching was Nobody; for the trick
   was wisdom.* — rests on **ὅν**, accusative, so the stranger is the
   object of **ἔβλεπεν** and the Cyclops its subject; on **ἔβλεπεν**,
   imperfect, the watching going on; and on **γάρ**. **Οὖτις** with the
   capital is the name; a pupil who translates *the stranger was not
   anyone* has made the Cyclops's mistake, and should be told what good
   company they are in.
7. *All the works of the man were fine.* — rests on **ἦν**, singular, with
   the neuter plural subject **πάντα τὰ ἔργα**: Lesson 7's rule, and the
   single most defensible thing in the sentence, because a pupil who
   translated *was* has misread the subject. **τοῦ ἀνδρός**, genitive of
   **ἀνήρ**, Lesson 15.
8. *The judge says that the witnesses are lying; for they did not tell the
   truth.* — rests on **τοὺς μάρτυρας** accusative with **ψεύδεσθαι**, the
   indirect statement; on **ψεύδεσθαι** present, the lying going on at the
   time of the saying; on **εἶπον**, aorist, which must here be *they*
   because the witnesses are plural; and on **γάρ**. A pupil who translates
   **εἶπον** as *I said* has lost the thread of the sentence.
9. *The man is wise* / *the wise man*. — rests on the position of **σοφός**
   relative to the article: outside the group it asserts, inside it
   describes. Lesson 10, and the only feature in either phrase, which is
   why the pair is here. The first is a sentence and the second is not;
   a pupil who notices that has defended both at once.
10. *Then the teacher was speaking, but now the children are speaking.* —
    rests on **μέν … δέ** carrying **τότε** and **νῦν**; on **ἔλεγεν**,
    imperfect, the speaking going on in the past; on **λέγουσιν**, present
    plural, agreeing with **οἱ παῖδες**. Theodoros's description of his
    own classroom, and they may say so.

Mark the *defence*, not the translation. A pupil with a slightly clumsy
English version and a correct account of the grammar has done better work
than a pupil with a fluent version and nothing to say about it, and should
be told so in front of the class.
:::

1. In the beginning was the word.
2. The wise man wants to learn; the bad man does not.
3. We know that the word was God.
4. The teacher said the truth to the people.
5. In the beginning the word was with God.
6. This man was just; for he always told the truth.
7. We know that the unjust man is not a friend.
8. The teacher, who was speaking, saw the child.
9. The judge said that the witness was lying.
10. The wise man said these things; so the children learned.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.** — Lesson 6, and they should recognise it before
   they have finished writing it.
2. **ὁ μὲν σοφὸς βούλεται μανθάνειν, ὁ δὲ κακὸς οὐ βούλεται.**
3. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν λόγον θεὸν εἶναι.** — this is the afternoon's sentence
   put through Lesson 16. Note what happened to the article: it is still on
   **λόγος**, which is still the thing being talked about, and it has
   followed **λόγος** into the accusative. **θεόν** still has none. Ask the
   class what that tells them, and let them find it.

   A sharp pupil will object that the English says *was* and **εἶναι** is
   present. They are right to object, and the answer is that Greek has no
   imperfect infinitive: the present infinitive does that work too. Tell
   them so, and tell them it was a good objection.
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος εἶπε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — accept any order of
   the three groups after the verb. If somebody objects that a different
   order is a different sentence, the answer is Lesson 4, and they should be
   made to give it themselves.
5. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν.** — **πρός** with the accusative,
   Lesson 6. Accept **ἦν ὁ λόγος**. The second clause of the afternoon's
   verse, and they should recognise this one too.
6. **οὗτος ὁ ἀνὴρ δίκαιος ἦν· ἀεὶ γὰρ τὴν ἀλήθειαν ἔλεγεν.** — **οὗτος**
   outside the article group; **γάρ** second; **ἔλεγεν** imperfect with
   **ἀεί**. A pupil who writes **εἶπεν** has made a habit into a single
   act, and should be asked how many times.
7. **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν ἄδικον οὐ φίλον εἶναι.** — **φίλον** accusative to
   agree with **τὸν ἄδικον**; the **οὐ** belongs with **φίλον**, not with
   **γιγνώσκομεν**. Lesson 16's sentence about the Cyclops, with new
   furniture.
8. **ὁ διδάσκαλος, ὃς ἔλεγεν, εἶδε τὸν παῖδα.** — **ὅς** nominative, the
   subject of its own clause; **εἶδε** the aorist from Lesson 15 (accept
   **ἔβλεψε**, and ask whether they remember which word the lesson gave);
   **τὸν παῖδα** the third-declension accusative.
9. **ὁ κριτὴς ἔλεγε τὸν μάρτυρα ψεύδεσθαι.** — **ἔλεγε**, not **εἶπε**:
   in Attic **εἶπε** with an accusative and infinitive usually means
   *ordered*, and a statement after **εἶπε** goes with **ὅτι**. Accept
   **ὁ κριτὴς εἶπεν ὅτι ὁ μάρτυς ψεύδεται**. The infinitive is present
   although the English has *was lying*, because the lying was going on
   at the time of the saying and the infinitive takes its time from the
   main verb. This is the same point as the sharp pupil's objection at
   number 3, and a class that has heard that answer should be able to
   produce this one.
10. **ταῦτα εἶπεν ὁ σοφός· οἱ οὖν παῖδες ἔμαθον.** — **ταῦτα** neuter
    plural; **οὖν** second, behind **οἱ**; **ἔμαθον** the second aorist of
    **μανθάνω** from Lesson 14, here *they*. Watch for **οὖν οἱ παῖδες**,
    still, from somebody.
:::

::: {.exercise title="The fifth question"}
Take your own answer to number 3 above — **γιγνώσκομεν τὸν λόγον θεὸν
εἶναι**.

1. What would have to be true in the Greek for your translation to be wrong?
2. Now do the same for **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**.
3. One of the two was much easier to answer. Why?
4. Now **ὁ διδάσκαλος εἶπε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις τὴν ἀλήθειαν**, your number 4.
   What would have to be true for your translation to be wrong? Does the
   order of the words come into it?
5. **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.** What would have to be true in the Greek for Julia's
   *the Word was God* to be wrong? And for Marcus's *God was the Word* to
   be right?
6. Felix wrote *a god*. What would have to be true in the Greek for his
   translation to be *required*, not merely allowed? Is there any such
   feature in Greek?
7. **πάντα τὰ ἔργα τοῦ ἀνδρὸς καλὰ ἦν.** You translated *were*. What would
   have to be true in the Greek for *were* to be wrong — and does the
   singular **ἦν** make it wrong?
8. **ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ** against **σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ** — what single change to each
   phrase would make your translation of it wrong?
9. Go back over your answers to 1–8 and say which of the four questions —
   endings, article, aspect, small words — each defence rested on most.
   Which question did you lean on most often?
10. Write one Greek sentence of your own that has only one way to be wrong,
    and one that has three. Say which is the more interesting sentence,
    and why.
:::

::: answers
1. **τὸν λόγον** would have to be nominative, or **θεόν** would have to carry
   the article, or **εἶναι** would have to be a finite verb. Any of the three
   would break the reading.
2. Very little. There is almost nothing in it that could be otherwise:
   **ἀρχῇ** is dative after **ἐν**, **ὁ λόγος** is nominative and has the
   article, **ἦν** is third singular. The sentence is about as unambiguous
   as Greek gets.
3. Because the second sentence has fewer places where a choice is being
   made. A sentence is hard to defend exactly where it is interesting, and
   the number of ways a translation could be wrong is a fair measure of how
   much the translator is actually deciding.
4. **τοῖς ἀνθρώποις** would have to be some case other than the dative, or
   **τὴν ἀλήθειαν** some case other than the accusative, or **εἶπε** some
   person other than the third. The order does not come into it: Lesson 4.
   A pupil who says *it would be wrong if the words were in a different
   order* has answered from English.
5. For Julia to be wrong, **θεός** would have to carry the article and
   **λόγος** not — then the sentence would be about God. For Marcus to be
   right, the same. His translation and hers rest on opposite readings of
   the same evidence, and the evidence is the one article, which is why
   the trial turned on it.
6. There is no such feature. Greek has no indefinite article, so nothing
   in the Greek can *require* *a god*; the absence of the article permits
   it and does not compel it, and a Greek predicate noun often has no
   article when it is entirely definite. Felix's reading is allowed by the
   grammar and not produced by it — which is the Voice's point, and this
   is where the pupil should arrive at it for themselves.
7. Nothing in the verb could make it wrong. A neuter plural subject takes a
   singular verb (Lesson 7), so **ἦν** is exactly what the grammar
   requires, and English must still say *were*. For *were* to be wrong the
   *subject* would have to be singular — **τὸ ἔργον … καλὸν ἦν** — and
   then **καλά** would have to change too. The defence rests on the
   endings of **τὰ ἔργα** and **καλά**, not on the verb; a pupil who
   answered *if **ἦν** were singular* has not noticed that it already is.
8. Move **σοφός** across the article: **σοφὸς ὁ ἀνήρ** becomes *the wise
   man* once **σοφός** goes inside, and **ὁ σοφὸς ἀνήρ** becomes *the man
   is wise* once it comes out. One word, one step, and the translation
   reverses. Lesson 10 is the whole defence.
9. Answers will vary; typically the article (1, 5, 6, 8) and the endings
   (1, 2, 4, 7), with aspect hardly appearing — because this exercise's
   sentences were built to turn on the first two. Say so, and say that the
   Reading below is built to turn on the third.
10. Answers will vary. A sentence like **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν ἵππον** has
    about one way to be wrong; one like **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** has three or
    four. The second is the more interesting, and the reason is question
    3 again: the places a translation could go wrong are the places the
    translator is actually deciding something, and a sentence with none
    of them needs no translator.
:::

::: {.voice source="John 1:1c"}
Five words. You have had all five since Part I.

**καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

- **καί** — and
- **θεός** — God *(nominative — and **no article**)*
- **ἦν** — was *(imperfect, Lesson 13: was, and went on being)*
- **ὁ λόγος** — the Word *(nominative — **with** the article)*

Here is what the class handed in.

| | Translation |
|:--|:--|
| Marcus | *and God was the Word* |
| Julia | *and the Word was God* |
| Felix | *and the Word was a god* |
| Quintus | *and the Word was divine* |
| Sabina | *and what God was, the Word was* |
| Theodosius | *et Deus erat Verbum* |

**Start with the two nominatives.** Both **θεός** and **ὁ λόγος** are
nominative, so the endings alone cannot tell you which one the sentence is
about. Greek has a second device for exactly this situation, and it is the
article.

**ὁ λόγος** has it. **θεός** does not. The article marks the subject — this
is Lesson 3, and it has been load-bearing ever since. So the sentence is
about **ὁ λόγος**, and **θεός** is what is being said about it.

**Now Marcus.** His translation follows the Greek word order exactly, and
that is what is wrong with it. In English, the word in front of *was* is the
subject, so *God was the Word* tells an English reader that the sentence is
about God. The Greek says it is about the Word. Marcus has reproduced the
order and reversed the grammar, and the reason he could do it without
noticing is that English assigns roles by position and Greek does not.

That is Lesson 4, three months later, with something at stake.

**Why is θεός in front, then?** Because Greek puts a word first for emphasis,
and the article is doing the work of marking the subject, so the writer is
free to. The fronting is not an accident and it is not nothing. It is simply
not what assigns the roles.

**And Theodosius.** He wrote out the Latin, and the Latin has the same word
order as the Greek, and Latin has no article at all — so *et Deus erat
Verbum* cannot make the distinction we have just spent ten minutes making. A
Latin reader has to decide from the surrounding argument. The distinction is
not mistranslated. It is *untranslatable*, into that language, and it is gone.

**Four translations are left.** Julia's, Felix's, Quintus's and Sabina's all
make **ὁ λόγος** the subject. All four are consistent with the grammar so
far.

And here the grammar stops.

A Greek predicate noun very often drops the article even when it is entirely
definite — so Felix cannot argue straight from *no article* to *a god*; that
inference does not hold as a rule. But neither does the grammar by itself
force *God* rather than *divine*. What is left is a question about what an
anarthrous predicate noun in this position is doing, and that is a question
about usage and about the rest of the book, not about these five words.

**This is the honest end of the lesson.** The grammar eliminates one
translation outright, shows a second to be describing a distinction its
language cannot carry, and narrows the rest. It does not choose between them.
People in the city outside this room are arguing about this sentence right
now, with a heat you would find alarming, and the argument is not going to be
settled by anything in this chapter.

What the grammar has given you is the ability to tell the difference between
an argument about this sentence and a feeling about it. That is a smaller
thing than certainty, and it is worth more.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The Greek is John 1:1c and follows the standard
text (see *Sources*); check it, with the punctuation, against the
edition in use.

**On the doctrinal question.** This passage is live, and the lesson is
deliberately built to teach what the grammar does and does not determine and
to stop there. That is not evasion: the four surviving renderings genuinely
differ on grounds that are not grammatical, and a course that pretended
otherwise would be teaching pupils to dress a conviction in evidence, which
is the exact habit the whole of Part III exists to break.

If you teach in a confessional setting and wish to say where your tradition
stands, say it — and say it *after* the grammar, and mark plainly that you
are now speaking as a believer and not as a grammarian. Pupils can hold both.
What damages them is the two being run together without a seam.

**Felix's translation deserves a straight answer, not a frown.** His
reasoning — no article, therefore indefinite — is a reasonable inference from
what he has been taught, and a pupil who makes it has been paying attention.
The answer is that Greek regularly omits the article from a predicate noun
that is nonetheless definite, which is a fact about usage he has not met yet.
Tell him that, and tell him his inference was sound and his premise
incomplete.

**On the arc.** This is the lesson in which to say, if you are going to say
it, that every pupil in the room has got better at something they began badly
at — Julia at speaking up, Marcus at stopping, Felix at reading rather than
guessing. They have now watched it happen for eighteen lessons, which is why
it can be said here and could not have been said in Lesson 1. A child who
decided in Lesson 4 that they were bad at Greek has usually decided they are
bad at one of four things, and the four questions at the head of this lesson
are how you show them the other three.
:::

::: {.reading title="The five words in their place" source="John 1:1–5 (NA28), with an adapted version"}
The trial was about five words. Here are the five verses they sit in —
twice. The first version was written for you, in Attic, with the words and
forms you have; it is **adapted**, and labelled so; its last paragraph is
not in John at all, but is the afternoon's sentence put through Lesson 16.
The second is the Gospel's own Greek, which is Koine — the common Greek of
the Roman world, four centuries after Plato. You will find that nearly all
of it is already yours.

**Adapted**

ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος. ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. οὗτος ὁ
λόγος ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν.

πάντα ἐγένετο διὰ τοῦ λόγου, χωρὶς δὲ τοῦ λόγου οὐδὲ ἓν ἔργον ἐγένετο. ἐν
τῷ λόγῳ ἦν ἡ ζωή, καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων. τὸ δὲ φῶς ἐν τῷ σκότῳ
φαίνει, καὶ ὁ σκότος τὸ φῶς οὐκ ἔλαβεν.

οὕτω γράφει ὁ Ἰωάννης. λέγει οὖν τὸν λόγον ἐν ἀρχῇ εἶναι, καὶ πρὸς τὸν
θεὸν εἶναι, καὶ θεὸν εἶναι· λέγει δὲ καὶ τὴν ζωὴν ἐν τῷ λόγῳ εἶναι, καὶ
τὴν ζωὴν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων εἶναι.

**John 1:1–5**

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.
οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν. πάντα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ χωρὶς αὐτοῦ
ἐγένετο οὐδὲ ἕν. ὃ γέγονεν ἐν αὐτῷ ζωὴ ἦν, καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν
ἀνθρώπων· καὶ τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει, καὶ ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Ἰωάννης** — John *(the writer, named only in the adapted version)*
- **διά** — through *(with the genitive; **δι᾽** before a vowel)*
- **χωρίς** — without, apart from *(with the genitive)*
- **οὐδὲ ἕν** — not even one thing *(**ἕν** is the neuter of **εἷς**, one;
  the adapted version gives it a noun to lean on)*
- **τὸ φῶς, φωτός** — light *(Lesson 27)*
- **ὁ σκότος** — darkness *(the Attic word, in the adapted version)*
- **φαίνει** — shines *(3rd singular of **φαίνω**)*
- **οὗτος** — this one, he *(Lesson 17; in John it stands alone, as a
  pronoun — a thing a demonstrative can do and an Attic article cannot)*
- **πάντα** — all things *(neuter plural of **πᾶς**, used as a noun)*
- **αὐτοῦ, αὐτῷ, αὐτό** — of him, in him, it *(the pronoun **αὐτός**,
  Lesson 21: genitive, dative, and neuter accusative — the last standing
  for **τὸ φῶς**)*
- **ὃ γέγονεν** — what has come to be *(**ὅ**, the neuter relative;
  **γέγονεν**, a perfect of **γίγνομαι**, the verb of **ἐγένετο** — take
  it whole; Part VI)*
- **ἡ σκοτία** — darkness *(the Koine word; Attic has **ὁ σκότος**)*
- **κατέλαβεν** — overcame; or grasped, understood *(aorist of
  **καταλαμβάνω**: **κατά** + **ἔλαβεν**, Lesson 14's aorist with a prefix;
  both meanings are alive in the word, Lesson 27)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the adapted version aloud, then John, slowly. Then answer.

1. How many times does **ἦν** occur in John's first two verses? Which tense
   is it, and what does that tense say about the kind of being described?
2. **ἐγένετο** in verse 3 — which tense? State the grammatical contrast
   with **ἦν** in verses 1–2: what shape of action does each tense give.
   Give the grammar only; what it means is the argument in the city
   outside, and is not asked.
3. **οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ** — **οὗτος** stands alone with no noun. Which word in
   the Homer of Lesson 15 did the same thing, and why can a demonstrative
   do in Attic what an article cannot?
4. **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** — put the four questions to it, in order, and say
   exactly where the grammar stops.
5. **ὃ γέγονεν** — the standard text puts a full stop before these words,
   so that they begin a new sentence: *what has come to be, in him was
   life*. Many older editions and most English Bibles attach them to the
   sentence before: *without him not one thing came to be that has come to
   be*. The Greek letters are identical. What differs, and can the grammar
   decide?
6. **ἡ σκοτία αὐτὸ οὐ κατέλαβεν** — the gloss gives **κατέλαβεν** two
   meanings. The adapted version chose **οὐκ ἔλαβεν**. What did it keep
   and what did it lose?
7. In the adapted version's last paragraph, **λέγει τὸν λόγον … θεὸν
   εἶναι** — which word has the article and which has not? Has the
   indirect statement kept the evidence the trial rested on?
8. **τὸ φῶς … φαίνει** is the only present-tense verb in the five verses;
   everything else is imperfect or aorist, apart from the perfect
   **γέγονεν**. What does the change of tense
   do to the sentence it is in?
9. Find two things in John's Greek that an Attic writer would do
   differently, using the gloss list and Lesson 17. (One is a word; one is
   the way the clauses are joined.)
10. Sabina's trial asked which translation could be *defended*. Choose one
    sentence from John 1:1–5 other than **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**, give a
    translation, and name the feature of the Greek it rests on.
:::

::: answers
1. Four: **ἦν ὁ λόγος**, **ὁ λόγος ἦν**, **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**, **οὗτος
   ἦν**. Imperfect, Lesson 13: a state going on, with no beginning or end
   in view. The verses describe a being that *was*, continuously, without
   saying when it started.
2. Aorist, Lesson 14: a thing that happened, looked at whole. The contrast
   is between being (**ἦν**, unbounded) and coming to be (**ἐγένετο**, an
   event). The Word is described with the first and *all things* with the
   second. That is as far as the grammar goes, and a pupil who stops there
   has done what was asked; a pupil who goes further is now talking
   theology, which is a different lesson and a different building.
3. **ὁ** in *Odyssey* 1.9 — **αὐτὰρ ὁ τοῖσιν ἀφείλετο**, *but he took away
   from them*. A demonstrative is a pointing word and can point on its own;
   the Attic article has become a label that needs a noun to label. Homer's
   **ὁ** was still a demonstrative; **οὗτος** always has been.
4. Endings: both **θεός** and **ὁ λόγος** are nominative — no help. Article:
   on **λόγος**, not on **θεός** — so **ὁ λόγος** is the subject, and
   Marcus is out. Aspect: **ἦν**, imperfect — a state. Small words: **καί**,
   which joins and does nothing else. The grammar stops at the article:
   it has made **ὁ λόγος** the subject and **θεός** the predicate, and it
   has nothing to say about whether that predicate means *God*, *a god*
   or *divine*. The Voice's answer, reached by the method.
5. What differs is where the sentence breaks, which decides whether **ὃ
   γέγονεν** is the subject of the sentence about life or the tail of the
   sentence about making. The grammar cannot decide: the Greek of the
   earliest manuscripts was written with no punctuation and no spaces,
   and both readings are good Greek. This is a case where the edition has
   made a choice the text does not make, and a reader should know that
   the full stop in a printed Greek Bible is an editor's. (Theodoros's
   scroll, in AD 400, would have had none.)
6. It kept *take* — **ἔλαβεν**, Lesson 14, the darkness did not *take* the
   light — which covers *overcome* reasonably well. It lost *grasp,
   understand*, the second sense of **καταλαμβάνω**, which the Gospel may
   well want at the same time: the darkness neither put out the light nor
   understood it. The class met the same kind of double sense in
   **Οὖτις** two lessons ago, and should be reminded.
7. **τὸν λόγον** has the article and **θεόν** has not — exactly as in the
   original, with both now in the accusative. Yes: the article followed
   **λόγος** into the indirect statement and **θεός** is still bare, so a
   reader can still tell which is subject and which predicate. The
   evidence survived the change of construction. (This is number 3 of the
   Into Greek exercise, and a pupil who recognises it has been listening.)
8. It brings the sentence into the present: everything before it was
   *then* — in the beginning, came to be — and this is *now*: the light
   *shines*, still, as the writer writes. Then the aorist **κατέλαβεν**
   drops back into the past for the one thing that did not happen. Two
   tenses in a verse, each placed.
9. **σκοτία** for Attic **σκότος** (a word); and the rhythm of **καί …
   καί … καί**, joining clause to clause with no **δέ** or **γάρ**
   anywhere — which is Hebrew habit showing through Greek, and which a
   pupil who has just done Lesson 17 may feel as an absence before they
   can name it. A pupil who offers **οὗτος** standing alone should be
   reminded that Attic does exactly that (question 3); one who offers the
   full stop before **ὃ γέγονεν** should be told that Attic texts are
   punctuated by their editors too.
10. Answers will vary. For example: **ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων** — *the
    life was the light of men* — rests on both nouns having the article,
    so that neither is marked as predicate by its absence; the subject is
    the one that carries on from the previous clause, **ἡ ζωή**, and the
    defence is partly grammatical and partly about the flow of the
    sentence. A pupil who picks this one and notices that its defence is
    *weaker* than the article-defence of verse 1c has understood the
    whole lesson. Accept any sentence with a named feature; mark the
    defence.
:::
