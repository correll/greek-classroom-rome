# The Past Comes Alive

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"A story is not a list," said Quintus.

Nobody had asked him, but he had been building to it for some minutes and
there was no stopping it now.

"If I say: *he came in, he sat down, he spoke, he left* — that is four
things, one after another. That is a list. But if I say: *the rain was
falling, and the lamp was going out, and nobody was speaking* — that is not
four things. That is a *situation*. It is the same afternoon seen from
inside instead of counted up from outside."

"Go on," said Theodoros.

"There is no going on. That is the thought."

"It is a better thought than you realise. Say it again, slowly, and listen
to your own verbs."

Quintus said it again. Partway through he stopped.

"...*came, sat, spoke, left*," he said. "And then *was falling, was going
out, was speaking*."

"And?"

"And in English I changed the shape of the verb to do it." He looked
faintly annoyed. "I did not do that on purpose."

"No. English did it for you, quietly, as it does most things." Theodoros
stood up. "Greek does it on purpose, with an ending, and it has done so for
a thousand years before you were born. You have just described the
difference between two Greek tenses without knowing either of them, which
is the most efficient piece of work anybody has done in this room since
September."

Marcus said that he did not see what the fuss was about, and that a past
was a past.

"Keep that," said Theodoros. "You will want it next week."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## A past with a shape

The **imperfect** is a past tense, and it is not simply *the past*. It
describes an action that was **going on**, **kept happening**, or **used to
happen** — a situation rather than an event.

> **ἔλεγον** — I was saying; I kept saying; I used to say

Not *I said*. That is a different tense, and it arrives next lesson.

## Two things happen to the verb

**At the front, an augment.** The imperfect puts **ἐ-** before the stem.
That prefix is the single clearest signal in Greek that you are looking at a
past tense:

> **λέγω** → **ἔλεγον**  ·  **ἀκούω** → **ἤκουον**  ·  **φέρω** → **ἔφερον**

When the verb already begins with a vowel, the augment cannot simply be
stuck on the front, so the vowel lengthens instead: **ἀ-** becomes **ἠ-**,
**ἐ-** becomes **ἠ-**. Hence **ἀκούω → ἤκουον**. The augment is still
there; it has been absorbed.

**At the back, a new set of endings.**

| | **λέγω** *present* | **ἔλεγον** *imperfect* |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λέγω** | **ἔλεγον** |
| 2 sing. | **λέγεις** | **ἔλεγες** |
| 3 sing. | **λέγει** | **ἔλεγε(ν)** |
| 1 plur. | **λέγομεν** | **ἐλέγομεν** |
| 2 plur. | **λέγετε** | **ἐλέγετε** |
| 3 plur. | **λέγουσι(ν)** | **ἔλεγον** |

::: note
**The first and third person singular look identical: ἔλεγον.** This is not
a misprint and it is not fixable. Greek lived with it quite happily, because
in a real sentence the subject is almost never in doubt.

When it is in doubt, a writer adds **ἐγώ** — which is exactly the use of the
pronoun you learned in Lesson 11. The emphatic pronoun turns out to have a
second job: removing an ambiguity the endings cannot.
:::

## εἰμί in the past

You have already read this verb in the past tense, in Lesson 6, without
being able to name it.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἦν** |
| 2 sing. | **ἦσθα** |
| 3 sing. | **ἦν** |
| 1 plur. | **ἦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἦτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἦσαν** |

**ἦν** is *was* — and, because it is an imperfect, *was and went on being*.
Hold on to that; it matters this afternoon.

::: latinbridge
Latin has an imperfect that does the same job — *dicebam*, *I was saying*,
*I used to say* — and if you have met it, the sense transfers exactly.

Latin marks it with **-ba-** in the middle of the word. Greek marks it with
an augment at the front and new endings at the back. Same idea, opposite
ends of the word.

What Latin does **not** have is the distinction you meet next lesson. Keep
Marcus's objection in mind: he is about to be right about Latin and wrong
about Greek.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἔλεγον** — I was saying *(imperfect of **λέγω**)*
- **ἦν** — he, she, or it was *(imperfect of **εἰμί**)*
- **τότε** — then, at that time
- **ἀεί** — always, ever
- **πάλιν** — again, back

::: note
**ἀεί** is the word in the motto of more schools than anyone has counted,
and it means *always* in the strong sense — continuously, without
interruption. It belongs with the imperfect the way *suddenly* belongs with
the aorist you meet next week.

**πάλιν** gives English *palindrome* — a thing that runs *back* again.
:::
:::

::: {.investigation time="15 min"}
Theodoros set the class to turning situations into events and back again.

::: {.exercise title="Present or past?"}
Say which tense, which person, and translate.

1. **ἔλεγες**
2. **ἀκούομεν**
3. **ἔφερον**
4. **ἐδίδασκε**
5. **ἦσαν**
6. **μανθάνουσιν**
:::

::: answers
1. Imperfect, 2nd singular — *you were saying.* The augment **ἐ-** gives it
   away before the ending does.
2. Present, 1st plural — *we hear.*
3. Imperfect — and either 1st singular (*I was carrying*) or 3rd plural
   (*they were carrying*). Both are **ἔφερον**; only a sentence can decide.
4. Imperfect, 3rd singular — *he was teaching, he used to teach.*
5. Imperfect, 3rd plural of **εἰμί** — *they were.*
6. Present, 3rd plural — *they learn.*

Question 3 is the one to dwell on. A pupil who gives only one answer has not
noticed the ambiguity; a pupil who gives both has read the table.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **τότε ἐλέγομεν τοὺς λόγους τῆς ἀληθείας.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἀεὶ ἐδίδασκε τοὺς υἱοὺς τῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
3. **ἤκουον πάλιν τὴν ἐπιστολήν, ἀλλὰ οὐκ ἐμάνθανον.**
4. **ἐν τῇ ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Then we were saying the words of truth.*
2. *The teacher was always teaching the sons of the men.* — **ἀεί** with an
   imperfect is the characteristic pairing: a thing that kept on happening.
3. *I kept hearing the letter again, but I was not understanding it.* Or
   *they kept hearing … they were not understanding* — **ἤκουον** and
   **ἐμάνθανον** are both ambiguous, and both must be resolved the same way.
4. *In the beginning was the Word.* They have seen this before; they can
   now name the tense.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I was saying the words.
2. They were always learning.
3. He was teaching the son.
4. We were not hearing the letter.
5. Then the gifts were small.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἔλεγον τοὺς λόγους.**
2. **ἀεὶ ἐμάνθανον.**
3. **ἐδίδασκε τὸν υἱόν.**
4. **οὐκ ἠκούομεν τὴν ἐπιστολήν.** — the augment lengthens the **ἀ-** to
   **ἠ-**, and **οὐκ** is chosen because the verb now begins with a vowel
   carrying a smooth breathing.
5. **τότε μικρὰ ἦν τὰ δῶρα.**

Question 4 catches two things at once and is worth setting again next week.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Quintus's distinction"}
Quintus said that *he came in, he sat down, he spoke* is a list, while *the
rain was falling, the lamp was going out* is a situation.

1. Which of those two sets of English verbs would be imperfect in Greek?
2. Write two Greek sentences about the same afternoon, one giving a
   situation and one giving an event, using only the imperfect you have.
3. Why might a storyteller want both? What would a story be like with only
   one?
:::

::: answers
1. The second — *was falling*, *was going out*. The first set is a sequence
   of completed events and belongs to the tense arriving next lesson.
2. Answers will vary; only imperfects are available, so the "event"
   sentence will be imperfect too and will feel wrong. **That is the
   intended discovery.** A pupil who complains that they cannot yet say *he
   sat down* properly has found next week's lesson, and should be told so.
3. A story with only imperfects never moves; a story with only events has no
   weather, no mood, and nobody waiting. Greek narrative uses the two
   against each other constantly, and Part III is mostly about learning to
   hear it.
:::
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 1:1"}
You read this in Lesson 6, when **ἦν** was a word somebody had to tell you.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
God.*

Three **ἦν**. You can now say what they are: third person singular,
imperfect, of **εἰμί**.

And because the imperfect is the tense of *going on*, the line does not say
that the Word existed at a moment in the past. It says it **was, and
continued to be** — through the beginning, and not starting at it. The tense
is carrying a claim the English *was* cannot quite carry, because English
*was* is just as happy reporting a thing that lasted an instant.

This is the first time in this course that a tense, rather than a word, has
been the argument. It will not be the last.

::: note
Greek had another option here and did not take it. There is a tense for
*came to be* — it is the one Marcus is about to meet — and the author of
this line uses it twelve verses later, about the same subject, to say
something entirely different. Next week you will be able to read both and
see the difference.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Quintus described two Greek tenses without knowing either. What does that
   suggest about where grammatical categories come from — the language, or
   the thing being described?
2. *Was, and went on being* takes five English words and one Greek ending.
   Is English worse at this, or just differently shaped?
3. **ἀεί** means *always*. Is there any difference between *he was always
   teaching* and *he always taught*?
:::
