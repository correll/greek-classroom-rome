# The Past Comes Alive

::: story
"A story is not a list," said Quintus.

Nobody had asked him, but he had been building to it, and there was no
stopping it now.

"If I say: *he came in, he sat down, he spoke, he left* — that is four
things, one after another. That is a list. But if I say: *the rain was
falling, and the lamp was going out, and nobody was speaking* — that is not
four things. That is a *situation*. It is the same scene seen from
inside instead of counted up from outside."

"Go on," said Theodoros.

"There is no going on. That is the thought."

"It is a better thought than you realise. Say it again, slowly, and listen
to your own verbs."

Quintus said it again. Partway through he stopped.

"...*came, sat, spoke, left*," he said. "And then *was falling, was going
out, was speaking*."

"And?"

"And in English I changed the shape of the verb to do it." He looked
faintly annoyed. "I did not do that on purpose."

"No. English did it for you, quietly, as it does most things." Theodoros
stood up. "Greek does it on purpose, with an ending, and it has done so for
a thousand years before you were born. You have just described the
difference between two Greek tenses without knowing either of them, which
is the most efficient piece of work anybody has done in this room since
Lesson 1."

Marcus said that he did not see what the fuss was about, and that a past
was a past.

"Keep that," said Theodoros. "You will want it next lesson."
:::

::: newgreek
## A past with a shape

Quintus's second set of verbs — *was falling, was going out, was speaking*
— has a Greek tense of its own, the **imperfect**. It describes an action
that was **going on**, **kept happening**, or **used to happen** — a
situation rather than an event.

> **ἔλεγον** — I was saying; I kept saying; I used to say

Not *I said*. That is a different tense, and it arrives next lesson.

## Two things happen to the verb

**At the front, an augment.** The imperfect puts **ἐ-** before the stem.
That prefix is the single clearest signal in Greek that you are looking at a
past tense:

> **λέγω** → **ἔλεγον**  ·  **ἀκούω** → **ἤκουον**  ·  **φέρω** → **ἔφερον**

When the verb already begins with a vowel, the augment cannot be stuck
on the front, so the vowel lengthens instead: **ἀ-** becomes **ἠ-**,
**ἐ-** becomes **ἠ-**. Hence **ἀκούω → ἤκουον**. The augment is still
there; it has been absorbed.

**At the back, a new set of endings.**

| | **λέγω** *present* | **ἔλεγον** *imperfect* |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λέγω** | **ἔλεγον** |
| 2 sing. | **λέγεις** | **ἔλεγες** |
| 3 sing. | **λέγει** | **ἔλεγε(ν)** |
| 1 plur. | **λέγομεν** | **ἐλέγομεν** |
| 2 plur. | **λέγετε** | **ἐλέγετε** |
| 3 plur. | **λέγουσι(ν)** | **ἔλεγον** |

::: note
**The first person singular and the third person plural look identical:
ἔλεγον.** It is not
a misprint. Greek lived with it, because in a real sentence the subject is
almost never in doubt.

When it is in doubt, a writer adds **ἐγώ** — the emphatic pronoun from
Lesson 11, which turns out to have a second job: removing an ambiguity the
endings cannot.
:::

## εἰμί in the past

You have already read this verb in the past tense, in Lesson 6, without
being able to name it.

| | |
|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἦν** |
| 2 sing. | **ἦσθα** |
| 3 sing. | **ἦν** |
| 1 plur. | **ἦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἦτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἦσαν** |

**ἦν** is *was* — and, because it is an imperfect, *was and went on being*.
Hold on to that; it matters later in this lesson.

::: latinbridge
Latin has an imperfect that does the same job — *dicebam*, *I was saying*,
*I used to say* — and the sense transfers exactly.

Latin marks it with **-ba-** in the middle of the word. Greek marks it with
an augment at the front and new endings at the back. Same idea, opposite
ends of the word.

What Latin does **not** do is carry that distinction beyond the indicative
— which is the part of next lesson Marcus is not expecting. Keep his
objection in mind: he is about to be right about Latin and wrong about
Greek.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἔλεγον** — I was saying *(imperfect of **λέγω**)*
- **ἦν** — he, she, or it was *(imperfect of **εἰμί**)*
- **τότε** — then, at that time
- **ἀεί** — always, ever
- **πάλιν** — again, back
- **χθές** — yesterday
- **ἔτι** — still, yet *(Lesson 6)*
- **οὐδέ** — and not, nor; not even *(Lesson 6)*
- **ὁ μαθητής** — pupil, learner *(declines like **ὁ κριτής**)* *(Lesson 7)*
- **ἄλλος, ἄλλη, ἄλλο** — other, another; **οἱ ἄλλοι** — the others *(Lesson 10)*
- **καθεύδω** — I sleep *(Lesson 5)*
- **μένω** — I stay, remain, wait *(Lesson 5)*
- **ἡ φωνή** — voice, sound *(Lesson 1)*

::: note
**ἀεί** is the word in the motto of more schools than anyone has counted,
and it means *always* in the strong sense — continuously, without
interruption. It belongs with the imperfect the way *suddenly* belongs with
the aorist you meet in Lesson 14.

**πάλιν** gives English *palindrome* — a thing that runs *back* again.

**ὁ μαθητής** is built on **μανθάνω**, *I learn*, from Lesson 11: a pupil is
*one who learns*. The same word, in the Gospels, is *disciple*. **ἡ φωνή**
is in *telephone* and *phonetics*; **ἄλλος** is in *allergy* — a reaction to
*another* thing.
:::

::: investigation
Theodoros set the class to turning situations into events and back again.
First, though, he went back over the last four lessons without warning, as
he had begun to do every lesson.

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory. Nothing here is from this lesson.

1. **τῷ διδασκάλῳ** — name the case and give one thing it can mean.
2. Translate **πέμπω**.
3. **ἀκούετε** — person and number.
4. **ἡ σοφὴ Ἰουλία** and **σοφὴ ἡ Ἰουλία** — which one is a whole sentence?
5. Turn it round so that the judge is the subject: **ὁ υἱὸς βλέπει τὸν
   κριτήν.**
6. Which case does **ἐν** take?
7. Translate **μικρὰ τὰ δῶρα τῶν φίλων.**
8. What is **τὸ μέτρον**?
9. **δίδωσι** — who is giving?
10. Put **ἡ ἐπιστολή** into the dative singular.
:::

::: answers
1. Dative singular — *to the teacher*, *for the teacher*. (Lesson 9.) A
   pupil who says *of the teacher* has confused **-ῳ** with **-ου**; make
   them say the iota subscript aloud.
2. *I send.* (Lesson 9.)
3. Second person plural — *you (all) hear*. (Lesson 11.) The likely slip is
   *they hear*, which is **ἀκούουσι**.
4. The second. **σοφή** outside the article group is a predicate: *Julia is
   wise*. The first is a phrase, *the wise Julia*. (Lesson 10.)
5. **ὁ κριτὴς βλέπει τὸν υἱόν.** Both article and ending must change on both
   nouns. (Lesson 4.)
6. The dative. (Lesson 9.) Watch for *accusative*, by false analogy with
   **πρός**.
7. *The gifts of the friends are small.* No verb; **μικρά** outside the
   article group asserts. (Lessons 10 and 12.)
8. *Measure, standard.* (Lesson 12.)
9. He, she, or it — third person singular. (Lesson 9.)
10. **τῇ ἐπιστολῇ.** The iota subscript on both words is the thing to check.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Present or past?"}
Say which tense, which person, and translate.

1. **ἔλεγες**
2. **ἀκούομεν**
3. **ἔφερον**
4. **ἐδίδασκε**
5. **ἦσαν**
6. **μανθάνουσιν**
7. **ἦσθα**
8. **γράφετε**
9. **ἐπέμπομεν**
10. **ἦν**
:::

::: answers
1. Imperfect, 2nd singular — *you were saying.* The augment **ἐ-** gives it
   away before the ending does.
2. Present, 1st plural — *we hear.*
3. Imperfect — and either 1st singular (*I was carrying*) or 3rd plural
   (*they were carrying*). Both are **ἔφερον**; only a sentence can decide.
4. Imperfect, 3rd singular — *he was teaching, he used to teach.*
5. Imperfect, 3rd plural of **εἰμί** — *they were.*
6. Present, 3rd plural — *they learn.*
7. Imperfect, 2nd singular of **εἰμί** — *you were.* The odd one in the
   table, and the one pupils forget first.
8. Present, 2nd plural — *you write.* No augment, so not past; a pupil who
   says *you were writing* has let the **-ετε** mislead them, because the
   imperfect shares it (**ἐγράφετε**). The front of the word decides.
9. Imperfect, 1st plural — *we were sending.*
10. Imperfect of **εἰμί** — and either 1st singular (*I was*) or 3rd singular
    (*he, she, it was*). The same ambiguity as 3, in a different place.

Questions 3 and 10 are the ones to dwell on. A pupil who gives only one
answer has not noticed the ambiguity; a pupil who gives both has read the
table.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **τότε ἐλέγομεν τοὺς λόγους τῆς ἀληθείας.**
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἀεὶ ἐδίδασκε τοὺς υἱοὺς τῶν ἀνθρώπων.**
3. **ἤκουον πάλιν τὴν ἐπιστολήν, ἀλλὰ οὐκ ἐμάνθανον.**
4. **ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος.**
5. **ὁ ἵππος ἐκάθευδεν, ἀλλὰ οἱ μαθηταὶ οὐκ ἐκάθευδον.**
6. **χθὲς ἦμεν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ τοῦ κριτοῦ.**
7. **ἡ φωνὴ τοῦ διδασκάλου ἦν μεγάλη, ἀλλὰ οἱ ἄλλοι οὐκ ἤκουον.**
8. **ἔτι ἔμενον ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ οἱ φίλοι.**
9. **οὐκ ἔγραφες οὐδὲ ἐμάνθανες, ὦ Μᾶρκε.**
10. **ἐγὼ ἔφερον τὰ βιβλία καὶ σὺ τὰ δῶρα.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Then we were saying the words of truth.*
2. *The teacher was always teaching the sons of the men.* — **ἀεί** with an
   imperfect is the characteristic pairing: a thing that kept on happening.
3. *I kept hearing the letter again, but I was not understanding it.* Or
   *they kept hearing … they were not understanding* — **ἤκουον** and
   **ἐμάνθανον** are both ambiguous, and both must be resolved the same way.
4. *In the beginning was the Word.* They have seen this before; they can
   now name the tense.
5. *The horse was sleeping, but the pupils were not sleeping.* — two
   imperfects, one situation.
6. *Yesterday we were in the house of the judge.* — **ἦμεν** is 1st plural;
   a pupil who writes *they were* has confused it with **ἦσαν**.
7. *The voice of the teacher was loud, but the others were not listening.*
   — accept *great* for **μεγάλη**; *loud* is what it means of a voice.
   **οἱ ἄλλοι**, the adjective with the article and no noun, is *the
   others*.
8. *The friends were still waiting on the road.* — **ἔμενον** could be *I
   was waiting*, but **οἱ φίλοι** settles it. Accept *staying* or
   *remaining*.
9. *You were not writing nor learning, Marcus.* — **οὐδέ** joins two
   negatives; **ὦ Μᾶρκε** is the vocative from Lesson 4.
10. *I was carrying the books and you the gifts.* — **ἐγώ** is there to
    settle **ἔφερον**, which is the second job of the pronoun described in
    the note above. The verb is not repeated; English does the same.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I was saying the words.
2. They were always learning.
3. He was teaching the son.
4. We were not hearing the letter.
5. Then the gifts were small.
6. The pupils were sleeping.
7. Yesterday I was in the house.
8. You were always writing.
9. The others were still listening.
10. We were not carrying the letter nor the book.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἔλεγον τοὺς λόγους.**
2. **ἀεὶ ἐμάνθανον.**
3. **ἐδίδασκε τὸν υἱόν.**
4. **οὐκ ἠκούομεν τὴν ἐπιστολήν.** — the augment lengthens the **ἀ-** to
   **ἠ-**, and **οὐκ** is chosen because the verb now begins with a vowel
   carrying a smooth breathing.
5. **τότε μικρὰ ἦν τὰ δῶρα.**
6. **οἱ μαθηταὶ ἐκάθευδον.** — watch for a missing augment (**καθεῦδον**).
7. **χθὲς ἦν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.** — **ἦν** is *I was* as well as *he was*; a pupil
   who adds **ἐγώ** to make it clear has done exactly what a Greek writer
   would do.
8. **ἀεὶ ἔγραφες.**
9. **οἱ ἄλλοι ἔτι ἤκουον.**
10. **οὐκ ἐφέρομεν τὴν ἐπιστολὴν οὐδὲ τὸ βιβλίον.** — *not … nor* is **οὐκ
    … οὐδέ**. A pupil who writes **καὶ οὐ** has said something a Greek
    could say but would not.

Question 4 catches two things at once and is worth setting again in the next lesson.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Quintus's distinction"}
Quintus said that *he came in, he sat down, he spoke* is a list, while *the
rain was falling, the lamp was going out* is a situation.

1. Which of those two sets of English verbs would be imperfect in Greek?
2. Write two Greek sentences about the same scene, one giving a
   situation and one giving an event, using only the imperfect you have.
3. Why might a storyteller want both? What would a story be like with only
   one?
4. Sort these into Quintus's two piles: *was waiting, arrived, used to
   sing, shouted, kept looking, fell.*
5. **ἀεί** and **τότε** are both in this lesson's list. Which of them sits
   more naturally with a situation, and why?
6. Put **ἔλεγε** into English in three different ways.
7. Which of your three would you choose for *the teacher ——— when the door
   opened*? Why that one?
8. *In the beginning was the Word.* Situation or event, by Quintus's test?
9. Marcus said a past is a past. Give one English sentence in which changing
   *was speaking* to *spoke* changes what happened.
10. Now give one in which the change makes no difference, and say what that
    shows about Marcus's objection.
:::

::: answers
1. The second — *was falling*, *was going out*. The first set is a sequence
   of completed events and belongs to the tense arriving next lesson.
2. Answers will vary; only imperfects are available, so the "event"
   sentence will be imperfect too and will feel wrong. **That is the
   intended discovery.** A pupil who complains that they cannot yet say *he
   sat down* properly has found the next lesson, and should be told so.
3. A story with only imperfects never moves; a story with only events has no
   weather, no mood, and nobody waiting. Greek narrative uses the two
   against each other constantly, and Part III is mostly about learning to
   hear it.
4. Situations: *was waiting, used to sing, kept looking*. Events: *arrived,
   shouted, fell*. A pupil who puts *used to sing* with the events has gone
   by the English form (*sang*-like) rather than the sense; *used to* is
   the imperfect's own idiom.
5. **ἀεί**. *Always* describes something going on or recurring, which is
   what the imperfect is for; **τότε** only places the action in time and
   is happy with either tense.
6. *He was saying*, *he kept saying*, *he used to say*. Accept *he went on
   saying*.
7. *Was speaking*. The door opening is the event; the speaking is the
   situation it interrupts. The other two renderings put the emphasis on
   repetition, which the sentence does not want.
8. A situation. **ἦν** is imperfect, and the Voice below makes the point at
   length. A pupil who says *event* should be asked what the English
   *was* is doing in it, and then what the Greek is doing.
9. For example, *when I came in he was speaking* (I interrupted him) against
   *when I came in he spoke* (he started as I entered). The two describe
   different scenes.
10. For example, *yesterday he was speaking in the forum* / *yesterday he
    spoke in the forum*. Both report the same fact. What it shows is that
    Marcus is right that both tenses place the action in the past — and
    wrong that this is all they do, because sentence 9 proved otherwise.
:::
:::

::: {.voice source="John 1:1"}
You read this in Lesson 6, when **ἦν** was a word somebody had to tell you.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.**

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
God.*

Three **ἦν**. You can now say what they are: third person singular,
imperfect, of **εἰμί**.

And because the imperfect is the tense of *going on*, the line does not say
that the Word existed at a moment in the past. It says it **was, and
continued to be** — through the beginning, and not starting at it. The tense
carries a claim the English *was* cannot quite carry, because English
*was* is just as happy reporting a thing that lasted an instant.

This is the first time in this course that a tense, rather than a word, has
been the argument. It will not be the last.

::: note
Greek had another option here and did not take it. There is a tense for
*came to be* — it is the one Marcus is about to meet — and the author of
this line uses it thirteen verses later, about the same subject, to say
something entirely different. In Lesson 14 you will be able to read both and
see the difference.
:::
:::

::: {.reading title="What happened yesterday" source="composed"}
Theodoros's tablet this time was about the day before. It was the first
time the class had read a story in Greek that was not happening now, and
every verb in it has an augment.

χθὲς ὁ διδάσκαλος ἔλεγε καὶ οἱ μαθηταὶ ἤκουον. ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ ἦμεν, καὶ
ἡ ἡμέρα ἦν καλή. ὁ διδάσκαλος ἔλεγε περὶ τῶν θεῶν καὶ περὶ τῶν ἀνθρώπων,
καὶ ἡ φωνὴ τοῦ διδασκάλου ἦν μεγάλη.

ἡ Ἰουλία ἤκουε καὶ ἔγραφεν· ἀεὶ ἔγραφεν ἡ Ἰουλία. ὁ Κόιντος ἤκουεν, ἀλλὰ
οὐκ ἔγραφεν· οἱ λόγοι ἔμενον ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ. τότε ἡ Σαβῖνα οὐκ ἤκουε τοὺς
λόγους τοῦ διδασκάλου· ἔβλεπε τὸν αἴλουρον. ὁ Μᾶρκος οὐκ ἤκουεν οὐδὲ ἔγραφεν· ἔλεγεν.
ὁ διδάσκαλος ἔλεγε περὶ τῶν θεῶν, ὁ Μᾶρκος ἔλεγε περὶ τῶν ἵππων. οἱ ἄλλοι
μαθηταὶ ἐμάνθανον.

ὁ Φῆλιξ ἔτι ἦν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ. ὁ παιδαγωγὸς ἔφερε τὰ βιβλία καὶ τὴν δέλτον· ὁ
Φῆλιξ οὐκ ἔφερεν.

ὁ αἴλουρος ἐκάθευδεν. ἐκάθευδεν ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις τοῦ διδασκάλου, καὶ ἡ
φωνὴ τοῦ διδασκάλου ἦν μεγάλη, καὶ ὁ αἴλουρος ἔτι ἐκάθευδεν. ἀεὶ καθεύδει
ὁ αἴλουρος.

ἐγὼ ἔβλεπον τοὺς μαθητὰς καὶ τὸν αἴλουρον, καὶ ἔγραφον. νῦν πάλιν γράφω.

::: gloss
- **τὸ διδασκαλεῖον** — the school room *(dative after **ἐν**)*
- **ὁ Κόιντος, ἡ Σαβῖνα** — Quintus, Sabina
- **ὁ αἴλουρος** — the cat
- **ἡ δέλτος** — the writing tablet *(feminine, like **ἡ ὁδός**)*
- **ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις** — among the books *(**ἐν** is *among* as well as *in*)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read the whole tablet aloud before you answer anything, and listen to the
augments.

1. Who was speaking yesterday, and about what? Which word puts the whole
   story in the past before any verb does?
2. In the second paragraph, find three pupils doing three different things
   while the teacher spoke.
3. **οἱ λόγοι ἔμενον ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ.** Translate. Whose soul must it be?
4. What was Sabina doing instead of listening?
5. Marcus was also speaking. What was the difference between his subject
   and the teacher's?
6. Where was Felix, and who was carrying his things? What was he carrying
   himself?
7. Where exactly was the cat asleep? Give the Greek phrase and name its
   case.
8. Count the imperfect verbs in the second paragraph — there are more than
   ten. How many of them are ambiguous between *I* and *they*, and what
   resolves each one?
9. **ἐγὼ ἔβλεπον.** Who is *I*? How did you decide? What would **ἔβλεπον**
   on its own have been able to mean?
10. The last sentence changes tense. Translate it, and say why the tense
    had to change.
:::

::: answers
1. The teacher, about the gods and about human beings. **χθές**, the first
   word, puts it in the past; the augments then confirm it on every verb.
2. Julia was listening and writing; Quintus was listening and not writing;
   Sabina was watching the cat; Marcus was talking. Any three.
3. *The words were staying in his soul* — Quintus's. The Greek has no *his*;
   the previous clause supplies it. A pupil who asks *whose?* has read
   correctly: Greek leaves it to the context, and here the context is
   clear.
4. Watching the cat: **ἔβλεπε τὸν αἴλουρον**.
5. The teacher was speaking **περὶ τῶν θεῶν**, Marcus **περὶ τῶν ἵππων** —
   about horses. Both verbs are **ἔλεγε**; the two were going on at the
   same time, which is what the imperfect is telling you.
6. Still on the road, **ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ**; the paedagogus was carrying the books
   and the tablet. Felix was carrying nothing — **οὐκ ἔφερεν** has no
   object at all, and the joke is in the gap.
7. **ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις τοῦ διδασκάλου** — dative plural after **ἐν**, *among
   the teacher's books*. Expect *in the books* and accept it, then ask what
   a cat in a book would look like.
8. Fourteen: ἤκουε, ἔγραφεν, ἔγραφεν, ἤκουεν, ἔγραφεν, ἔμενον, ἤκουε,
   ἔβλεπε, ἤκουεν, ἔγραφεν, ἔλεγεν, ἔλεγε, ἔλεγε, ἐμάνθανον. Two are
   **-ον** forms: **ἔμενον** (resolved by **οἱ λόγοι**, so *they*) and
   **ἐμάνθανον** (resolved by **οἱ ἄλλοι μαθηταί**). A pupil who counts
   fewer has usually skipped the bare **ἔλεγεν.** that stands alone after a
   raised point, because there is no noun beside it to catch the eye.
9. Theodoros — the person writing the tablet, who has called himself *the
   teacher* throughout and only now says *I*. **ἐγώ** is doing the job the
   note in this lesson described: **ἔβλεπον** alone could have been *I was
   watching* or *they were watching*, and the pronoun removes the doubt.
   A pupil who says *I is the teacher, because he is the one who was
   watching everybody* has read the whole tablet to answer one word, which
   is the right way round.
10. *Now I am writing again.* **νῦν** is *now*, so the tense is present: the
    story was yesterday, the writing of it is today. The imperfect
    **ἔγραφον** was what he was doing then; **γράφω** is what he is doing
    as the class reads.
:::

1. Quintus described two Greek tenses without knowing either. What does that

2. *Was, and went on being* takes five English words and one Greek ending.

3. **ἀεί** means *always*. Is there any difference between *he was always
