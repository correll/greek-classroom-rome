# Verbs That Melt

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodosius had been reading ahead, which was not forbidden, and had brought
the result to the lesson, which was.

"The last chapter of John," he said. "By the sea. He asks Peter three times
whether he loves him. I know it in Latin. *Diligis me? Diligis me? Amas me?*
And Peter says *amo te* all three times. I always thought Jerome was being
elegant." He put the scroll on the bench. "He was not. He was copying. The
Greek asks it twice with one verb and the third time with another.
**ἀγαπᾷς με; ἀγαπᾷς με; φιλεῖς με;** And Peter answers **φιλῶ σε**, three
times, with the second verb, every time."

"So?" said Marcus.

"So does it *matter*? Are they two words for one thing, or is Peter refusing
the word he is offered and giving back a smaller one?"

"Two words," said Felix. "People say things twice in different words. My
father does it in every letter."

"Then why change on the third?" said Marcus, who had already decided it
mattered because that was the more interesting position.

Livia had the scroll. "The sheep change too," she said. "**ἀρνία** the first
time, **πρόβατα** the second and third. And *feed* changes — **βόσκε**,
**ποίμαινε**, **βόσκε**. And *you know* changes at the end, **οἶδας** to
**γινώσκεις**. If the verb for love means something by changing, so do the
sheep."

There was a silence while the class considered the sheep.

"Before anyone argues about what the two verbs mean," said Theodoros, "the
class will learn why they look as they do. **ἀγαπᾷς** has a circumflex and
an iota hiding under it, and you have been reading it since Lesson 11
without being told why. **φιλεῖς** has an **ει** that is not in **φιλέω**.
Those are not decorations. They are the remains of a collision. Learn the
collision; then we argue; and I tell you now that I shall not settle it."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## Verbs that melt

Some verbs have a stem that ends in a vowel — **α**, **ε** or **ο**. In
most of the verb that is no trouble: the future, the aorist and the perfect
put a consonant between stem and ending (**ἀγαπή-σ-ω**, **ἐ-ποίη-σ-α**,
**δε-δήλω-κ-α**), and the stem vowel simply lengthens before it, **α** and
**ε** to **η**, **ο** to **ω**.

But in the **present** and the **imperfect** there is no consonant. The stem
vowel runs straight into the vowel of the ending, and the two fuse into one
long vowel or diphthong. These are the **contract verbs** — verbs that melt
— and you have been reading them, unexplained, since Lesson 11: **ἀγαπᾶτε**,
**ποιεῖ**, **εὐλογεῖ**, **λαλεῖ**, **ἐρωτᾷ**. Only two tenses melt. The
rest of the verb is as regular as **λύω**.

## The rules of melting

| stem vowel | + ε | + ει | + ο | + ου | + ω | + η | + ῃ | + οι |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **α** | **ᾱ** | **ᾳ** | **ω** | **ω** | **ω** | **ᾱ** | **ᾳ** | **ῳ** |
| **ε** | **ει** | **ει** | **ου** | **ου** | **ω** | **η** | **ῃ** | **οι** |
| **ο** | **ου** | **οι** | **ου** | **ου** | **ω** | **ω** | **οι** | **οι** |

Three sentences hold the whole table.

- **α swallows.** Whatever meets it, an **ε**-sound comes out **ᾱ** and an
  **ο**-sound comes out **ω**; if there was an iota anywhere in the ending,
  it survives underneath (**ᾳ**, **ῳ**).
- **ε vanishes** before any long vowel or diphthong, leaving it unchanged.
  Before short **ε** it gives **ει**; before short **ο**, **ου**.
- **ο** gives **ου** with a short vowel, **ω** with a long one, and **οι**
  whenever an iota is anywhere in sight.

The **accent** follows one rule. In the uncontracted form, the accent falls
where it always does. If it fell on the *first* of the two vowels that
melted, the new long vowel takes a **circumflex**: **ποιέ-ω → ποιῶ**. If it
fell on the *second*, an acute: **ἀγαπα-όμεθα → ἀγαπώμεθα**. If it fell
earlier in the word than either, nothing happens: **ἐποίε-ε → ἐποίει**.
Lesson 11 called the circumflex a scar. It is: the mark of an accent that
was standing on the first vowel when the two became one.

## The present active

| | **-άω** ἀγαπάω, *I love* | **-έω** ποιέω, *I make* | **-όω** δηλόω, *I show* |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἀγαπῶ** | **ποιῶ** | **δηλῶ** |
| 2 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷς** | **ποιεῖς** | **δηλοῖς** |
| 3 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷ** | **ποιεῖ** | **δηλοῖ** |
| 1 plur. | **ἀγαπῶμεν** | **ποιοῦμεν** | **δηλοῦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἀγαπᾶτε** | **ποιεῖτε** | **δηλοῦτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἀγαπῶσι(ν)** | **ποιοῦσι(ν)** | **δηλοῦσι(ν)** |
| infinitive | **ἀγαπᾶν** | **ποιεῖν** | **δηλοῦν** |
| imperative 2 sing. | **ἀγάπα** | **ποίει** | **δήλου** |
| imperative 2 plur. | **ἀγαπᾶτε** | **ποιεῖτε** | **δηλοῦτε** |

Under the melt, the endings are still there and still visible: **-ς** on
the second person, **-μεν**, **-τε**, **-σι**. Ask of **ποιοῦμεν** *what in
the word tells you that?* and the answer is **-μεν**, as it was for
**λύομεν**. The stem has changed its surface; the evidence has not moved.

## The imperfect active

| | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἠγάπων** | **ἐποίουν** | **ἐδήλουν** |
| 2 sing. | **ἠγάπας** | **ἐποίεις** | **ἐδήλους** |
| 3 sing. | **ἠγάπα** | **ἐποίει** | **ἐδήλου** |
| 1 plur. | **ἠγαπῶμεν** | **ἐποιοῦμεν** | **ἐδηλοῦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἠγαπᾶτε** | **ἐποιεῖτε** | **ἐδηλοῦτε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἠγάπων** | **ἐποίουν** | **ἐδήλουν** |

The augment is the one you know (Lesson 13): **ἐ-** before a consonant,
**α** lengthened to **η** in **ἠγάπων**. First singular and third plural
are identical, as they were in **ἔλεγον**.

## The present and imperfect middle

The middle and passive endings of Lesson 31 melt in exactly the same way.

| present | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἀγαπῶμαι** | **ποιοῦμαι** | **δηλοῦμαι** |
| 2 sing. | **ἀγαπᾷ** | **ποιῇ** | **δηλοῖ** |
| 3 sing. | **ἀγαπᾶται** | **ποιεῖται** | **δηλοῦται** |
| 1 plur. | **ἀγαπώμεθα** | **ποιούμεθα** | **δηλούμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **ἀγαπᾶσθε** | **ποιεῖσθε** | **δηλοῦσθε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἀγαπῶνται** | **ποιοῦνται** | **δηλοῦνται** |

| imperfect | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἠγαπώμην** | **ἐποιούμην** | **ἐδηλούμην** |
| 2 sing. | **ἠγαπῶ** | **ἐποιοῦ** | **ἐδηλοῦ** |
| 3 sing. | **ἠγαπᾶτο** | **ἐποιεῖτο** | **ἐδηλοῦτο** |
| 1 plur. | **ἠγαπώμεθα** | **ἐποιούμεθα** | **ἐδηλούμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **ἠγαπᾶσθε** | **ἐποιεῖσθε** | **ἐδηλοῦσθε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἠγαπῶντο** | **ἐποιοῦντο** | **ἐδηλοῦντο** |

Notice **ἀγαπᾷ** in the second singular of the middle. It is identical to
the third singular of the active. **ἀγαπᾷ** is *he loves* and also *you are
loved*, and only the sentence can tell you which. Melting costs something.
It is the price of a language that will not keep two vowels apart when it
can say them as one.

::: note
**Four verbs you already own are contract verbs**, and you can now explain
every form of them you have met. **ποιέω** (Lesson 22): **ποίει** in the
Samaritan is its imperative. **εὐλογέω** (20) and **ἐλεέω** (22): **-έω**.
**λαλέω** (23): **-έω**. **ἐρωτάω** (25): **-άω**, so *he asks* is
**ἐρωτᾷ**. And **ὁράω**, *I see*, whose aorist **εἶδον** you met in Lesson 22
and whose imperative **ἰδού** you met in Lesson 27: its present is **ὁρῶ,
ὁρᾷς, ὁρᾷ**, and its imperfect takes a double augment, **ἑώρων**, which you
should simply recognise.

**καλέω** keeps its **ε** short outside the present: future **καλῶ**
(which is a contracted future, Lesson 32), aorist **ἐκάλεσα**. It is the
one common exception to the lengthening rule and it is in the Gospels on
every page.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin has no contraction to speak of — and that is because it finished
melting before anyone wrote it down.** *Amō* is **amā-ō** with the vowels
already fused; *amās*, *amat*, *amāmus* are what an **-άω** verb looks
like when the collision happened a thousand years ago and the dictionary
has forgotten the uncontracted form. The Latin first conjugation *is* the
Greek **-άω** class, cooled and set. Greek keeps both layers in view:
**ἀγαπάω** in the lexicon, **ἀγαπῶ** on the page. That is why the Greek
table has a fold in it and the Latin table is flat.

So Latin will not help you with the forms. It helps with the argument.
Jerome, translating John 21, had *amō* and *dīligō* and could have used
either three times. He did not. **ἀγαπᾷς** became *dīligis* and
**φιλεῖς** became *amās*, and he kept the change exactly where John put
it. Whether that is because the Greek meant something by it, or because
Jerome, like John, disliked saying the same thing twice in the same
words, is the question the class is about to spend ten minutes on.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἀγαπάω** — I love *(ἀγαπῶ; aorist **ἠγάπησα**)*
- **φιλέω** — I love, I am fond of; I kiss *(φιλῶ; aorist **ἐφίλησα**)*
- **δηλόω** — I show, I make clear *(δηλῶ; aorist **ἐδήλωσα**)*
- **ζητέω** — I seek, look for
- **καλέω** — I call, I name *(aorist **ἐκάλεσα**; passive **καλοῦμαι**, I am called)*
- **ὁράω** — I see *(ὁρῶ; imperfect **ἑώρων**; aorist **εἶδον**)*
- **τιμάω** — I honour
- **αἰτέω** — I ask for, I request *(a thing; **ἐρωτάω** asks a question)*
- **τηρέω** — I keep, I guard, I observe
- **λυπέω** — I grieve, I cause pain *(passive **λυποῦμαι**, I am grieved)*
- **τὸ πρόβατον** — sheep
- **πᾶς, πᾶσα, πᾶν** — all, every; *(neuter plural **πάντα**)* everything
- **ναί** — yes
- **δεύτερος** — second *(**τὸ δεύτερον**, a second time)*
- **τρίτος** — third *(**τὸ τρίτον**, the third time)*

::: note
**ἀγαπάω and φιλέω.** In classical prose **φιλέω** is the ordinary word
for loving — friends, family, one's city — and for kissing. **ἀγαπάω** is
rarer and cooler: *to be content with*, *to prize*. The Septuagint chose
**ἀγαπάω** for the love of God and of neighbour, and the Gospels inherited
the choice, and by John's day the two verbs overlapped a great deal. That
overlap is the whole difficulty of the Ancient Voice.

**πᾶς** is third declension (**παντός**, **παντί**, **πάντα**); the
feminine **πᾶσα** is first. With the article inside the group, **πᾶσα ἡ
πόλις**, it means *the whole city*; outside, **πᾶσα πόλις**, *every
city*. **πάντα**, neuter plural, is *everything*: **πάντα σὺ οἶδας**.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory, not from the book.

1. **τὸν ἵππον** — which case, and what two things tell you?
2. **ἔγραφε** and **ἔγραψε**: which is the aorist, and what is the evidence?
3. Translate **δεῖ τοὺς παῖδας μανθάνειν.**
4. **πιστεύω** takes which case in Attic?
5. **ἐλύθη** — tense and voice?
6. **γράψω** — tense? What one letter tells you?
7. **πεπίστευκα** — tense? What does the **πε-** at the front tell you?
8. Why is it **μὴ ψεύδου** and not **οὐ ψεύδου**?
9. **τίς** and **τις** — which asks a question, and what is the difference on
   the page?
10. **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός** against **καλὸς ὁ ποιμήν** — translate both.
:::

::: answers
1. Accusative — **τόν** and **-ον** (Lesson 3).
2. **ἔγραψε** — the **ψ** is **φ + σ**, the first-aorist marker (Lesson 14).
3. *The children must learn.* — **δεῖ** with accusative and infinitive
   (Lesson 26).
4. The dative (Lesson 19); Koine adds **εἰς** + accusative.
5. Aorist passive — **-θη-** (Lesson 31). *He, she or it was loosed.*
6. Future — the **σ** between stem and ending, with no augment in front
   (Lesson 32).
7. Perfect — reduplication, the first consonant repeated with **ε** (Lesson
   33). *I have come to believe, and still do.*
8. It is a command, and commands take the **μή** family (Lesson 26).
9. **τίς** with the acute asks; **τις** without an accent is *someone*, an
   enclitic (Lesson 25). The accent is the question.
10. *The good shepherd* (attributive, inside the article group) against
    *the shepherd is good* (predicate, outside it) — Lesson 10.
:::

For each form give the verb, the person and number, the tense and voice,
and the *uncontracted* form the rules would produce. Say which letter in
the ending told you.

1. **ποιεῖτε**
2. **ἀγαπᾷ**
3. **δηλοῦσιν**
4. **ἐφίλει**
5. **ἠρώτων**
6. **ζητοῦμεν**
7. **ὁρᾷς**
8. **ἐτίμα**
9. **τηροῦνται**
10. **ἐκαλεῖτο**
:::

::: answers
1. **ποιέω**, 2 plur. present active — or imperative, which is identical —
   **ποιέ-ετε**; the **-τε**.
2. **ἀγαπάω**, 3 sing. present active, **ἀγαπά-ει** — but also 2 sing.
   present middle, **ἀγαπά-ῃ**, *you are loved*. Both are correct; a
   pupil who gives only one has missed half the lesson.
3. **δηλόω**, 3 plur. present active, **δηλό-ουσι(ν)**; the **-σι**.
4. **φιλέω**, 3 sing. imperfect active, **ἐφίλε-ε**; the augment, and the
   bare **-ε**.
5. **ἐρωτάω**, 1 sing. or 3 plur. imperfect active, **ἠρώτα-ον**; the
   augment (**ε** became **η**) and **-ν**. Ambiguous, like **ἔλεγον**.
6. **ζητέω**, 1 plur. present active, **ζητέ-ομεν**; the **-μεν**.
7. **ὁράω**, 2 sing. present active, **ὁρά-εις**; the **-ς**, with the iota
   of **-εις** surviving under the **ᾳ**.
8. **τιμάω**, 3 sing. imperfect active, **ἐτίμα-ε**. The accent has not
   moved because it was never on either melting vowel.
9. **τηρέω**, 3 plur. present middle or passive, **τηρέ-ονται**; *they are
   kept*, or *they keep for themselves*.
10. **καλέω**, 3 sing. imperfect middle or passive, **ἐκαλέ-ετο**; almost
    always *was called* — the ordinary way Greek gives a name.
:::

2. **τί ζητεῖτε;** *(John 1:38 — the first words Jesus speaks in that

3. **ὁ αἴλουρος ὁρᾷ τὸ πρόβατον· τὸ δὲ πρόβατον οὐχ ὁρᾷ τὸν αἴλουρον.**
4. **ἐποίουν τὸ ἔργον ἀεί, ἀλλ᾽ οὐκ ἐποίησαν.**
5. **ὁ παῖς Φῆλιξ καλεῖται.**
6. **ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον ἀγαπᾷ· τοῦτο δηλοῖ ἡ γραφή.**
7. **τίς ἐστιν ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς τηρεῖ τοὺς λόγους τοῦ κυρίου;**
8. **τὸν μὲν Μᾶρκον ἠρώτων, ὁ δὲ οὐκ ἀπεκρίνετο.**
9. **αἰτοῦμεν τὸν διδάσκαλον δῶρον.**
10. **ἐλυπεῖτο ὁ Πέτρος ὅτι τὸ τρίτον ἠρώτα αὐτὸν ὁ κύριος.**
:::

::: answers
1. *The teacher loves the pupils, but does not honour them all.* —
   **πάντας**, accusative plural of **πᾶς**.
2. *What are you looking for?* — present: an activity, not a single
   request. **ζητέ-ετε**.
3. *The cat sees the sheep; but the sheep does not see the cat.* — **ὁρᾷ**
   twice, and **οὐχ** before the rough breathing (Lesson 6).
4. *They were always doing the work, but they did not do it.* — the joke of
   Lesson 14 again, now with a contract verb: imperfect **ἐποίουν**, aorist
   **ἐποίησαν** with the **η** of the lengthened stem and no melting at all.
5. *The boy is called Felix.* — **καλεῖται**, passive; **Φῆλιξ** without an
   article is the name he is called, a predicate (Lesson 10).
6. *God loves the world; the scripture makes this clear.* — **δηλοῖ**:
   **δηλό-ει**, 3 sing.; **τοῦτο** is its object.
7. *Who is the man who keeps the words of the Lord?* — **τίς** with the
   accent (Lesson 25); **ὅς** the relative (Lesson 17); **τηρεῖ**.
8. *They kept asking Marcus, but he would not answer.* — **ἠρώτων**,
   imperfect, repeated asking; **ἀπεκρίνετο**, imperfect middle of
   **ἀποκρίνομαι** (Lesson 31); **ὁ δέ** *and he* (Lesson 17).
9. *We ask the teacher for a gift.* — **αἰτέω** takes two accusatives, the
   person asked and the thing asked for. English needs *for*; Greek does not.
10. *Peter was grieved because the Lord was asking him a third time.* —
    **ἐλυπεῖτο**, imperfect passive of **λυπέω**; **ἠρώτα**, imperfect of
    **ἐρωτάω**. Adapted from John 21:17, which has the aorist **ἐλυπήθη**;
    the Reading will ask what the change of aspect does.
:::

   unsuccessful.
4. **ποιοῦμεν τὸ ἔργον.** — **ποιέ-ομεν → ποιοῦμεν**. Watch for

5. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἐτίμα τὸν κριτήν.** — **ἐτίμα** with the acute on the
   **ι**; a circumflex here is the commonest accent error in the exercise.
6. **τὸ πρόβατον ὁρᾷ τὸν ποιμένα.** — **ποιμένα**, third declension

7. **ἡ γραφὴ δηλοῖ τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — **δηλοῖ**, not **δηλεῖ**: an **-όω**

8. **τηροῦσι τοὺς λόγους τοῦ πατρός.** — **τηροῦσι(ν)**; **πατρός** from

9. **ναί, κύριε, σὺ οἶδας ὅτι φιλῶ σε.** — Peter's answer, John 21:15.
   **σύ** written out because it is emphatic (Lesson 11): *you* know, whatever
   I say.
10. **ὁ αἴλουρος κύριος καλεῖται.** — **κύριος** predicate, no article; the

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 21:15"}
The first of the three questions, entire. It is Koine; read it in Attic
and say what the Koine did instead.

**Ὅτε οὖν ἠρίστησαν λέγει τῷ Σίμωνι Πέτρῳ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· Σίμων Ἰωάννου, ἀγαπᾷς
με πλέον τούτων; λέγει αὐτῷ· ναὶ κύριε, σὺ οἶδας ὅτι φιλῶ σε. λέγει αὐτῷ·
βόσκε τὰ ἀρνία μου.**

- **ὅτε οὖν ἠρίστησαν** — so when they had breakfasted *(**ἀριστάω**, a
  contract verb in its aorist: the **η** of the lengthened stem and no
  melting)*
- **λέγει** — says *(present, in a past story: the Gospels do this
  constantly)*
- **τῷ Σίμωνι Πέτρῳ** — to Simon Peter *(dative; both names decline)*
- **Σίμων Ἰωάννου** — Simon, son of John *(the genitive alone says
  *son of*)*
- **ἀγαπᾷς με** — do you love me *(**ἀγαπά-εις**; **με** enclitic)*
- **πλέον τούτων** — more than these *(**τούτων**, genitive of comparison:
  more than these [men love me]? or more than [you love] these [boats]?
  The Greek allows both and the argument has been going on since
  antiquity)*
- **ναὶ κύριε** — yes, Lord
- **σὺ οἶδας** — *you* know *(Koine second singular; Attic **οἶσθα**)*
- **φιλῶ σε** — I love you *(**φιλέ-ω**; **σε** enclitic)*
- **βόσκε** — feed! *(present imperative: keep feeding)*
- **τὰ ἀρνία μου** — my lambs *(a diminutive, Lesson 23; **μου** enclitic,
  so **ἀρνία** takes a second accent)*

*So when they had breakfasted, Jesus says to Simon Peter: Simon, son of
John, do you love me more than these? He says to him: Yes, Lord, you know
that I love you. He says to him: Feed my lambs.*

**Three contract forms in one verse** — **ἠρίστησαν**, **ἀγαπᾷς**,
**φιλῶ** — and only two of them melted. The aorist put a **σ** between stem
and ending and the vowel lengthened instead. That is the whole of this
lesson's rule, and the verse happens to illustrate it.

**And the question.** Jesus asks with **ἀγαπάω**. Peter answers with
**φιλέω**. Peter also answers **ναί**, *yes* — which is not the answer to a
question in which the verb had been refused. He says *yes* and changes the
verb, and whether the second cancels the first is the argument. Notice at
least this: **σύ** is written. Peter does not say *I love you*. He says
*you know that I love you* — and throws the weight on the *you*. He is not
describing his feeling. He is appealing to the other man's knowledge of it.
Lesson 11 told you a written pronoun is a raised voice. Here it is raised.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text follows the standard Greek New
Testament (see *Sources*). Check in particular **Ἰωάννου**, where older
editions and the Vulgate's *Simon Ioannis* rest on a different reading
from the **Ἰωνᾶ** of the Received Text, and **ἀρνία** against **πρόβατα**
and **προβάτια** in the verses that follow; editions differ.

**On the argument.** Do not settle it. The older commentators mostly held
that the change of verb is deliberate and that Peter, having denied three
times, will not now claim the greater word; many modern readers, noting that
John varies **βόσκε/ποίμαινε**, **ἀρνία/πρόβατα** and **οἶδας/γινώσκεις** in
the same three verses and uses **φιλέω** elsewhere of the Father's love for
the Son (5:20), hold that the variation is John's style and carries no
weight. Both positions are respectable; both are defended from the page.
Livia's point about the sheep is the second position in a sentence, and it
should be allowed its full force before Marcus answers it — which he can,
by observing that Peter was *grieved* at the third question and not at the
first two, and asking what changed. The class should leave with the
evidence on both sides and no verdict, and should be told that this is
what reading a real text is like.

**On Theodosius.** He has brought the Latin to the Greek and found the
Greek *behind* it, which is Lesson 19's discovery made a second time with
a harder passage. He should be allowed to notice that Jerome kept the
change; it is his best evidence that it mattered to at least one careful
reader, and it is also, as Theodoros will point out, no evidence at all
about what it meant.
:::
:::

::: {.reading time="20 min" title="Three questions on the shore" source="John 21:15–17"}
The whole exchange, in two tiers. Read the adapted Attic first and unmelt
every contract form; then read the original and find what the Koine did
differently.

**First tier — adapted.** *Attic; composed in the words of the course.*

τότε λέγει τῷ Πέτρῳ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· "ὦ Σίμων, ἀγαπᾷς με πλέον τούτων;" λέγει
αὐτῷ ὁ Πέτρος· "ναί, κύριε· σὺ οἶσθα ὅτι φιλῶ σε." λέγει αὐτῷ· "ποίμαινε
τὰ πρόβατά μου."

λέγει αὐτῷ πάλιν τὸ δεύτερον· "ὦ Σίμων, ἀγαπᾷς με;" λέγει αὐτῷ· "ναί,
κύριε· σὺ οἶσθα ὅτι φιλῶ σε." λέγει αὐτῷ· "ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατά μου."

λέγει αὐτῷ τὸ τρίτον· "ὦ Σίμων, φιλεῖς με;" ὁ δὲ Πέτρος ἐλυπεῖτο ὅτι τὸ
τρίτον ἠρώτα αὐτόν· "φιλεῖς με;" καὶ λέγει αὐτῷ· "κύριε, πάντα σὺ οἶσθα·
σὺ γιγνώσκεις ὅτι φιλῶ σε." λέγει αὐτῷ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· "ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατά
μου."

**Second tier — the original.** *John 21:15–17, Koine, from the standard
text.*

Ὅτε οὖν ἠρίστησαν λέγει τῷ Σίμωνι Πέτρῳ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· Σίμων Ἰωάννου, ἀγαπᾷς
με πλέον τούτων; λέγει αὐτῷ· ναὶ κύριε, σὺ οἶδας ὅτι φιλῶ σε. λέγει αὐτῷ·
βόσκε τὰ ἀρνία μου. λέγει αὐτῷ πάλιν δεύτερον· Σίμων Ἰωάννου, ἀγαπᾷς με;
λέγει αὐτῷ· ναὶ κύριε, σὺ οἶδας ὅτι φιλῶ σε. λέγει αὐτῷ· ποίμαινε τὰ
πρόβατά μου. λέγει αὐτῷ τὸ τρίτον· Σίμων Ἰωάννου, φιλεῖς με; ἐλυπήθη ὁ
Πέτρος ὅτι εἶπεν αὐτῷ τὸ τρίτον· φιλεῖς με; καὶ λέγει αὐτῷ· κύριε, πάντα σὺ
οἶδας, σὺ γινώσκεις ὅτι φιλῶ σε. λέγει αὐτῷ [ὁ Ἰησοῦς]· βόσκε τὰ πρόβατά
μου.

::: gloss
- **ὁ Ἰησοῦς** — Jesus *(dative **Ἰησοῦ**)*
- **ὁ Πέτρος, ὁ Σίμων** — Peter; Simon *(dative **Σίμωνι**)*
- **με, σε** — me, you *(accusative; the enclitic forms of **ἐγώ** and
  **σύ**)*
- **μου** — my, of me *(enclitic genitive)*
- **πλέον τούτων** — more than these
- **οἶσθα** — you know *(Attic second singular of **οἶδα**; the Koine has
  **οἶδας**)*
- **ἐλυπεῖτο** — was grieved *(imperfect passive of **λυπέω**)*
- **ὅτε οὖν ἠρίστησαν** — so when they had breakfasted *(aorist of
  **ἀριστάω**)*
- **Ἰωάννου** — of John; *son of John*
- **βόσκε** — feed! *(present imperative)*
- **τὰ ἀρνία** — the lambs
- **δεύτερον** — a second time
- **ἐλυπήθη** — was grieved *(aorist passive of **λυπέω**)*
- **γινώσκεις** — you know *(Koine spelling of **γιγνώσκεις**)*
- **[ὁ Ἰησοῦς]** — the brackets are the editors': some manuscripts have
  the words and some do not
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. Which verb does the first question use, and which the third? Quote the
   forms.
2. Which verb does Peter answer with, each of the three times? What does
   that tell you — and what does it *not* tell you?
3. **ἀγαπᾷς** and **φιλεῖς**: unmelt both, and say which letter in each
   ending gives you the person.
4. In the adapted tier Peter **ἐλυπεῖτο**; in the original he **ἐλυπήθη**.
   Name both forms. What does the change of aspect do to the sentence?
5. Find every imperative in the original. Are they present or aorist, and
   what does that choice ask of Peter?
6. The adaptation has **ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατά μου** three times. The original
   does not. List what the original varies, verse by verse.
7. **σὺ οἶδας** — why is **σύ** written? What does Lesson 11 say about a
   pronoun that need not be there?
8. **πάντα σὺ οἶδας** — parse **πάντα**. Why is it first in its clause?
9. Attic **οἶσθα** against Koine **οἶδας**: which is the regular-looking
   form, and what does that tell you about how Koine changed (Lesson 23)?
10. In one sentence, give the best evidence that the change of verb matters,
    and in one more the best evidence that it does not. Do not decide.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἀγαπᾷς** (verses 15 and 16); **φιλεῖς** (verse 17).
2. **φιλῶ**, all three times. It tells you Peter does not take up the word
   he is offered; it does not tell you whether that is a refusal, a
   modesty, or simply his word for the thing. The third question adopts his
   verb, which can be read as concession or as pressure.
3. **ἀγαπά-εις**, **φιλέ-εις**; the **-ς** is the second person in both,
   and the **ει** of the ending survives whole in **φιλεῖς** and as the
   iota under **ᾷ** in **ἀγαπᾷς**.
4. **ἐλυπεῖτο** — 3 sing. imperfect passive, **ἐλυπέ-ετο**: *was grieving*,
   a state going on. **ἐλυπήθη** — 3 sing. aorist passive (Lesson 31):
   *was grieved*, a thing that happened to him at that moment, with the
   lengthened **η** and no melt. The original reports the moment the third
   question landed; the adaptation reports his mood. The original is
   sharper.
5. **βόσκε**, **ποίμαινε**, **βόσκε** — all present imperatives: *keep
   feeding*, a way of life and not an errand (Lesson 26), which is what one
   would expect of a shepherd's instructions.
6. Verse 15: **ἀγαπᾷς / φιλῶ / βόσκε τὰ ἀρνία**. Verse 16: **ἀγαπᾷς / φιλῶ /
   ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατα**. Verse 17: **φιλεῖς / φιλῶ / βόσκε τὰ πρόβατα**;
   and **οἶδας** becomes **οἶδας … γινώσκεις**. Four things vary, not one.
7. Because the ending **-ας** already says *you*; the pronoun is spent on
   emphasis. Peter is not stating his love but appealing to the other's
   knowledge of it: *you know*.
8. Neuter plural accusative of **πᾶς**, object of **οἶδας**: *everything*.
   First for weight, as Marcus explained of **θεός** in Lesson 30 — *you
   know everything; so you know this.*
9. **οἶδας** looks regular: it has the ordinary **-ς** of the second person.
   **οἶσθα** is an old irregular ending. Koine, spoken by people learning
   it as adults, replaced the irregular form with the regular one (Lesson
   23). The same thing produced **γινώσκω** for **γιγνώσκω**.
10. For: Peter is grieved at the third question and not at the first two,
    and the third is the one that changes verb — something changed, and the
    verb is the only candidate the text offers. Against: the lambs, the
    feeding and the knowing all vary too, and nobody argues that
    **βόσκε** and **ποίμαινε** ask for different things. A pupil who gives
    both and stops has done what was asked. A pupil who decides has done
    what Theodoros said he would not.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Peter says *yes* and changes the verb. If someone asks whether you are
   their friend and you answer *yes, I like you*, have you agreed?
2. **ἀγαπᾷ** is *he loves* and also *you are loved*. A language that lets
   two sounds melt into one loses a distinction every time. What does it
   gain, and is the trade worth it?
3. Jerome kept the two verbs. A translator who smooths them into one has
   lost something; a translator who keeps them may be inventing a
   difference. Which error would you rather make, and which would you
   rather read?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Theodosius asked, at the end, whether — if the two verbs were the same —
John had wasted a word.

"John never wastes a word," said Theodoros, "and John never says a thing
the same way twice when he can vary it, and both of those are true, and I
told you I would not settle it."

Theodosius wrote both verbs at the top of his tablet, uncontracted and
contracted, four forms in a row. Under them he wrote **σὺ οἶδας**, and
underlined the **σύ**, and did not translate it, because by now he did not
need to.
:::
