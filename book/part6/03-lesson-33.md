# What Stands Done

::: story
The written papers came back on a Tuesday, face down, one to a desk,
which was how Theodoros returned anything he had marked and the reason the
room was quiet for the first time that week.

It had been the first examination of the second term: a page of Greek of
their own, composed, not copied — *write what you can read* — and they had
been waiting nine days.

Julia turned hers over, read the single line at the foot of it, and said,
not loudly, "**τετέλεσται.**"

Theodosius looked up sharply.

"That is the word from the cross."

"It is a word," said Julia. "It means *it is finished*. The paper is
finished. I have been waiting for it since —"

"It is *the* word. John 19. You cannot use it for an examination."

"Both of you stop," said Theodoros, "and both of you are right, and I will
take the grammar first and the text second, because the text deserves to
be taken on its own and will be, in the second period. Julia. You said
**τετέλεσται**. Is that a past or a present?"

"A past. It is finished. It *was* finished — you marked it last week."

"Then why did you not say **ἐτελέσθη**? *It was finished.* Aorist passive,
Lesson 31; you can form it."

Julia opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked at the word.

"Because that is not what I meant," she said slowly. "**ἐτελέσθη** says
something happened to it. I did not mean that something happened. I meant
— it is *done*. Now. It is in the state of being finished."

"So it is a present."

"It is about now." She was frowning. "But the finishing is in the past.
Both. It is a present with the past inside it."

"That," said Theodoros, "is the perfect tense, and it has taken
grammarians a good deal longer than that to say it as well. Livia, write
it on the board as she said it."

Livia wrote: *a present with the past inside it*.

"Marcus. What is at the foot of yours?"

Marcus had been looking at it for some time. "One word. **μεμάθηκας.**"

"Which is?"

"*You have learned.*" He paused. "Not **ἔμαθες**. *You learned* — that
would be about last term. You wrote — you have learned. I am in the state
of someone who has."

"I chose the tense," said Theodoros. "I always do."

Felix, at the back, had his hand half up. "Sir. **οἶδα**. *I know.* Is that
one? It looks like one. It has no present ending and no past ending and we
were told in Lesson 25 to take it whole."

"It is one. It is the perfect of a verb that means *see*: *I have seen,
and therefore I know*. You have been using the perfect tense since Lesson
25 without being told, and you have been using it correctly, because the
meaning was never in doubt — only the name. Today you get the name, and
the rest of the paradigm, and Theodosius gets his text back at the end,
intact, which he is right to want."
:::

::: newgreek
## The perfect: a state that stands

The **perfect** describes a present state that results from a past action.
**τετέλεσται** — *it is finished*: the finishing happened, and the thing
now stands finished, and the tense is about the standing.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἐτελέσθη** | it was finished | aorist: an event, past, complete |
| **τετέλεσται** | it is finished; it stands finished | perfect: the state, now |

Both have a finishing in them. The aorist reports it. The perfect reports
where things stand because of it. English has *has finished* and *is
finished* and uses them loosely; Greek means the second by the perfect
and means it exactly.

## Reduplication: the mark of the perfect

The perfect announces itself at the *front* of the word, as the augment
did, but differently. The first consonant of the stem is repeated with an
**ε** between: **λύω → λέ-λυκα**, **γράφω → γέ-γραφα**, **πιστεύω →
πε-πίστευκα**. This is **reduplication**, and it is the perfect's badge in
every mood, not only the indicative — unlike the augment, which belongs to
the past indicative and nowhere else.

Four adjustments. A stem beginning with **φ, θ** or **χ** reduplicates
with **π, τ, κ**: **θνῄσκω → τέθνηκα**, **φιλέω → πεφίληκα**. A stem
beginning with a vowel lengthens it, as the augment would: **ἄγω →
ἦχα**. A stem beginning with two consonants usually takes a plain **ἐ-**
(but a stop followed by **λ** or **ρ** reduplicates normally, as in
**γέγραφα**): **γιγνώσκω → ἔγνωκα**. A few verbs double a whole syllable — **ἀκούω →
ἀκήκοα**, **ἐγείρω → ἐγήγερμαι**, **ἔρχομαι → ἐλήλυθα** — which is old and
Attic and only needs recognising.

## The perfect active

::: paradigm
| | Perfect active | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λέλυκα** | I have loosed |
| 2 sing. | **λέλυκας** | |
| 3 sing. | **λέλυκε(ν)** | |
| 1 plur. | **λελύκαμεν** | |
| 2 plur. | **λελύκατε** | |
| 3 plur. | **λελύκασι(ν)** | |
:::

The mark after the stem is **-κ-**, and the endings are nearly the first
aorist's — **-α, -ας, -ε, -αμεν, -ατε** — with **-ασι** in the third
plural. Some verbs have no **κ** and keep their stem consonant, sometimes
changed: **γέγραφα**, **πέπομφα** (πέμπω), **γέγονα** (γίγνομαι),
**ἀκήκοα**. Grammarians call these second perfects; you need only learn
them with the verb.

A short list, worth having whole:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **γράφω** | **γέγραφα** | I have written |
| **μανθάνω** | **μεμάθηκα** | I have learned |
| **γιγνώσκω** | **ἔγνωκα** | I have come to know |
| **πιστεύω** | **πεπίστευκα** | I have believed |
| **ἔρχομαι** | **ἐλήλυθα** | I have come — I am here |
| **γίγνομαι** | **γέγονα** | I have become — I am |
| **λαμβάνω** | **εἴληφα** | I have taken |
| **ἀποθνῄσκω** | **τέθνηκα** | I have died — I am dead |

Read the glosses of **ἐλήλυθα**, **γέγονα** and **τέθνηκα** together. *I have come* and so
*I am here*; *I have become* and so *I am*; *I have died* and so *I am
dead*. That is the perfect, every time: the English on the right is what
the Greek means, and the English in the middle is how it got there.

## The perfect middle and passive

::: paradigm
| | Perfect middle/passive | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λέλυμαι** | I have been loosed |
| 2 sing. | **λέλυσαι** | |
| 3 sing. | **λέλυται** | |
| 1 plur. | **λελύμεθα** | |
| 2 plur. | **λέλυσθε** | |
| 3 plur. | **λέλυνται** | |
:::

Reduplication, then the bare stem, then the middle endings of Lesson 31
with no vowel between — **-μαι, -σαι, -ται**. Where the stem ends in a
consonant, it meets the ending and changes: **γράφω → γέγραμμαι,
γέγραψαι, γέγραπται**. The third singular, **γέγραπται** — *it is written,
it stands written* — is the form the Gospels use to quote scripture, and
you will meet it more than any other perfect in Greek.

**τετέλεσται** is this form, from **τελέω**: the stem **τελε-** gained a
**σ** before the ending, as some vowel stems do. **ἐγήγερται**, *he has
been raised — he is risen*, is the same ending on **ἐγείρω**.

## οἶδα, explained at last

**οἶδα** is a perfect. Its root is the one in **εἶδον**, *I saw*; the
perfect of *see* is *I have seen*, and in Greek, to have seen is to know.
It has a perfect's endings and no present at all:

::: paradigm
| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **οἶδα** | I know |
| 2 sing. | **οἶσθα** | |
| 3 sing. | **οἶδε(ν)** | |
| 1 plur. | **ἴσμεν** | |
| 2 plur. | **ἴστε** | |
| 3 plur. | **ἴσασι(ν)** | |
:::

The infinitive is **εἰδέναι**; the participle **εἰδώς, εἰδυῖα, εἰδός**.
Koine evens the plural out — **οἴδαμεν, οἴδατε, οἴδασι** — and you will
see those in the Gospels; in this room, write the Attic.

This is the whole perfect in one word. A past act — the seeing — of which
nothing remains but the state it produced, and the tense names the state.
Lesson 25 set **οἶδα** against **γιγνώσκω**: *to know* against *to come to
know*. You can now say why. **γιγνώσκω** is a process in the present;
**οἶδα** is a perfect, the process over and its result in hand.

## The pluperfect, in a paragraph

What the perfect does for the present, the **pluperfect** does for the
past: the state as it *then* stood. It takes the augment in front of the
reduplication. **ἐγέγραπτο** — *it had been written, it stood written
then*; **ἐτεθνήκει** — *he had died, he was dead by then*; **ἐλελύκη,
ἐλελύκης, ἐλελύκει** — *I had loosed*. The pluperfect of **οἶδα** is
**ᾔδη, ᾔδησθα, ᾔδει** — *I knew*. It is the only one of these you will
need to write this year, and you will need it: *I knew* is a common thing
to have to say. The rest, recognise.

## Past or present?

Julia's question. The honest answer is that the perfect is about *now* and
is built on *then*, and that a language with this tense can say in one
word what English needs a clause for: *it is in the condition of having
been done*.

So **τετέλεσται** is not *it was finished* and not quite *it has been
finished*. It is *it stands finished*. **γέγραπται** is not *it was
written* but *it is written* — it stands on the page, now, and you may go
and look. **μεμάθηκας** is not *you learned* but *you have learned*: you
are, now, someone who knows.

When you read a perfect, ask what state the world is in because of it.
When you write one, be sure that is what you mean.

::: latinbridge
**Latin's perfect does two jobs, and you were told so in Lesson 14.**
*Scrīpsī* is *I wrote* — the Greek aorist, **ἔγραψα** — and *I have
written* — the Greek perfect, **γέγραφα**. One Latin form, two Greek
tenses. Every time you translate a Latin perfect you have been deciding
which, and Greek now makes you say which.

**Latin's perfect passive is the same ambiguity in two words.** *Scrīptum
est* is *it was written* (**ἐγράφη**) or *it is written* (**γέγραπται**).
Jerome's *scrīptum est* for the Gospels' **γέγραπται** is right because he
chose the second reading; a Latin reader has to choose it too. *Cōnsummātum
est* for **τετέλεσται** is the same choice, made the same way.

**And Latin has perfects that mean the present — exactly οἶδα's case.**
*Nōvī*, *I have come to know* and so *I know*; *meminī*, *I have called to
mind* and so *I remember*; *ōdī*, *I hate*. If you have ever wondered why
*nōvī* is a perfect that your grammar translates as a present, you have
already met the Greek perfect and were not told its name. **οἶδα** and
*nōvī* are the same idea in two languages — and **οἶδα** and *vīdī* are the
same ancient root.
:::

::: vocab

  **ἐτελέσθην**; perfect passive **τετέλεσμαι** — **τετέλεσται**, it is
  finished)*
- **γέγραπται** — it is written, it stands written *(perfect passive of
  **γράφω**; the active is **γέγραφα**)*
- **οἶδα** — I know *(a perfect: I have seen, and so I know; **οἶσθα,
  οἶδε, ἴσμεν, ἴστε, ἴσασι**; pluperfect **ᾔδη**, I knew)* *(Lesson 25)*
- **ἀποθνῄσκω** — I die *(aorist **ἀπέθανον**; perfect **τέθνηκα**, I am
  dead)*
- **ἐγείρω** — I raise, I wake *(passive **ἐγείρομαι**, I rise; perfect
  passive **ἐγήγερμαι** — **ἐγήγερται**, he is risen)* *(Lesson 27)*
- **θάπτω** — I bury *(aorist passive **ἐτάφην**)*
- **ἡ ἁμαρτία** — sin; a missing of the mark *(Lesson 19)*
- **παραδίδωμι** — I hand over; I hand on *(aorist **παρέδωκα**)*
- **παραλαμβάνω** — I receive, I take over *(aorist **παρέλαβον**)*
- **ὤφθη** — he was seen; he appeared *(aorist passive of **ὁράω**;
  with the dative of the person who saw)*
- **ὑπέρ** — on behalf of, for *(with the genitive)*
- **κατά** — according to *(with the accusative)*; down from, against
  *(with the genitive)*
- **τρίτος** — third
- **ὁ ἀπόστολος** — one sent out; messenger; apostle
- **ἡμεῖς** — we *(**ἡμῶν, ἡμῖν, ἡμᾶς**)* *(Lesson 19)*

::: note
**τελέω** is a contract verb in the present (**τελεῖ**, Lesson 34), which
is why this lesson gives you its aorist and perfect and not its present.
It is the verb of **τὸ τέλος**, *end, completion*, which you have had as
the adverb **τέλος** since Lesson 14. **τετέλεσται** is *it has reached
its τέλος and is there*.

**παραδίδωμι** and **παραλαμβάνω** are a pair — *hand on* and *take
over* — and Paul uses them of a teaching that passed through his hands:
he received it and handed it on. **παρά** on the front of each is the
*alongside* that makes a handing-over of a giving.

**ὤφθη** is the aorist passive of **ὁράω**, on yet another stem — the
course's list of forms that will not undress (Lesson 29) gains an entry.
*He was seen by Cephas* is the grammar; *he appeared to Cephas* is the
English; the person who did the seeing is in the dative, not after
**ὑπό**.

::: {.exercise title="Aorist or perfect, and what tells you?"}
1. **ἔγραψα** — **γέγραφα**
2. **ἐλύθη** — **λέλυται**
3. **ἔμαθον** — **μεμάθηκα**
4. **ἐγένετο** — **γέγονε**
5. **ἀπέθανε** — **τέθνηκε**
6. **ἐγράφη** — **γέγραπται**
7. **ἠκούσαμεν** — **ἀκηκόαμεν**
8. **ἐπιστεύσατε** — **πεπιστεύκατε**
9. **ἦλθον** — **ἐλήλυθα**
10. **εἶδον** — **οἶδα**
:::

::: answers
1. Aorist — augment and **σα**; perfect — reduplication **γε-** and a
   stem with no **σ**. *I wrote; I have written.*
2. Aorist passive — **θη**; perfect passive — reduplication and **-ται**
   with no vowel. *It was loosed; it is loosed.*
3. Second aorist — stem **μαθ-** with past endings; perfect — **με-** and
   **-κα**. *I learned; I have learned.*
4. Second aorist middle; second perfect — **γε-** and the stem **γον-**
   with no **κ**. *It happened; it has come to be — it is.*
5. Aorist — *he died*; perfect — **τε-** for **θ**, and the meaning *he is
   dead*.
6. Second aorist passive — *it was written*; perfect passive — *it is
   written*, the scripture formula.
7. Aorist — augment **ἠ-**; perfect — Attic reduplication **ἀκηκο-**,
   which must simply be known. *We heard; we have heard.*
8. Aorist; perfect, with **πε-** and **-κατε**. *You believed; you have
   believed* — and so you now believe.
9. Second aorist; perfect, with the doubled **ἐληλυθ-**. *I came; I have
   come* — and so I am here.
10. Aorist of *see*; perfect of the same root, meaning *know*. The one
    pair in the list where the two tenses have different English verbs,
    and the reason is the lesson.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ἡ γραφὴ τετέλεσται· ἐτελέσθη γὰρ χθὲς ὑπὸ τῆς Ἰουλίας.** *(**χθές**
   = yesterday)*
2. **γέγραπται ἐν τῷ βιβλίῳ ὅτι ὁ θεὸς φῶς ἐστιν.**
3. **ὁ Μᾶρκος μεμάθηκε τὰ γράμματα· νῦν οὖν οἶδεν.**
4. **ὁ αἴλουρος λέλουται, ἀλλ᾽ οὐχ ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
5. **ὁ Χριστὸς ἀπέθανε καὶ ἐτάφη καὶ ἐγήγερται.**
6. **πεπιστεύκαμεν καὶ ἐγνώκαμεν ὅτι σὺ εἶ ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ.** *(**ἅγιος**
   = holy; John 6:69)*
7. **ἐλήλυθεν ὁ διδάσκαλος· ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ οὖν ἐστιν.**
8. **οἱ πολλοὶ τῶν ἀδελφῶν ἔτι μένουσιν, τινὲς δὲ τεθνήκασιν.** *(**ἔτι** =
   still)*
9. **ᾔδη ὅτι ὁ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἐβούλετο λούεσθαι.**
10. **ὃ γέγραφα, γέγραφα.** *(John 19:22)*
:::

::: answers
1. *The paper is finished; for it was finished yesterday by Julia.* — the
   perfect for the state, the aorist for the event that produced it, in
   one sentence; **γάρ** joining them as result and cause.
2. *It is written in the book that God is light.* — **γέγραπται**: the
   formula; 1 John 1:5, from Lesson 30, as the thing written.
3. *Marcus has learned the letters; so he now knows.* — a perfect, and
   then the perfect that is **οἶδα**, with **οὖν** between them doing what
   the tense already did.
4. *The cat has been washed, but not by Felix.* — the perfect passive
   with an agent denied: the state holds and the cat is responsible for
   it.
5. *Christ died and was buried and has been raised.* — two aorists and a
   perfect, which is the shape of the Reading, and the reason the third
   verb is in a different tense is the whole of the lesson.
6. *We have believed and have come to know that you are the Holy One of
   God.* — Peter's two perfects: not *we believed* once, but *we are now
   in the state of believing and knowing*. **ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ** with the
   article: a title, the predicate marked as definite.
7. *The teacher has come; so he is in the schoolroom.* — **ἐλήλυθεν**
   means *is here*; the second clause only spells it out.
8. *Most of the brothers still remain, but some have died.* — Paul's
   sentence, nearly; **οἱ πολλοί** with the article and a partitive
   genitive is *the majority*.
   **τεθνήκασιν** — *are dead*.
9. *I knew that the cat did not want to be washed.* — the pluperfect of
   **οἶδα**; the imperfect **ἐβούλετο** keeps the time of the knowing.
10. *What I have written, I have written.* — Pilate, refusing to change the
    inscription. Two perfects: the writing stands, and the refusal is
    carried by the tense. **ἔγραψα, ἔγραψα** would mean only that he did
    it twice.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Julia's question"}
Each sentence has a perfect in it. Say (a) what state now holds, (b) what
past act produced it, and (c) how the sentence would change if the verb
were made aorist.

1. **τετέλεσται.**
2. **γέγραπται.**
3. **μεμάθηκας.**
4. **ἐλήλυθεν ὁ πατήρ.**
5. **ὁ αἴλουρος λέλουται.**
6. **τέθνηκεν ὁ ποιμήν.**
7. **ἐγήγερται τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ.**
8. **πεπίστευκα.**
9. **οἶδα.**
10. **γεγόναμεν μάρτυρες.**
:::

::: answers
1. (a) It is in the finished state. (b) Somebody finished it. (c)
   **ἐτελέσθη** — *it got finished*, an event, and nothing said about now.
2. (a) The words stand on the page. (b) Somebody wrote them. (c)
   **ἐγράφη** — *it was written*, once, by someone, and perhaps since
   erased.
3. (a) You are one who knows. (b) You learned. (c) **ἔμαθες** — *you
   learned*, which does not say whether it stuck.
4. (a) The father is here. (b) He came. (c) **ἦλθεν** — *he came*, which
   is compatible with his having left again.
5. (a) The cat is clean. (b) Somebody — or the cat — washed it. (c)
   **ἐλούθη** — *it was washed*, which the cat may by now have undone.
6. (a) The shepherd is dead. (b) He died. (c) **ἀπέθανεν** — *he died*, a
   report of the event; the perfect reports the loss.
7. (a) He is risen — alive, now. (b) He was raised, on the third day. (c)
   **ἠγέρθη** — *he was raised*, the event on its day. Paul chooses the
   perfect among aorists, and the Reading asks why.
8. (a) I am a believer. (b) I came to believe. (c) **ἐπίστευσα** — *I
   believed*, at some point; a conversion, not a condition.
9. (a) I know. (b) I saw. (c) **εἶδον** — *I saw*, with the knowing gone
   out of it: the two tenses have become two English verbs.
10. (a) We are witnesses. (b) We became them. (c) **ἐγενόμεθα** — *we
    became witnesses*, which is Lesson 31's sentence and says nothing about
    whether we still are.

The exercise is (c) ten times over. A pupil who can say in each case what
the aorist would *fail* to say has understood what the perfect is for.
:::

3. It is written in the book.
4. I have learned the letters.
5. We know the truth.
6. The teacher has come.
7. The cat has been washed.
8. They are dead.
9. Christ has been raised on the third day.

::: answers
1. **τετέλεσται.**
2. **ἐτελέσθη χθές.** — aorist passive. A pupil who writes **τετέλεσται
   χθές** has put a *now* tense with a *then* adverb, which is Marcus's
   error of Lesson 32 in reverse.
3. **γέγραπται ἐν τῷ βιβλίῳ.**
4. **μεμάθηκα τὰ γράμματα.**
5. **ἴσμεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν.** — accept **οἴδαμεν**, which is Koine, if the
   pupil says so.
6. **ἐλήλυθεν ὁ διδάσκαλος.** — **ἦλθεν** is correct Greek and the wrong
   answer: it reports the arrival and not the presence.
7. **ὁ αἴλουρος λέλουται.**
8. **τεθνήκασιν.** — one word; English needs three. The perfect is the
   claim that they *are* dead, not that they died; **ἀπέθανον** is the
   likely wrong answer and is a different sentence.
9. **ὁ Χριστὸς ἐγήγερται τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ.** — dative of the time *at
   which*; the Reading has it in that order.
10. **ὃ γέγραφα, γέγραφα.**

1 and 2 are the examination. A pupil who writes the same Greek for both
has not heard Julia's answer; a pupil who writes **τετέλεσται** for 1 and
**ἐτελέσθη** for 2 and can say what each refuses to claim has.

::: {.voice source="John 19:30"}
Theodosius's text, on its own, as promised. Three aorists and a participle
tell what happened. One perfect is spoken.

**ὅτε οὖν ἔλαβεν τὸ ὄξος [ὁ] Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν· τετέλεσται, καὶ κλίνας τὴν
κεφαλὴν παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα.**

- **ὅτε** — when
- **οὖν** — then, so *(second in its clause)*
- **ἔλαβεν** — took *(aorist of **λαμβάνω**)*
- **τὸ ὄξος** — the sour wine *(the soldiers' drink)*
- **[ὁ] Ἰησοῦς** — Jesus *(the brackets are the editors': the article is
  in some manuscripts and not in others)*
- **εἶπεν** — said *(aorist)*
- **τετέλεσται** — it is finished; it stands completed *(perfect passive,
  third singular, of **τελέω**)*
- **κλίνας** — having bowed *(aorist participle of **κλίνω**)*
- **τὴν κεφαλήν** — his head
- **παρέδωκεν** — handed over *(aorist of **παραδίδωμι**)*
- **τὸ πνεῦμα** — his spirit, his breath

*When, then, Jesus had taken the sour wine, he said, "It is finished," and
bowing his head he handed over his spirit.*

**Read the tenses.** **ἔλαβεν, εἶπεν, παρέδωκεν**: three aorists, three
steps, with an aorist participle, **κλίνας**, for the step between the
second and the third. This is the choreography of Lesson 22 — a sequence
of complete acts, each one before the next — and it is how the narrator
tells what happened.

Inside it, one word is not the narrator's. **τετέλεσται** is spoken, and
it is a perfect. The man speaking does not say **ἐτελέσθη**, *it was
finished*, which would report an event; nor **τέλος**, *at last*, which
would be a comment. He says that the thing stands complete — that the
world is, now, in the state of its being done. The narrator uses the
tense of events because he is telling events. The speaker uses the tense
of states because he is naming one.

**ὅτε … οὖν**: *when, then* — the **οὖν** is not *therefore* here but the
narrative *so*, picking up the thread from the verse before, where the wine
is offered. Lesson 17's connector, in its Koine use.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** John 19:30 is quoted from NA28, with its
bracketed article; check it against the edition in use.

**On the two uses of τετέλεσται.** Julia's use of the word for her
examination is the kind of thing Theodoros does not allow, and the lesson
has him not allow it — gently, by separating the grammar from the text and
giving the text its own ten minutes. Theodosius's objection is right and
should be played as right, not as fussiness; Julia's answer to the grammar
question is also right. Keep both. The course has held since Lesson 16
that scripture is read with reverence and that the grammar is the same
grammar wherever it stands; this is the lesson where those two rules meet,
and the way through is the one Theodoros takes: the word's *form* is
anyone's to study, its *use* on the cross is not anyone's to borrow.

**On Julia.** *A present with the past inside it* is hers, and it is the
best one-line account of the perfect the course offers; Livia writes it
on the board so that it is seen to be Julia's. Her arc is learning to
commit before she is certain, and she does it here, thinking aloud, in
front of the class, and is right. Let that land.

**On consummatum est.** Jerome's Latin is the perfect passive, which Latin
uses for both the aorist and the perfect; here it carries the Greek
perfect correctly because the reader supplies the *is* reading. A pupil
who has done Lesson 14 should be able to say why this is the one place
Latin's double-duty perfect does no harm.
:::

::: {.reading title="What was handed on" source="1 Corinthians 15:3–8 (adapted, then NA28)"}
**First, adapted.** Attic forms you have; the connective **ὅτι** kept
each time, as Paul keeps it; one verb changed where Paul's is a figure
of speech. Two aorists and a perfect, then four of the same aorist. Find
the perfect first and ask why it is alone.

παρέδωκα γὰρ ὑμῖν πρῶτον ὃ καὶ αὐτὸς παρέλαβον· ὅτι ὁ Χριστὸς ἀπέθανεν
ὑπὲρ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν κατὰ τὰς γραφάς, καὶ ὅτι ἐτάφη, καὶ ὅτι ἐγήγερται
τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ κατὰ τὰς γραφάς, καὶ ὅτι ὤφθη τῷ Κηφᾷ, ἔπειτα δὲ τοῖς
δώδεκα.

ἔπειτα ὤφθη πεντακοσίοις ἀδελφοῖς ἅμα, ὧν οἱ μὲν πολλοὶ ἔτι μένουσιν ἐν
τῷ βίῳ, τινὲς δὲ τεθνήκασιν. ἔπειτα ὤφθη τῷ Ἰακώβῳ, ἔπειτα τοῖς
ἀποστόλοις πᾶσιν. τέλος δὲ πάντων ὤφθη καὶ ἐμοί, ὥσπερ τῷ ἐκτρώματι.

**Then Paul's own words.** Koine; one sentence, in the standard text,
from **παρέδωκα** to **κἀμοί**.

παρέδωκα γὰρ ὑμῖν ἐν πρώτοις, ὃ καὶ παρέλαβον, ὅτι Χριστὸς ἀπέθανεν ὑπὲρ
τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν κατὰ τὰς γραφὰς καὶ ὅτι ἐτάφη καὶ ὅτι ἐγήγερται τῇ
ἡμέρᾳ τῇ τρίτῃ κατὰ τὰς γραφὰς καὶ ὅτι ὤφθη Κηφᾷ εἶτα τοῖς δώδεκα· ἔπειτα
ὤφθη ἐπάνω πεντακοσίοις ἀδελφοῖς ἐφάπαξ, ἐξ ὧν οἱ πλείονες μένουσιν ἕως
ἄρτι, τινὲς δὲ ἐκοιμήθησαν· ἔπειτα ὤφθη Ἰακώβῳ εἶτα τοῖς ἀποστόλοις πᾶσιν·
ἔσχατον δὲ πάντων ὡσπερεὶ τῷ ἐκτρώματι ὤφθη κἀμοί.

::: gloss
- **ἐν πρώτοις** — among the first things, first of all *(the adaptation
  has the adverb **πρῶτον**)*
- **ὃ καὶ παρέλαβον** — which I also received *(**ὅς** neuter, the thing
  handed on)*
- **αὐτός** — myself *(nominative, emphatic: I myself)*
- **ὁ Χριστός** — the Anointed; Christ *(Paul uses it without the article,
  as a name)*
- **ἀπέθανεν** — died *(aorist of **ἀποθνῄσκω**)*
- **αἱ γραφαί** — the scriptures *(Lesson 3's **γραφή**, *writing*, with a
  new weight)*
- **ἐτάφη** — was buried *(second aorist passive of **θάπτω**)*
- **ἐγήγερται** — has been raised, is risen *(perfect passive of
  **ἐγείρω**)*
- **τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ τρίτῃ** — on the third day *(the adjective after the noun
  with the article repeated — Lesson 10's second attributive position)*
- **Κηφᾶς** — Cephas, the Aramaic name of Peter *(dative **Κηφᾷ**)*
- **εἶτα, ἔπειτα** — then, next
- **οἱ δώδεκα** — the Twelve
- **ἐπάνω** — more than, above *(with a number)*
- **πεντακόσιοι** — five hundred
- **ἐφάπαξ, ἅμα** — at one time, all at once
- **οἱ πλείονες** — the greater number, the majority *(the adaptation has
  **οἱ πολλοί**)*
- **ἕως ἄρτι** — until now
- **ἔτι** — still
- **ἐκοιμήθησαν** — have fallen asleep *(aorist passive of **κοιμάομαι**;
  Paul's word for dying — the adaptation says **τεθνήκασιν**)*
- **Ἰάκωβος** — James
- **ἔσχατον** — last *(adverb; the adaptation has **τέλος**)*
- **ὡσπερεί** — as it were, as if *(**ὥσπερ** + **εἰ**)*
- **τὸ ἔκτρωμα** — a child born too early, an untimely birth *(Paul's word
  for himself: the apostle born out of due time)*
- **κἀμοί** — to me also *(**καὶ ἐμοί** run together)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. **παρέδωκα … παρέλαβον.** Translate the pair and say what the
   **παρα-** on each contributes.
2. Four **ὅτι** clauses hang from **παρέδωκα**. Give the four verbs and
   name the tense and voice of each.
3. Three of those four verbs are aorist and one is perfect. Which is the
   perfect, and what does the change of tense claim that an aorist would
   not?
4. Rewrite **ἐγήγερται** as an aorist passive. Translate both. Which one
   would Paul's readers, reading in the present, have more reason to care
   about?
5. **ὤφθη** occurs four times in Paul's tier. What voice is it, and why
   are the people who saw in the dative rather than after **ὑπό**?
6. **κατὰ τὰς γραφάς**, twice. Which two events does Paul attach it to,
   and which two does he not?
7. **οἱ πλείονες μένουσιν … τινὲς δὲ ἐκοιμήθησαν.** Paul puts the living
   in the present and the dead in the aorist. The adaptation puts the
   dead in the perfect. What is the difference in claim?
8. **ἔσχατον δὲ πάντων … κἀμοί.** Where does Paul put himself in the
   list, and what word tells you how he regards the position?
9. Translate the adaptation's second paragraph.
10. Paul's whole passage is one sentence held together by **ὅτι** and
    **ἔπειτα**. Read it aloud once more as a list. What is being listed —
    events, or witnesses? Where in the sentence does the list change
    kind?
:::

::: answers
1. *I handed on … I received.* **παρά**, *alongside*: a giving and a
   taking that pass a thing from one hand to the next. What was handed on
   is what was received; the two verbs make a chain and Paul is one link.
2. **ἀπέθανεν** — aorist active; **ἐτάφη** — aorist passive; **ἐγήγερται**
   — perfect passive; **ὤφθη** — aorist passive.
3. **ἐγήγερται.** The aorists report events that happened and are over:
   he died, was buried, was seen. The perfect claims a state that still
   holds: he *is* risen, now, as Paul writes. The death and burial are
   over; the rising is not reported as over.
4. **ἠγέρθη** — *he was raised*; **ἐγήγερται** — *he has been raised and
   is risen*. The second: it is the one of the two that is about the
   reader's own present. (Both forms are Paul's elsewhere; here he chose
   this one.)
5. Aorist passive of **ὁράω**: *he was seen*. The seers are in the dative
   because the idiom is *he appeared to*, not *he was seen by*: the
   grammar treats them as the ones the appearing was directed at, not as
   agents. **ὑπὸ Κηφᾶ** would make Cephas the doer, and the sentence is
   not about what Cephas did.
6. To the death and the raising; not to the burial or the appearances.
   The scriptures are cited for what needed foretelling.
7. Paul's aorist says what happened to them — they fell asleep. The
   adaptation's **τεθνήκασιν** says the state they are in — they are dead.
   Paul's word, **ἐκοιμήθησαν**, is itself a figure that declines to say
   *dead* at all, and that is why the adaptation could not keep it.
8. Last — **ἔσχατον πάντων**. **ὡσπερεὶ τῷ ἐκτρώματι**: he regards it as
   the place of one born out of time, not entitled to it.
9. *Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, of whom
   most still remain alive, but some have died. Then he appeared to James,
   then to all the apostles. And last of all he appeared also to me, as to
   one born out of time.*
10. The first four **ὅτι** clauses list events — died, buried, raised,
    seen. From **εἶτα τοῖς δώδεκα** onward the verb stays **ὤφθη** and
    what changes is the dative: the list has become a list of witnesses.
    The hinge is the fourth **ὅτι**, where the last event is a seeing, and
    the seers take over the sentence.
:::
:::

::: question
1. Is **τετέλεσται** a past or a present? Does the answer change if you
   ask what it *says* rather than when it *happened* — and which question
   does a grammar exist to answer?
2. Greek says *I know* with a word that means *I have seen*. What does
   that suggest the Greeks thought knowing was? Is **γιγνώσκω**, *coming
   to know*, a different theory or the same one earlier?
3. Latin's one perfect does two jobs and Romans managed. What is lost
   when a language stops marking a difference — and who is the first to
   notice, the speakers or the people who learn it afterwards?
:::

::: story
Marcus kept his paper. He had kept the tablet from Lesson 14 too, the one
headed **things Latin cannot do**, and that evening he added a line to it,
then crossed the line out, then wrote it again lower down under a heading
of its own.

The new heading read: **things Latin does with one word and Greek with
two**.

Under it: *scrīpsī* — **ἔγραψα, γέγραφα**.

And beneath that, in smaller letters, after some thought: *nōvī* —
**οἶδα**, *and this one Latin got right*.

He showed it to Julia the next morning, which he had never done with the
first tablet. She read it twice, corrected an accent, and said that the
second heading was better than the first because it was true in both
directions.

"**μεμάθηκας**," she said, handing it back. It was not entirely clear
whether she was quoting Theodoros or agreeing with him. Marcus decided,
on reflection, that it did not matter which, and that the tense was the
point.
