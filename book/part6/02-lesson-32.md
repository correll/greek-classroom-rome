# What Will Be

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"One sentence each," said Theodoros, "about the end of the year. In Greek.
What you will have done by then. You may be ambitious; you may not be
vague."

Marcus wrote six.

He read them standing, which was his habit when he expected to be right,
and the class, which had learned to tell, settled in.

> **γράψω τὰ γράμματα καλῶς.**
>
> **μαθήσομαι πάντα τὰ ῥήματα.**
>
> **οὐ ψεύσομαι.**
>
> **ἔσομαι σοφός.**
>
> **μένω ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ.**
>
> **ἐλεύσομαι αὔριον.**

"*I will write the letters well. I will learn all the verbs. I will not
lie. I will be wise. I will stay in the school. I will come tomorrow.*"
He sat down. "Six promises. All in the future. You may check them in
July."

"I will check them now," said Theodoros, "because a promise is a sentence
whose truth is decided later, and its grammar is decided at once. Julia.
Line four."

"**ἔσομαι.** The future of **εἰμί**." Julia paused. "It ends in **-ομαι**.
It is middle."

"It is. The future of *I am* has middle endings and no middle meaning, and
no grammarian has given a reason for it that survives being asked a second
question. Line two."

"**μαθήσομαι** — also middle. *I will learn.*" Julia frowned at it. "Is it
a deponent in the future only?"

"It is. So is **γνώσομαι**, so is **ἀκούσομαι**, so is **ὄψομαι**, *I will
see*. A whole family of common verbs that are active everywhere else and
go middle to talk about what has not happened yet. You will learn the list
and you will not be given a reason, because there is not one that
survives examination. Now line five."

Marcus looked at it. "**μένω ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ.**"

"Which means?"

"*I will stay in —*" He stopped. "*I stay.* It is present."

"It is present. You have promised to be sitting where you are, which I
grant you are. The future of **μένω** is **μενῶ**. The accent moves to the
end and becomes a circumflex, and that is the entire difference between
staying now and staying later. Lesson 25: one accent turned a question
into a statement. Here one accent turns a fact into a promise."

"That is unreasonable."

"It is Attic. Liquid verbs — **μένω**, **κρίνω**, **ἀποκρίνομαι** — will not
take the **σ**, and they contract instead, and the accent is what is left.
Line six."

Marcus had been expecting this one. "**ἐλεύσομαι.** *I will come.* Felix
says it is in the Gospels."

"Felix is right. It is in the Gospels; it is good Koine; you will read it
in church on Sunday and you may write it in Lesson 35. In this room we
write Attic, and Attic has no future of **ἔρχομαι** at all. It uses a
different verb. **εἶμι**: *I am going*, *I am on my way*. In Attic, to say
you will come is to say you have already started."

"That is not a future. That is a present with its mind made up."

"That is the best description of **εἶμι** I have heard, and you may keep
it." Theodoros picked up the tablet. "Lines one and three are correct.
**γράψω**: the **σ**, on the stem of **γράφω**, fused to **ψ** exactly as
in the aorist — but no augment, and the endings of the present. **οὐ
ψεύσομαι**: deponent, **σ**, future. Two out of six, and the two you
wrote first, before you began to feel confident."

He set the tablet on the shelf.

"I am keeping this. It will be read aloud in the last week of the year,
and each sentence will be checked against the facts. That is what the
future tense is for. It is the only tense whose sentences can be marked
twice."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The future: the σ without the augment

The **future** says that something will happen. Its mark is the **σ** you
know from the first aorist — but with no augment at the front, and the
*present* endings behind it.

::: paradigm
| | Future active | Future middle |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύσω** | **λύσομαι** |
| 2 sing. | **λύσεις** | **λύσῃ** |
| 3 sing. | **λύσει** | **λύσεται** |
| 1 plur. | **λύσομεν** | **λυσόμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **λύσετε** | **λύσεσθε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύσουσι(ν)** | **λύσονται** |
:::

Compare the three you now have:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **λύω** | I loose | present stem, present endings |
| **ἔλυσα** | I loosed | augment + **σα** + past endings |
| **λύσω** | I will loose | **σ** + present endings |

The **σ** fuses with a stem consonant as it did in the aorist: **γράφω →
γράψω**, **πέμπω → πέμψω**, **διδάσκω → διδάξω**, **ἄγω → ἄξω**, **πείθω →
πείσω**, **βλέπω → βλέψω**.

::: note
**How to tell a future from an aorist**, since both have a **σ**: look at
the front and the back. An aorist has an augment and past endings
(**ἔγραψα**, **ἔγραψε**); a future has no augment and present endings
(**γράψω**, **γράψει**). **ἔγραψε** and **γράψει** differ at both ends and
share only the middle, and the middle is the **σ**, which only tells you
that one of the two is in play.
:::

## Verbs that will not take the σ

Verbs whose stem ends in **λ, μ, ν** or **ρ** — the *liquid* verbs — refuse
the **σ**. They form the future with **-ε-** and then contract it, so that
the future looks like the present with the accent moved to the end:

::: paradigm
| | **μένω** I stay | **μενῶ** I will stay |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **μένω** | **μενῶ** |
| 2 sing. | **μένεις** | **μενεῖς** |
| 3 sing. | **μένει** | **μενεῖ** |
| 1 plur. | **μένομεν** | **μενοῦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **μένετε** | **μενεῖτε** |
| 3 plur. | **μένουσι(ν)** | **μενοῦσι(ν)** |
:::

**ἀποκρίνομαι → ἀποκρινοῦμαι**, *I will answer*. **κρίνω → κρινῶ**, *I will
judge*. And **λέγω** — which has a second aorist of its own — has a future
of its own too: **ἐρῶ**, *I will say*, on this same pattern. Learn it as a
pair with **εἶπον**.

## The future of *to be*

::: paradigm
| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἔσομαι** | I will be |
| 2 sing. | **ἔσῃ** | |
| 3 sing. | **ἔσται** | |
| 1 plur. | **ἐσόμεθα** | |
| 2 plur. | **ἔσεσθε** | |
| 3 plur. | **ἔσονται** | |
:::

Middle in form, with no middle meaning. Note **ἔσται**, without the vowel
the rest of the table has; it is the form you will meet most.

## The future of *to come*: εἶμι

Attic **ἔρχομαι** has no future. It borrows one from an older verb,
**εἶμι**, which in the present means *I am going* and in Attic prose is
used as the future *I will go, I will come*:

::: paradigm
| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **εἶμι** | I will go |
| 2 sing. | **εἶ** | |
| 3 sing. | **εἶσι(ν)** | |
| 1 plur. | **ἴμεν** | |
| 2 plur. | **ἴτε** | |
| 3 plur. | **ἴασι(ν)** | |
:::

Two warnings. **εἶμι** is not **εἰμί**: the breathing is the same and the
accent is not, and *I will go* has it on the first syllable. And **εἶ** —
*you will go* — is spelled exactly like **εἶ**, *you are*. The sentence
decides, as it decided **λούεται**.

Koine let **ἔρχομαι** grow a future of its own, **ἐλεύσομαι**, and the
Gospels use it. Recognise it; in this room, write **εἶμι**.

## The futures that go middle

A number of common verbs are active in the present and **middle in the
future**, with no change of meaning. There is no rule. There is a list.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἀκούω** | **ἀκούσομαι** | I will hear |
| **γιγνώσκω** | **γνώσομαι** | I will know |
| **μανθάνω** | **μαθήσομαι** | I will learn |
| **λαμβάνω** | **λήψομαι** | I will take |
| **ὁράω** | **ὄψομαι** | I will see |
| **εἰμί** | **ἔσομαι** | I will be |

The deponents stay deponent: **βούλομαι → βουλήσομαι**, **δύναμαι →
δυνήσομαι**, **γίγνομαι → γενήσομαι**, **ψεύδομαι → ψεύσομαι**, **εὔχομαι →
εὔξομαι**.

## What the future does not do

Every tense you have learned so far asked two questions: *when*, and *what
shape*. **ἔλεγε** and **εἶπε** are both past and differ in aspect; **λέγε**
and **εἰπέ** are both commands and differ in aspect. The future asks only
*when*. **γράψω** is *I will write* — once, or at length, or habitually;
the form does not say. It is the one tense in Greek that behaves the way
Marcus wanted them all to behave in Lesson 14, and he may add it to the
other side of his column.

::: note
**For reading: the future infinitive.** Reported speech (Lesson 16) can
carry a future: **λέγει γράψειν** — *he says he will write*; **λέγει
τοὺς παῖδας σοφοὺς ἔσεσθαι** — *he says the children will be wise*.
**λύσειν**, **ἔσεσθαι**: the **σ** with the infinitive endings. You need
only recognise them.

**And the future passive**, which you have seen twice: **παρακληθήσονται**
in the Beatitudes, *they will be comforted*; **προστεθήσεται** in today's
Reading, *will be added*. Its mark is **-θησ-**: the **θη** of the aorist
passive with the **σ** of the future behind it. **λυθήσομαι**, *I will be
loosed*. Recognise it; you will not be asked to write it this year.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin has a future, and it has two of them, and that is the trouble.**
*Amābō*, *monēbō* — the **-bō** future of the first two conjugations — is
clear enough. But *dīcam*, *dūcam*, *audiam* — the future of the third and
fourth — is spelled exactly like the present subjunctive: *dīcam* is *I
will say* and *let me say* and *I might say*, and Latin leaves you to
work it out. Greek's **σ** closes that door. **λέξω** or **ἐρῶ** is a
future and nothing else.

**And Latin's *eō* is Greek's εἶμι.** The same verb, from the same ancient
root: *eō, īs, it* beside **εἶμι, εἶ, εἶσι**. Latin kept it as a plain
present, *I go*; Attic took its present and made it do the future's work.
If you have conjugated *eō* you have conjugated **εἶμι** and need only
move the meaning forward a day.

**Where Latin is richer:** it has a future perfect, *scrīpserō*, *I will
have written*, and uses it constantly. Greek almost never does. When a
Latin sentence has *cum scrīpserō*, the Greek will have an aorist
participle or a subjunctive, and no tense at all for the *will have*.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **μένω** — I remain, I stay; I wait *(future **μενῶ**; aorist **ἔμεινα**)*
- **εἶμι** — I will go, I will come *(the Attic future of **ἔρχομαι**)*
- **ἔσομαι** — I will be *(future of **εἰμί**; third singular **ἔσται**)*
- **λαμβάνω** — I take, I receive *(future **λήψομαι**; aorist **ἔλαβον**)*
- **ὁράω** — I see *(future **ὄψομαι**; aorist **εἶδον**)*
- **ζητέω** — I seek, I look for *(future **ζητήσω**)*
- **μεριμνάω** — I worry, I am anxious *(future **μεριμνήσω**)*
- **ὑμεῖς** — you *(plural; **ὑμῶν, ὑμῖν, ὑμᾶς**)*
- **πᾶς, πᾶσα, πᾶν** — all, every; *(plural)* everyone, everything
- **σήμερον** — today
- **αὔριον** — tomorrow
- **πρῶτον** — first, first of all
- **μᾶλλον** — more; rather
- **οὕτως** — thus, so, in this way *(**οὕτω** before a consonant)*
- **τὸ σῶμα** — body *(genitive **σώματος**)*

::: note
**ζητέω** and **μεριμνάω** are *contract* verbs — their present forms run
two vowels together (**ζητεῖτε**, **μεριμνᾶτε**) and Lesson 34 unpacks
them. Their futures are regular, **ζητήσω** and **μεριμνήσω**, with the
stem vowel lengthened before the **σ**, and those you can write today.
The present forms, when you meet them in the Reading, are glossed whole.

**πᾶς** is of the third declension: **πᾶς, παντός** masculine, **πᾶσα,
πάσης** feminine, **πᾶν, παντός** neuter. **πάντα** is *all things,
everything*. With the article inside the group — **πᾶσα ἡ γῆ** — it is
*all the earth*; without an article — **πᾶσα γῆ** — *every land*.

**αὔριον** is an adverb, *tomorrow*. Matthew gives it an article, **ἡ
αὔριον**, and makes a noun of it, as Luke made **ὁ πλησίον** a noun in
Lesson 22. *The tomorrow* then has a verb of its own, and that is the point
of the verse.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory.

1. **ἐλούθη** — give the tense and the voice.
2. Translate **ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός**.
3. After **ὑπό**, which case names the agent of a passive?
4. **ἦν** and **ἐγένετο**: which one has a beginning in it?
5. Give the vocative of **Μᾶρκος**.
6. Translate **γνῶθι σαυτόν** and say what aspect the imperative is.
7. What does **ὅτι** do after **λέγει**?
8. **πιστεύω** takes which case?
9. Turn **βούλομαι μανθάνειν** into *we want to learn*.
10. **χαίρειν** at the head of a letter: what form is it, and what is it
    doing there?
:::

::: answers
1. Aorist passive — **-θη-**.
2. *The good shepherd* — attributive position, Lesson 10, with the
   article repeated.
3. The genitive.
4. **ἐγένετο** — *came to be*. **ἦν** has no edges.
5. **Μᾶρκε.**
6. *Know yourself* — aorist: come to know, once, completely.
7. Introduces reported speech: *he says that …*
8. The dative (and in the New Testament also **εἰς** with the accusative).
9. **βουλόμεθα μανθάνειν.**
10. An infinitive, *to rejoice*; it is the greeting formula, with a verb
    of wishing understood.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Present or future, and what tells you?"}
1. **γράφω** — **γράψω**
2. **μένω** — **μενῶ**
3. **ἀκούετε** — **ἀκούσεσθε**
4. **ἐστί** — **ἔσται**
5. **ἔρχεται** — **εἶσι**
6. **λαμβάνει** — **λήψεται**
7. **πέμπομεν** — **πέμψομεν**
8. **διδάσκεις** — **διδάξεις**
9. **γίγνεται** — **γενήσεται**
10. **βλέπω** — **ὄψομαι**
:::

::: answers
1. Present; future — the **σ** fused to **ψ**.
2. Present; future — nothing but the accent: liquid verb.
3. Present active; future *middle* — **ἀκούω** goes middle in the future.
   Watch for pupils who call **ἀκούσεσθε** a passive: there is no **θη**.
4. Present; future of **εἰμί**.
5. Present (deponent); future — a different verb altogether, **εἶμι**.
6. Present; future middle, with the stem changed: **λαμβάνω** keeps its
   **-αν-** only in the present.
7. Present; future — **π + σ = ψ**.
8. Present; future — **κ + σ = ξ**.
9. Present; future — a deponent staying deponent, with the stem **γεν-**
   and a lengthened vowel.
10. Present; future — **ὄψομαι** is the future of **ὁράω**, and the class
    has **βλέπω** for *see*. Accept the pair as *I look at* / *I will see*;
    the point is that the future comes from a different stem, like
    **εἶπον**.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **αὔριον γράψω τὴν ἐπιστολήν· σήμερον δὲ οὐ γράφω.**
2. **μενοῦμεν ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ καὶ μαθησόμεθα τὰ γράμματα.**
   *(**τὸ διδασκαλεῖον** = the schoolroom)*
3. **ὁ διδάσκαλος εἶσι πρὸς τὸν πατέρα καὶ ἐρεῖ τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
4. **οὐκ ἔσεσθε σοφοί· οὐ γὰρ ἀκούετε.**
5. **τίς ὄψεται τὸν αἴλουρον; οὐδεὶς ὄψεται· ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ γάρ ἐστιν.**
   *(**οὐδείς** = nobody)*
6. **ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν δώσει ὑμῖν πάντα.** *(**δώσει** = will give, future of
   **δίδωμι**)*
7. **ἡ αὔριον μεριμνήσει περὶ ἑαυτῆς.** *(**ἑαυτῆς** = itself)*
8. **ζητήσομεν πρῶτον τὴν δικαιοσύνην, καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ληψόμεθα.** *(**τὰ
   ἄλλα** = the other things)*
9. **ὁ Μᾶρκος λέγει πάντα γράψειν.**
10. **σήμερον μὲν παῖδές ἐσμεν, αὔριον δὲ ἄνδρες ἐσόμεθα.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Tomorrow I will write the letter; but today I am not writing.* — the
   two forms differ by **σ** and nothing else.
2. *We will stay in the schoolroom and will learn the letters.* — a liquid
   future beside a middle future. **μενοῦμεν**: the accent is the tense.
3. *The teacher will go to the father and will tell the truth.* —
   **εἶσι** third singular of **εἶμι**; **ἐρεῖ** the liquid-pattern future
   of **λέγω**.
4. *You will not be wise; for you do not listen.* — **ἔσεσθε** future;
   **ἀκούετε** present; **γάρ** promising a reason, and here delivering
   one.
5. *Who will see the cat? Nobody will see it; for it is in the house.* —
   **ὄψεται** twice, with the object understood the second time.
6. *Your father will give you everything.* — Matthew 6:33, nearly.
7. *Tomorrow will worry about itself.* — Matthew 6:34, nearly; the adverb
   with the article as subject, and a future for its verb.
8. *We will seek justice first, and will receive the rest.* — a **σ**
   future beside **λήψομαι**.
9. *Marcus says he will write everything.* — the future infinitive in
   reported speech. The subject of the infinitive is the same as the
   subject of **λέγει**, so it stays unexpressed and in the nominative.
10. *Today we are children, but tomorrow we will be men.* — **μέν … δέ**
    carrying the two times; **παῖδες** and **ἄνδρες** both predicates,
    both without the article.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Marcus's tablet"}
Marcus's six sentences, and four more he added that evening. For each, say
whether it is a correct future, and if it is not, say what it is and
correct it.

1. **γράψω τὰ γράμματα καλῶς.**
2. **μαθήσομαι πάντα τὰ ῥήματα.** *(**τὸ ῥῆμα** = word; verb)*
3. **οὐ ψεύσομαι.**
4. **ἔσομαι σοφός.**
5. **μένω ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ.**
6. **ἐλεύσομαι αὔριον.**
7. **ἀκούσω τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
8. **ἔγραψα τὴν ἐπιστολὴν αὔριον.**
9. **γνώσομαι τὴν ἀλήθειαν.**
10. **ἔσται καλῶς.**
:::

::: answers
1. Correct. **φ + σ = ψ**, no augment, present ending.
2. Correct. The future of **μανθάνω** is middle.
3. Correct. Deponent; **δ + σ = σ**.
4. Correct, and middle in form, as Julia said.
5. Present. *I stay.* Correct to **μενῶ**.
6. Koine. Correct in a Gospel; in Attic, **εἶμι αὔριον**. Accept a pupil
   who says it is a future and *not wrong*, and ask them whose Greek it is.
7. Half right. The sense is future but the Attic form is **ἀκούσομαι**:
   **ἀκούω** goes middle. (**ἀκούσω** does exist — as an aorist
   subjunctive, which is Lesson 35's business, and is why it is dangerous
   here.) **τοῦ διδασκάλου** is the right case: one hears a *person* in
   the genitive.
8. Aorist with a future adverb. **ἔγραψα** has an augment and a past
   ending; **αὔριον** contradicts it. Correct to **γράψω τὴν ἐπιστολὴν
   αὔριον** — or keep the aorist and change the adverb to **χθές**,
   *yesterday*, which Marcus would prefer not to.
9. Correct. Middle future of **γιγνώσκω**.
10. Correct. *It will be well.* **ἔσται**, the form without the vowel.

Six correct out of ten is an improvement on two out of six, and the two
wrong ones are wrong in the two ways the lesson warned about: a present
posing as a future, and an aorist with the **σ** but the wrong ends.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
1. I will write the letter tomorrow.
2. We will stay in the house.
3. You *(one person)* will be wise.
4. The teacher will go to the father.
5. They will learn all the letters.
6. I will not lie.
7. Who will see the cat?
8. We will hear the voice of the teacher.
9. Tomorrow will worry about itself. *(itself: **ἑαυτῆς**)*
10. Today we are children; tomorrow we will be men.
:::

::: answers
1. **αὔριον γράψω τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
2. **μενοῦμεν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.** — a pupil who writes **μένομεν** has
   promised to be where they are.
3. **ἔσῃ σοφός.** — accept **ἔσει**, which Attic also wrote.
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος εἶσι πρὸς τὸν πατέρα.** — **εἶσι**, not **ἐστί**; the
   two are one breathing and one accent apart, and a pupil who confuses
   them has written *the teacher is to the father*.
5. **μαθήσονται πάντα τὰ γράμματα.** — middle. **μαθήσουσι** is not
   Greek.
6. **οὐ ψεύσομαι.**
7. **τίς ὄψεται τὸν αἴλουρον;**
8. **ἀκουσόμεθα τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ διδασκάλου.** — the *thing* heard in the
   accusative. (Accept **τῆς φωνῆς**; Greek does both.) **ἀκούσομεν** is
   the error to expect.
9. **ἡ αὔριον μεριμνήσει ἑαυτῆς.** — Matthew's own sentence, with the
   article making a noun of the adverb; accept **περὶ ἑαυτῆς**.
10. **σήμερον μὲν παῖδές ἐσμεν, αὔριον δὲ ἄνδρες ἐσόμεθα.** — **παῖδές
    ἐσμεν**: the enclitic throws its accent back onto the noun. A pupil who
    writes **παῖδες ἐσμέν** has forgotten Lesson 25.

The examination is 2 and 5: the liquid future that is all accent, and the
middle future that English gives no warning of.
:::

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 6:34"}
The last sentence of the passage you will read in the second period. One
future in it, and it is not given to you.

**Μὴ οὖν μεριμνήσητε εἰς τὴν αὔριον, ἡ γὰρ αὔριον μεριμνήσει ἑαυτῆς·
ἀρκετὸν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ ἡ κακία αὐτῆς.**

- **μὴ … μεριμνήσητε** — do not worry *(**μή** with a form you have not
  learned — the aorist subjunctive, the *don't* of a single act that Lesson
  26 said to let wait; it waits until Lesson 35)*
- **οὖν** — therefore *(second in its clause, as always)*
- **εἰς τὴν αὔριον** — about tomorrow *(Koine **εἰς** for *concerning*;
  the adverb with the article, made a noun)*
- **ἡ αὔριον** — tomorrow *(now the subject)*
- **μεριμνήσει** — will worry *(future, third singular)*
- **ἑαυτῆς** — about itself *(genitive, after the verb of worrying)*
- **ἀρκετόν** — enough, sufficient *(neuter; the predicate, with no
  article and no verb)*
- **τῇ ἡμέρᾳ** — for the day
- **ἡ κακία** — trouble, badness *(the noun of **κακός**)*
- **αὐτῆς** — its *(the day's)*

*Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about
itself. Enough for the day is its own trouble.*

**Read the tenses.** The command to the listener is a prohibition; the
only future tense in the sentence belongs to **ἡ αὔριον**. Tomorrow has a
verb. You do not. The grammar gives the future to the future and leaves
you the day, and **ἀρκετόν** — no article, no verb, Lesson 10's predicate
standing alone — says the day is enough.

**And read the γάρ.** *For tomorrow will worry about itself.* Lesson 26
taught you to audit a **γάρ**: does the reason given support the
instruction? Here the claim is that worry is tomorrow's business and
tomorrow is competent to do it. Whether that is consolation or irony
depends on how you read **μεριμνήσει**, and the class will not agree.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** Matthew 6:34 is quoted from NA28; check it
against the edition in use. The verse is the close of the Reading, by
design: the class meets the sentence in the first period and the passage
it closes in the second.

**On ἀρκετόν.** It is a one-word sentence of the kind the course has read
since **χαλεπὰ τὰ καλά** in Lesson 3: predicate, no article, verb
understood. A pupil who sees that without being told has carried Lesson 3
through thirty lessons.

**On Marcus.** His promises are a genuine attempt, not a performance, and
the class should hear that. He is wrong about the grammar four times in
six and right about what the future tense is *for* — a sentence that can
be checked later — which is the lesson's point, and Theodoros gives him
that in the closing scene. Do not let the room laugh at line five; let it
notice that the error is one accent, and that an accent is enough.

**On the subjunctive.** **μεριμνήσητε** is the second time a pupil has had
to take the aorist subjunctive whole (Lesson 26 named the construction).
Keep the promise the gloss makes: Lesson 35 pays it.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. List every future tense in the adapted tier. There are five. Give each
   with its subject.
2. Matthew's tier has only two futures. Find them. Then say what Matthew
   wrote in place of each of the adaptation's other three.
3. **ἐδόμεθα** and **πιόμεθα** are futures. What is unusual about their
   form, and which verbs in the vocabulary box behave the same way?
4. **σήμερον … αὔριον** in the sentence about the grass. What is the
   grass's verb for today, and for tomorrow? Name the voice of the second.
5. **ἡ αὔριον μεριμνήσει ἑαυτῆς.** What part of speech is **αὔριον** here,
   and what has made it so?
6. **προστεθήσεται.** Divide it into its marks and say what each one
   tells you.
7. Twice Matthew says **ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν ὁ οὐράνιος**, and the adaptation gives
   the father a third verb besides. What does the father *do* in each of
   the three? Give the verbs and their tenses.
8. **οἶδε γὰρ ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν** — *for your father knows*. What is the
   **γάρ** giving a reason for? Audit it, as in Lesson 26.
9. Compare **ζητεῖτε δὲ πρῶτον** with **μὴ μεριμνᾶτε**. One is a command
   and one is a prohibition; what do they have in common, and what does
   the passage put in the place of worrying?
10. Translate verse 33 from Matthew's tier.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐνδύσει** (ὁ θεός); **ἐδόμεθα** (we); **πιόμεθα** (we); **δώσει**
   (ὁ πατήρ, understood from the sentence before — accept *God*);
   **μεριμνήσει** (ἡ αὔριον).
2. **μεριμνήσει** and **προστεθήσεται**. For **ἐνδύσει** Matthew has no
   verb at all: **οὐ πολλῷ μᾶλλον ὑμᾶς**, with *clothe* understood from
   **ἀμφιέννυσιν** before it. For **ἐδόμεθα** and **πιόμεθα** he has
   **τί φάγωμεν; τί πίωμεν;** — subjunctives, *what are we to eat, what are
   we to drink*: not a prediction but a deliberation. For **δώσει** he has
   the passive **προστεθήσεται**, *will be added*, with no giver named.
   Each change says something: the adaptation names who will act; Matthew
   twice declines to.
3. They are middle in form, though *eat* and *drink* are active in the
   present. **ἀκούω, γιγνώσκω, μανθάνω, λαμβάνω, ὁράω, εἰμί** do the same.
4. **ἐστιν**, present — *it is*, today; **βάλλεται**, present passive —
   *it is thrown*, tomorrow. Matthew has participles, **ὄντα** and
   **βαλλόμενον**, both agreeing with **τὸν χόρτον**. The grass's whole
   life is two days and two verbs.
5. A noun. The article **ἡ** has made it one: *the tomorrow*. Compare
   **ὁ πλησίον** in Lesson 22. Without the article it is an adverb, as in
   **αὔριον … βάλλεται**.
6. **προσ-** (to, in addition) + **τε-** (the stem of *put*) + **-θησ-**
   (the aorist passive **θη** with the future **σ**) + **-εται** (present
   middle ending, third singular). Future passive: *it will be put to you*,
   *will be added*.
7. **τρέφει αὐτά** — feeds the birds; **οἶδεν** — knows what you need;
   and in the adaptation **δώσει** — will give. Two presents and a future:
   what he does now and what he will do.
8. For the prohibition before it: *do not go about asking what to eat …
   for your father knows you need these things.* The reason supports the
   instruction only if knowing implies providing; the next sentence
   (**δώσει**, **προστεθήσεται**) supplies the missing step. A pupil who
   says the **γάρ** is doing more work than it can carry alone, and that
   the passage then pays the debt, has audited it properly.
9. Both are present imperatives, both about a way of living rather than a
   single act (Lesson 26). In place of worrying, seeking: **ζητεῖτε** is
   what the passage puts where **μεριμνᾶτε** was.
10. *But seek first the kingdom [of God] and his justice, and all these
    things will be added to you.* — **πρῶτον** adverb; **αὐτοῦ** *his*,
    which is why the editors suspect **τοῦ θεοῦ** was added to make the
    *his* clear; **ταῦτα πάντα** neuter plural subject with a singular
    verb, Lesson 7's rule.
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. A promise is a sentence in the future tense. Does writing it down make
   it truer, or only checkable — and is checkable worth anything on its
   own?
2. The future is the one tense where Greek asks *when* and not *what
   shape*. Why might a language that cares about aspect everywhere else
   give it up exactly there?
3. **εἶμι**: in Attic, to say *I will go* is to say *I am on my way*. Does
   that make an Athenian's promise more binding than a Roman's *ībō*, or
   only differently worded?
:::

Marcus stayed behind.

"The tablet on the shelf. When you read it in July — if I have done the
things, do the four wrong sentences count?"

"Which four?"

"**ἔσομαι** was right. **μαθήσομαι** was right. You said so. The present
and the Koine and —" he counted — "two. Two wrong."

"Two wrong. You have been recounting them all afternoon, and you have the
number now." Theodoros took the tablet down and looked at it. "The
sentences will be read as written. **μένω ἐν τῷ διδασκαλείῳ** will be
checked against whether you are, at that moment, in the schoolroom, and I
expect you will be, so it will be true, and it will also not be a promise,
and the class will see the difference. That is worth more than a mark."

"And **ἐλεύσομαι**?"

"Is a future, and will be checked as one, and I will note that it is
Felix's Greek and not yours, and that you borrowed it from the one person
in the room whose ear you have spent a year pretending not to trust." He
put the tablet back. "**εἶ.**"

Marcus hesitated. "*You are*? Or *you will go*?"

"Yes," said Theodoros, and went back to his scrolls.
