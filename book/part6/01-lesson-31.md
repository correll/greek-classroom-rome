# The Voice That Looks Back

::: story
Nobody saw how the cat got into the ink, and the cat was not saying.

What was certain was that it had, and that it had then walked the length
of the courtyard and up the step and across the first clean tablet of the
second term, which was Julia's, and that the household had decreed it
washed. Asked who should do the washing, Theodoros said that the pupil who
had most often claimed the cat might reasonably be given it, and so Felix,
who had labelled it in Lesson 3 and written to it in Lesson 28, was in the
courtyard with a basin, and the cat was in the courtyard with opinions.

The class was supposed to be writing. It was watching through the door.

Theodoros did not stop them. He wrote three sentences on the board and
waited for the noise from the courtyard to reach a natural pause.

> **ὁ Φῆλιξ λούει τὸν αἴλουρον.**
>
> **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
>
> **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται.**

"Which of these is true?"

"The first two," said Sabina, "and they are the same thing. Felix washes
the cat; the cat is washed by Felix. One event. You have written it from
both ends."

"And the third?"

"That one will be true in about an hour," said Livia, "when the cat has
finished removing the washing."

"The second and third are the same word," said Marcus. "**λούεται**,
twice. One means *is being washed* and the other means *washes itself*,
and the form does not care which."

"It does not. So what in the sentence tells you which?"

"**ὑπό**. *By Felix.* Take it away and there is nothing."

"Nothing in the *form*," said Theodoros. "Something in the world: you know
what cats do, and you know what Felix is doing, and you read the sentence
with both in your head. The ending **-εται** is honest about exactly one
thing — the cat is the subject and the washing is happening to it. Whether
the cat is doing it or having it done is a question the ending declines to
answer, and that is the voice you are learning today. It is called the
middle, and it looks back at its own subject."

The door opened. Felix came in wet to the elbows and did not sit down.

"Sir. In the street, when a man says he is off to the baths, he says
**λοῦμαι** — *I'm washing*. He does not mean someone is going to do it to
him."

"No. He means your third sentence, said of himself, with the vowels run
together the way the street runs them. Your ear is ahead of your grammar
again, which is the right order." Theodoros turned back to the board. "Now
— one word. What happened out there?"

"**ἐλούθη**," said Felix, with feeling. *It got washed.*

"And after that?"

Livia, from the back: "**ἐλούσατο.**" *It washed itself.*

"Two different endings. Listen to them. In the present the two voices
share a form and let **ὑπό** or the world decide. In the aorist they do
not share: **-θη-** is passive, **-σατο** is middle, and nobody can confuse
the cat being washed with the cat washing. That is the whole lesson. The
cat has done most of the teaching, and Felix the rest, and I will now do
the paradigms, which is the part nobody else wanted."

"The cat is not persuaded," said Felix, drying his hands.

"**ὁ αἴλουρος οὐ πείθεται.** Lesson 26: the middle of *persuade* is *obey*.
No. It is not."
:::

::: newgreek
## Three voices

Every verb you have met has had its subject *doing* the action. That is
the **active** voice, and until today it was the only one you could write.

Greek has two more. In the **passive**, the subject has the action done to
it: *the cat is washed*. In the **middle**, the subject does the action and
is caught up in it — does it to itself, or for itself, or with its own
interest in the result: *the cat washes* (itself). English has no middle;
it borrows a reflexive, *itself*, or does without.

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Active** | **λούει** | he washes (someone) |
| **Middle** | **λούεται** | he washes (himself) |
| **Passive** | **λούεται** | he is being washed |

The middle and the passive share their forms in the present and the
imperfect. Only the aorist keeps them apart. That is the shape of the
lesson, and it is why the paradigms come in two halves.

## The present and the imperfect: one set of endings, two voices

Here is **λύω** — *I loose* — which the course has used as its pattern verb
since Lesson 11, in its middle-and-passive form.

::: paradigm
| | Present | Imperfect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύομαι** | **ἐλυόμην** |
| 2 sing. | **λύῃ** | **ἐλύου** |
| 3 sing. | **λύεται** | **ἐλύετο** |
| 1 plur. | **λυόμεθα** | **ἐλυόμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **λύεσθε** | **ἐλύεσθε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύονται** | **ἐλύοντο** |
:::

**λύομαι** is *I loose myself* or *I am loosed*; **ἐλυόμην** is *I was
loosing myself* or *I was being loosed*. The imperfect takes the augment
exactly as it did in the active. Everything you learned in Lesson 13 about
the **ἐ-** still holds.

You have been reading these endings for seventeen lessons without a name
for them. **βούλεται**, **δύναται**, **ψεύδεται**, **γίγνονται**,
**ἔρχεται**, **ἀποκρίνομαι**, **εὔχομαι**, **ἀσπάζομαι** — every one is in
this table.

## The aorist: two sets, and they do not share

**The aorist middle** has the **-σα-** of Lesson 14 with a second set of
endings behind it:

::: paradigm
| | First aorist middle | Second aorist middle |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἐλυσάμην** | **ἐγενόμην** |
| 2 sing. | **ἐλύσω** | **ἐγένου** |
| 3 sing. | **ἐλύσατο** | **ἐγένετο** |
| 1 plur. | **ἐλυσάμεθα** | **ἐγενόμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **ἐλύσασθε** | **ἐγένεσθε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἐλύσαντο** | **ἐγένοντο** |
:::

**ἐγένετο**, which you took whole in Lesson 14 — *it came to be* — is the
third singular of a second aorist middle, built on the stem **γεν-** the
way **εἶπον** is built on **εἰπ-**. You may now stop taking it whole.

**The aorist passive** is a different animal. It has no **-σα-** at all.
Its mark is **-θη-**, and its endings are the *active* ones of Lesson 13:

::: paradigm
| | Aorist passive | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἐλύθην** | I was loosed |
| 2 sing. | **ἐλύθης** | |
| 3 sing. | **ἐλύθη** | |
| 1 plur. | **ἐλύθημεν** | |
| 2 plur. | **ἐλύθητε** | |
| 3 plur. | **ἐλύθησαν** | |
:::

Before the **θ**, a stem consonant shifts: **πέμπω → ἐπέμφθην**, **διδάσκω
→ ἐδιδάχθην**, **πείθω → ἐπείσθην**, **ἀκούω → ἠκούσθην**, **βαπτίζω →
ἐβαπτίσθην**. A few verbs drop the **θ** and keep only the **η** — a
*second* aorist passive, like a second aorist active: **γράφω →
ἐγράφην**, *I was written*. The dictionary tells you which.

So, for the cat:

| | | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **ἔλουσε** | he washed (it) | active |
| **ἐλούσατο** | it washed (itself) | middle |
| **ἐλούθη** | it was washed | passive |

Three endings, three voices, one afternoon.

## Who did it

The person a passive action is done *by* goes after **ὑπό** with the
genitive. **ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούθη ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος** — *the cat was washed by
Felix*. A *thing* it was done *with* goes in the plain dative, as the
dative of means always did: **ὕδατι**, *with water*.

**ὑπό** is the word that settles the cat. Without it, **λούεται** is middle
or passive and only the world can tell you; with it, the sentence has
named an agent, and an agent means a passive.

## The verbs that laid down their active

Some verbs have middle or passive forms and no active at all, and mean
something perfectly active by them. Grammarians call them **deponents** —
verbs that have *laid aside* one voice. You have met ten:

**βούλομαι, δύναμαι, ψεύδομαι, γίγνομαι, ἔρχομαι, ἀποκρίνομαι, εὔχομαι,
ἀσπάζομαι, σπλαγχνίζομαι, παρέρχομαι.**

There is nothing passive about *I want* or *I come*. The form is middle
because, to a Greek ear, wanting and coming and praying and greeting are
things the subject is *in*. Do not translate the voice; translate the verb.

In the aorist, some deponents go middle (**ἐγενόμην**, **ἐψευσάμην**,
**ηὐξάμην**, and in Attic **ἀπεκρινάμην**, *I answered*) and some go
passive (**ἐβουλήθην**, *I wanted*; the Gospels' **ἀπεκρίθην**, *I
answered*, is Koine). It is a fact about each verb, learned with the
verb, like a second aorist.

## For reading: the infinitive and the participle

The middle-and-passive infinitives are **λύεσθαι** (present), **λύσασθαι**
(aorist middle) and **λυθῆναι** (aorist passive); you had **ψεύδεσθαι** in
Lesson 16. The second aorist middle is **γενέσθαι**, *to become*.

The participles are **λυόμενος, -η, -ον** (present), **λυσάμενος** (aorist
middle) and **λυθείς, λυθεῖσα, λυθέν** (aorist passive). Mark's account of
the baptism has two of the present kind: **σχιζομένους**, *being torn
open*, and **πειραζόμενος**, *being tested*. They decline like adjectives
and agree like participles, exactly as in Lesson 22.

## What "middle" means

A Greek grammarian called this voice **μέση**, *the middle one*, because it
sits between doing and being done to. That is a fair name and a slightly
misleading one. The middle is not a compromise; it is a claim that the
subject is inside the action. **λούομαι** — the washing is mine, I am its
object and its agent. **πείθομαι** — I let myself be persuaded: I obey.
**εὔχομαι** — I pray, which is something one does *to* nobody but on one's
own account. The voice looks back at its subject and says: *this concerns
you*.

::: latinbridge
**Latin has a passive, and you have had it for years.** *Lavātur*, *he is
washed*; *lavābātur*, *he was being washed*; the agent with *ā* or *ab* and
the ablative, which is exactly **ὑπό** and the genitive. Everything in the
second sentence on the board is Latin you already know.

**Latin has deponents, and the name is Latin.** *Loquor*, *sequor*,
*morior*, *cōnor* — passive forms, active meanings, *dēpōnentia*, verbs
that have *laid down* their active. **βούλομαι** and **ἔρχομαι** are the
same beast. The grammarians of both languages noticed the same thing, but
only the Latin grammarians named it; we borrow their name for Greek.

**What Latin does not have is the middle**, and that is where Latin will
mislead you. *Lavātur* is usually passive (though *lavor* can mean *I
bathe*); a Roman who wanted to
say *the cat washes itself* said *sē lavat*, with a reflexive, as English
does. (The bath-house Latin *lavor*, *I am bathing*, is the nearest Latin
comes to a middle, and it has no name for it and no separate form.) So
when you meet **λούεται** without **ὑπό**, do not reach for *lavātur*. It
may be *sē lavat*. Only the sentence, or the cat, can tell you.
:::
:::

::: note
**λούω** in the mouths of Athenians often ran its vowels together —
**λοῦμαι**, **λοῦται**, as Felix heard in the street — and the tidy
**λούομαι** of the table is the grammarian's form. Both are Attic. You
will write the tidy one and should recognise the other.

**ὑπό** has other jobs with other cases — *under*, with the dative or the
accusative — which you will meet as they come. With the genitive and a
passive verb it means *by*, and that is the one to learn today.

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory, not from the book.

1. **τοῦ διδασκάλου** — name the case and say what it does.
2. **ἔγραψε** — imperfect or aorist, and what told you?
3. Translate **δεῖ τοὺς παῖδας μανθάνειν**.
4. **τίς** or **τις** — which one asks a question, and how do you know?
5. Turn **ὁ ἄνθρωπος βλέπει τὸν ἵππον** round so that the horse sees the
   man, without moving a word.
6. In **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος**, what does the article tell you?
7. **ὁ σοφὸς διδάσκαλος** and **σοφὸς ὁ διδάσκαλος** — which is a sentence?
8. Translate **ἰδὼν ἀντιπαρῆλθεν**, and name the aspect of the participle.
9. Give the aorist infinitive of **λέγω**.
10. **μὴ λέγε** or **οὐ λέγε** — which is Greek, and why?
:::

::: answers
1. Genitive singular; *of the teacher* — possession, or the *from* and
   *about* relations of Lesson 8.
2. Aorist — the **-ψ-** is **φ + σ**. The imperfect is **ἔγραφε**.
3. *The children must learn* — **δεῖ** + accusative + infinitive.
4. **τίς**, with the accent: the question. **τις**, enclitic, is *someone*.
5. **τὸν ἄνθρωπον βλέπει ὁ ἵππος.** Only the endings and the articles
   change.
6. That **ὁ λόγος** is the subject; **θεός** without the article is the
   predicate. *The Word was God.*
7. The second. Predicate position: *the teacher is wise*. The first is a
   noun phrase, *the wise teacher*, and says nothing yet.
8. *Having seen, he passed by on the other side.* Aorist: one complete
   step before the main verb.
9. **εἰπεῖν.**
10. **μὴ λέγε.** Commands are negated with **μή**; **οὐ** with an
    imperative is not Greek.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Which voice, and what tells you?"}
For each, say *active*, *middle*, *passive*, or *cannot tell from the
form*, and name the evidence.

1. **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
2. **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται.**
3. **ὁ Φῆλιξ λούει τὸν αἴλουρον.**
4. **ἐλούθη ὁ αἴλουρος.**
5. **ἐλούσατο ὁ αἴλουρος.**
6. **ἐγενόμην σοφός.**
7. **ἡ ἐπιστολὴ ἐγράφη ὑπὸ τῆς Ἰουλίας.**
8. **βούλονται μανθάνειν.**
9. **ἐπείσθησαν οἱ παῖδες.**
10. **ἐπέμφθη ὁ ἄγγελος πρὸς τὸν διδάσκαλον.**
:::

::: answers
1. Passive — **ὑπό** with the genitive names an agent. *The cat is being
   washed by Felix.*
2. Cannot tell from the form. **-εται** is middle or passive; there is no
   **ὑπό**. In the world, a cat on its own washes itself: middle. A pupil
   who says *I cannot tell yet* has read the ending correctly.
3. Active — **-ει**, and the cat is in the accusative.
4. Passive — **-θη-**. *The cat was washed.*
5. Middle — **-σατο**: the **-σα-** of the aorist with a middle ending.
   *The cat washed itself.*
6. Deponent: middle in form, active in sense. *I became wise.* There is no
   passive meaning to be had.
7. Passive — **ἐγράφη** is the second aorist passive of **γράφω** (no
   **θ**), and **ὑπό** names the agent. *The letter was written by Julia.*
8. Deponent — *they want to learn*.
9. Passive — **-θησαν**. *The children were persuaded* — which, by Lesson
   26, is *the children obeyed*. Both translations are right and they are
   one fact.
10. Passive — **-θη** with the consonant shift **π → φ**. *The messenger
    was sent to the teacher.*

2 is the item that matters. The rest can be answered from the ending;
2 cannot, and the correct answer is to say so.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
1. **ὁ Φῆλιξ ἔλουσε τὸν αἴλουρον· ὁ δὲ αἴλουρος οὐκ ἐβούλετο λούεσθαι.**
2. **ἐλούθη οὖν ὁ αἴλουρος ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος, καὶ εὐθὺς ἐλούσατο πάλιν.**
3. **ἡ ἐπιστολὴ ἐγράφη ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος καὶ ἐπέμφθη πρὸς τὸν αἴλουρον.**
4. **οἱ παῖδες ἐδιδάσκοντο ὑπὸ τοῦ Θεοδώρου καὶ ἐμάνθανον.**
5. **φωνὴ ἐγένετο ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ· οἱ δὲ ἄνθρωποι οὐκ ἤκουσαν.**
6. **ὁ ἄγγελος ἐπέμφθη ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ πρὸς τοὺς ἀνθρώπους.**
7. **ἐπειράζετο ὁ Μᾶρκος ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος, ἀλλ᾽ οὐκ ἐπείσθη.**
8. **ἐγενόμεθα μάρτυρες τοῦ ἔργου.**
9. **ὁ κύριος ἐβαπτίσθη ἐν τῷ ὕδατι ὑπὸ τοῦ Ἰωάννου.** *(**Ἰωάννου** = of
   John)*
10. **τὸ ὕδωρ ἐφέρετο εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν ὑπὸ τοῦ παιδαγωγοῦ.**
:::

::: answers
1. *Felix washed the cat; but the cat did not want to be washed.* —
   **ἔλουσε** aorist active; **λούεσθαι** present passive infinitive after
   **ἐβούλετο**, which is the imperfect of a deponent.
2. *So the cat was washed by Felix, and at once washed itself again.* —
   **ἐλούθη** passive, **ἐλούσατο** middle, in one sentence, which is the
   lesson.
3. *The letter was written by Felix and sent to the cat.* — Lesson 28,
   in the passive. Two aorist passives: a second (**ἐγράφη**) and a first
   (**ἐπέμφθη**).
4. *The children were being taught by Theodoros and were learning.* —
   imperfect passive beside imperfect active; both unfinished.
5. *A voice came from the sky; but the people did not hear.* — **φωνή**
   without the article: *a* voice. **ἐγένετο** deponent.
6. *The messenger was sent by God to the people.* Accept *angel*; the
   word is the same and the class should say so.
7. *Marcus was being tempted by Felix, but was not persuaded.* — or *did
   not obey*; imperfect passive for the attempt, aorist passive for the
   result. The sentence is about the cat, at one remove.
8. *We became witnesses of the deed.* — **μάρτυρες** nominative plural,
   agreeing with the subject across **ἐγενόμεθα** as across **εἰμί**.
9. *The Lord was baptised in the water by John.* — Mark 1:9, nearly; the
   Reading has the original.
10. *The water was being carried into the house by the paedagogus.* —
    the paedagogus's whole job, in the imperfect passive.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Turn it round"}
Make each active sentence passive, keeping the tense and naming the agent
with **ὑπό**.

1. **ὁ Φῆλιξ λούει τὸν αἴλουρον.**
2. **ὁ Φῆλιξ ἔλουσε τὸν αἴλουρον.**
3. **ὁ διδάσκαλος διδάσκει τοὺς παῖδας.**
4. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἐδίδασκε τοὺς παῖδας.**
5. **ἡ Ἰουλία ἔγραψε τὴν ἐπιστολήν.**
6. **ὁ πατὴρ ἔπεμψε τὸν ἄγγελον.**
7. **ὁ Ἰωάννης ἐβάπτισε τὸν Ἰησοῦν.** *(**Ἰησοῦν** — accusative of
   **Ἰησοῦς**, Jesus)*
8. **τὸ πνεῦμα ἐκβάλλει αὐτόν.**
9. **ὁ Μᾶρκος πείθει τὴν Σαβῖναν.**
10. **οἱ ἄνθρωποι ἤκουσαν τὴν φωνήν.**
:::

::: answers
1. **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
2. **ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούθη ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
3. **οἱ παῖδες διδάσκονται ὑπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
4. **οἱ παῖδες ἐδιδάσκοντο ὑπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
5. **ἡ ἐπιστολὴ ἐγράφη ὑπὸ τῆς Ἰουλίας.** — second aorist passive; a
   pupil who writes **ἐγράφθη** has applied the rule to the one verb in the
   list that breaks it, and should be told the rule was right and the verb
   is odd.
6. **ὁ ἄγγελος ἐπέμφθη ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός.**
7. **ὁ Ἰησοῦς ἐβαπτίσθη ὑπὸ τοῦ Ἰωάννου.**
8. **ἐκβάλλεται ὑπὸ τοῦ πνεύματος.** — no pronoun; the ending carries
   him. **αὐτός** in the nominative would mean *he himself*.
9. **ἡ Σαβῖνα πείθεται ὑπὸ τοῦ Μάρκου.** — *Sabina is persuaded by
   Marcus*, and also *Sabina obeys Marcus*, which is a different claim
   about Sabina and is why the sentence was set.
10. **ἡ φωνὴ ἠκούσθη ὑπὸ τῶν ἀνθρώπων.** — the augment lengthens **ἀ-** to
    **ἠ-** as in the imperfect.

In every item the object became the subject, the subject went after
**ὑπό**, and the verb changed its ending and nothing else. Word order was
free before and is free now.
:::

1. **ὁ αἴλουρος λούεται ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**

   and the meaning is different, and nothing in the verb says so.
3. **ὁ Φῆλιξ λούει τὸν αἴλουρον.**
4. **ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούθη ὑπὸ τοῦ Φήλικος.**
5. **ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούσατο πάλιν.** — middle. Watch for **ἐλούθη πάλιν**,
   which says somebody washed it twice, and is not what the cat did.
6. **βούλομαι σοφὸς γενέσθαι.** — the aorist infinitive of a deponent;
   **σοφός** nominative, agreeing with the subject of **βούλομαι**.
7. **ἡ ἐπιστολὴ ἐγράφη ὑπὸ τῆς Ἰουλίας.**
8. **οἱ παῖδες ἐδιδάσκοντο ὑπὸ τοῦ διδασκάλου.**
9. **φωνὴ ἐγένετο ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ.** — no article on **φωνή**.
10. **ἐπειράζετο ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ τετταράκοντα ἡμέρας.** — imperfect passive
    for forty days of it. **τῇ ἐρήμῳ** is feminine; a pupil who writes
    **τῷ ἐρήμῳ** has trusted the **-ος**.

1, 2 and 3 are the board. A pupil who produces three different verb forms
has not understood that 1 and 2 share one; a pupil who produces the same
form for 1 and 2 and can say why has understood the lesson.

::: {.voice source="Mark 1:9"}
One verse, and two of this afternoon's three voices in it. You read it in
Lesson 23 beside Xenophon, for the Greek it was written in. Read it now
for the endings.

**Καὶ ἐγένετο ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις ἦλθεν Ἰησοῦς ἀπὸ Ναζαρὲτ τῆς
Γαλιλαίας καὶ ἐβαπτίσθη εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου.**

- **καὶ ἐγένετο** — and it happened *(aorist middle, deponent; a Hebrew
  way of opening a story, carried into Greek)*
- **ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις** — in those days
- **ἦλθεν** — came *(aorist of **ἔρχομαι**)*
- **Ἰησοῦς** — Jesus *(no article: Mark introduces him)*
- **ἀπὸ Ναζαρὲτ τῆς Γαλιλαίας** — from Nazareth of Galilee
- **ἐβαπτίσθη** — was baptised *(aorist passive, third singular)*
- **εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην** — into the Jordan
- **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου** — by John *(**ὑπό** with the genitive: the agent)*

*And it happened in those days that Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee and
was baptised into the Jordan by John.*

**Three verbs, three different things.** **ἐγένετο** is a deponent: middle
in form, and the meaning is simply *happened*. **ἦλθεν** is active: he
came, he did the coming. **ἐβαπτίσθη** is passive — the **-θη-** says so —
and **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου** names the one who did it. Mark could have written
**Ἰωάννης ἐβάπτισεν αὐτόν**, *John baptised him*; the event is the same.
He wrote it from the other end, and the sentence is about Jesus from its
first verb to its last, and John is in the genitive at the back of it.

**And one preposition doing something Attic would not.** **εἰς τὸν
Ἰορδάνην**, *into the Jordan*, where an Attic writer would say **ἐν**: Koine
is reaching for the motion in the verb — **βαπτίζω** is *dip*, and one dips
*into*. Felix should be asked whether that is a mistake or a meaning.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** Mark 1:9 is quoted from NA28; check it against
the edition in use. Westcott–Hort reads identically here.

**On the cat and the Jordan.** The grammar of the two is shared; the events
are not, and a class that has spent twenty minutes on a wet cat will be
tempted to run them together. Do not let it. Theodoros's rule — scripture
is read with reverence, and the pupils may not turn it into a joke — is the
rule here, and Theodosius will be watching to see whether you keep it. The
right move is the one the text makes: the *voice* of **ἐβαπτίσθη** is what
the cat taught, and the cat's lesson ends at the ending. If a pupil makes
the joke anyway, ask them what **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου** is doing, and move on.

**On Felix.** This is his lesson. His ear caught **λοῦμαι** in the street
before his grammar could name the middle, and the course has been saying
since Part IV that his ear is a talent. Let him be right first and taught
second; that is the order the story keeps.

**On ἐγένετο.** Some pupils will have been waiting since Lesson 14 to find
out what it was. Tell them, and let them read the paradigm it comes from;
it is the first time the course has repaid a *take it whole* in full, and
there will be more.
:::
:::

::: {.reading title="The baptism and the desert" source="Mark 1:9–13 (adapted, then NA28)"}
**First, adapted.** The forms are the ones you have, Attic where the
original is Koine, and the baptism is told twice, once in each voice, so
that you can see both. What is glossed is new or not yet yours.

ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις ἦλθεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς ἀπὸ τῆς Ναζαρὲτ τῆς Γαλιλαίας
πρὸς τὸν Ἰωάννην. καὶ ὁ Ἰωάννης ἐβάπτισεν αὐτὸν ἐν τῷ Ἰορδάνῃ· ὁ οὖν
Ἰησοῦς ἐβαπτίσθη ὑπὸ τοῦ Ἰωάννου ἐν τῷ ὕδατι.

καὶ εὐθὺς ἀνέβη ἐκ τοῦ ὕδατος καὶ εἶδε τοὺς οὐρανούς· οἱ γὰρ οὐρανοὶ
ἐσχίζοντο, καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα κατέβαινεν ὡς περιστερὰ εἰς αὐτόν. καὶ φωνὴ
ἐγένετο ἐκ τῶν οὐρανῶν· σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός· ἐν σοὶ εὐδόκησα.

καὶ εὐθὺς τὸ πνεῦμα ἐξέβαλεν αὐτὸν εἰς τὴν ἔρημον. καὶ ἦν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ
τετταράκοντα ἡμέρας, καὶ ἐπειράζετο ὑπὸ τοῦ σατανᾶ· καὶ ἦν μετὰ τῶν
θηρίων, καὶ οἱ ἄγγελοι ἐδιακόνουν αὐτῷ.

**Then Mark's own words.** Koine, unaltered. Four participles where the
adaptation had clauses, **εἰς** where it had **ἐν**, and one present tense
in the middle of a story in the past.

Καὶ ἐγένετο ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις ἦλθεν Ἰησοῦς ἀπὸ Ναζαρὲτ τῆς
Γαλιλαίας καὶ ἐβαπτίσθη εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου. καὶ εὐθὺς ἀναβαίνων
ἐκ τοῦ ὕδατος εἶδεν σχιζομένους τοὺς οὐρανοὺς καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα ὡς περιστερὰν
καταβαῖνον εἰς αὐτόν· καὶ φωνὴ ἐγένετο ἐκ τῶν οὐρανῶν· σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ὁ
ἀγαπητός, ἐν σοὶ εὐδόκησα.

Καὶ εὐθὺς τὸ πνεῦμα αὐτὸν ἐκβάλλει εἰς τὴν ἔρημον. καὶ ἦν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ
τεσσεράκοντα ἡμέρας πειραζόμενος ὑπὸ τοῦ σατανᾶ, καὶ ἦν μετὰ τῶν θηρίων,
καὶ οἱ ἄγγελοι διηκόνουν αὐτῷ.

::: gloss
- **ἐκείναις** — those *(dative plural, with **ταῖς ἡμέραις**)*
- **ὁ Ἰησοῦς** — Jesus *(genitive and dative **Ἰησοῦ**, accusative
  **Ἰησοῦν**)*
- **ἡ Ναζαρέτ, ἡ Γαλιλαία** — Nazareth, Galilee
- **ὁ Ἰωάννης** — John *(genitive **Ἰωάννου**)*
- **ὁ Ἰορδάνης** — the Jordan *(a river)*
- **ἀνέβη** — went up *(aorist of **ἀναβαίνω**; take it whole)*
- **ἀναβαίνων** — going up *(present participle)*
- **εἶδε(ν)** — saw *(aorist of **ὁράω**)*
- **σχίζω** — I split, I tear open *(**ἐσχίζοντο** were being torn open;
  **σχιζομένους** being torn open, accusative plural with **τοὺς
  οὐρανούς**)*
- **καταβαίνω** — I come down *(**κατέβαινεν** was coming down;
  **καταβαῖνον** coming down, neuter, with **τὸ πνεῦμα**)*
- **μου** — my *(genitive of **ἐγώ**, enclitic)*
- **σοί** — you *(dative of **σύ**)*
- **εὐδόκησα** — I am well pleased *(aorist; take it whole)*
- **τετταράκοντα** — forty *(Attic; Mark's Koine spells it
  **τεσσεράκοντα**)*
- **ὁ σατανᾶς** — Satan, the adversary *(genitive **σατανᾶ**)*
- **πειραζόμενος** — being tested *(present passive participle)*
- **τὸ θηρίον** — wild animal
- **ἐδιακόνουν, διηκόνουν** — were serving, were looking after *(imperfect
  of **διακονέω**, with the dative; take it whole)*
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. In the adapted tier, the baptism is told twice. Give the two verbs and
   name the voice of each.
2. Mark tells it once. Which voice did he choose, and what word names the
   agent?
3. **ἀνέβη** in the adaptation becomes **ἀναβαίνων** in Mark. What kind of
   word is each, and what does the change do to the sentence?
4. **ἐσχίζοντο** — give the tense and voice. Who or what is the subject?
5. Find the deponent verb that introduces the voice from the sky in each
   tier. What does
   it mean, and why is its form middle?
6. **ἐκβάλλει** in Mark is present tense in a story told in the past.
   What is the adaptation's verb, and which is the stranger choice?
7. **ἐπειράζετο** and **πειραζόμενος** say the same thing in two shapes.
   Which is a finite verb and which a participle? What does each agree
   with or depend on?
8. **ὑπό** appears twice in Mark. Give both phrases and say what each is
   the agent of.
9. **ἦν … ἦν … διηκόνουν**: three imperfects in a row. What do they say
   about the forty days?
10. Translate the last sentence of Mark's tier.
:::

::: answers
1. **ἐβάπτισεν** — active, John the subject; **ἐβαπτίσθη** — passive,
   Jesus the subject. One event.
2. Passive, **ἐβαπτίσθη**; the agent is **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου**.
3. **ἀνέβη** is a finite aorist, *he went up*; **ἀναβαίνων** is a present
   participle, *going up*. Mark's sentence has one main verb, **εἶδεν**,
   and the going-up hangs from it as the circumstance: *as he came up, he
   saw*. The adaptation gave each action its own clause.
4. Imperfect passive, third plural; the subject is **οἱ οὐρανοί**, *the
   skies were being torn open*. In Mark the same thing is a participle,
   **σχιζομένους**, agreeing with **τοὺς οὐρανούς** in the accusative,
   because the skies are what he *saw*.
5. **ἐγένετο** — *there came, there was* (a voice). Deponent: the form is
   middle, the sense is simply that it happened. Lesson 14's word, with
   its paradigm now behind it.
6. The adaptation has **ἐξέβαλεν**, aorist. Mark's present, **ἐκβάλλει**,
   is the stranger: it is the storyteller's present — *and at once the
   Spirit drives him out* — and it makes the listener watch it happen.
   Mark does this constantly; Attic historians do it too, more sparingly.
   It is not a mistake about time. It is a choice about distance.
7. **ἐπειράζετο** is a finite verb, imperfect passive, with **ἦν**'s
   subject as its own; **πειραζόμενος** is a participle, nominative
   masculine singular, agreeing with that subject and depending on **ἦν**:
   *he was in the desert … being tested*.
8. **ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου**, agent of **ἐβαπτίσθη**; **ὑπὸ τοῦ σατανᾶ**, agent of
   **πειραζόμενος**. Both genitive; both with a passive form.
9. That they were a state, not an event: he *was* there, he *was* with
   the animals, the angels *kept* serving him. Nothing in the forty days is
   reported as a single completed thing; Lesson 13's tense, doing Lesson
   13's job.
10. *And he was in the desert forty days, being tested by Satan, and he was
    with the wild animals, and the angels were serving him.* —
    **τεσσεράκοντα ἡμέρας** accusative for the stretch of time; **αὐτῷ**
    dative after **διηκόνουν**.
:::
:::

::: question
1. The middle says that the subject is inside its own action. English has
   no voice for this. Does English lose something, or does it say the same
   thing another way — and if the latter, why did Greek bother?
2. **πείθομαι**: to obey is to have been persuaded. Is that a fact about
   Greek, or a claim about obedience? Could a language make that claim
   without anyone noticing?
3. **λούεται** means *is washed* or *washes itself*, and only **ὑπό** or
   the world decides. When a language lets two meanings share one form,
   who carries the burden — the writer, the reader, or the cat?
:::

::: story
At the end of the afternoon the cat was clean, dry, on the wall, and
washing itself, and Felix had written three sentences on his tablet
without being asked.

**ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούθη ὑπ᾽ ἐμοῦ.**

**ὁ αἴλουρος ἐλούσατο.**

**ὁ αἴλουρος οὐ πείθεται.**

"The first is passive," said Theodoros, reading over his shoulder. "The
second is middle. The third is a deponent, and it is also the truest
sentence anyone has written this term. **ὑπ᾽ ἐμοῦ** — *by me* — you have
put yourself in the genitive, at the end, after the cat. That is where the
agent goes."

"I know, sir."

"I know you know. I am saying that you chose it." He handed the tablet
back. "Julia's tablet is still ruined, by the way. Somebody will have to
copy it out."

Felix looked at the wall. The cat looked at the courtyard. Neither of them
volunteered.
