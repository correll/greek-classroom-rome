# What Might Be

::: {.story time="5 min"}
"Two lessons remain," said Theodoros. "This one and one more. I have not
decided what the last will contain, and since you have opinions about
everything else you may as well have one about that."

Quintus had his hand up before the sentence ended. He had been waiting all
year for a question with philosophy in it, and this was one.

"**τί ποιοῦμεν;**" he said, in his best Greek.

Theodoros looked at him with interest.

"You are sitting on a bench," he said. "Marcus is sitting on a bench. Felix
is looking at the cat. Livia is looking at you. That is what we are doing,
and you asked, and I have answered, and I do not think it is what you
wanted to know."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is exactly what you asked. **ποιοῦμεν** is the indicative. The
indicative *reports*. You asked what we are doing, and I reported. What you
wanted to ask is what we are *to* do — what might be done, what should be
done, what is open — and you cannot ask that in any mood you own. Every
finite verb you have learned this year states a fact or gives an order.
You wanted a third thing."

Livia had the Luke open. She had been looking for something since Quintus
spoke.

"The crowds ask John the Baptist a question," she said. "Three times, in
one chapter. **τί οὖν ποιήσωμεν;** I thought it was a mistake in the copy.
It has an omega where the indicative would have an omicron, and no augment,
and it is not asking what they are doing. It is asking what they are to
do." She turned the scroll round. "I think it is the mood he means. I think
Quintus wants to say **τί ποιῶμεν;** — with the long vowel."

Quintus said it. One vowel longer.

"Now you have asked," said Theodoros. "And I shall not answer until you can
read every form of that mood in the third chapter of John, because that is
where it lives, and because the question of what we are to do with the last
lesson is, as it happens, the question that chapter is about."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## A mood for what is not yet

The **indicative** states what is. The **imperative** orders what is to be
done. The **subjunctive** is the mood of what *might* be — what is
intended, feared, hoped, supposed, open. English has almost lost it (*if I
were*, *lest he fall*, *be that as it may* are its fossils) and does the
work with *may*, *might*, *should*, *let*. Greek has a full set of forms,
and they are the easiest forms in the language, because they are the
indicative with its vowel lengthened.

**ο becomes ω; ε becomes η; an iota stays, underneath.** That is the entire
rule. The present subjunctive of **λύω**:

| | active | middle and passive |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύω** | **λύωμαι** |
| 2 sing. | **λύῃς** | **λύῃ** |
| 3 sing. | **λύῃ** | **λύηται** |
| 1 plur. | **λύωμεν** | **λυώμεθα** |
| 2 plur. | **λύητε** | **λύησθε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύωσι(ν)** | **λύωνται** |

Set it beside **λύω, λύεις, λύει, λύομεν, λύετε, λύουσι** and every
difference is a vowel grown longer. The first singular, **λύω**, is
identical in both moods; the sentence decides.

## The aorist subjunctive

The aorist subjunctive has **no augment**. The augment is a mark of past
time (Lesson 13), and the subjunctive has no time in it — only aspect, as
the infinitive had none in Lesson 14 and the imperative none in Lesson 26.
Take the aorist stem, drop the augment, add the long vowels.

| | first aorist | second aorist | aorist middle | aorist passive |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **λύσω** | **εἴπω** | **λύσωμαι** | **λυθῶ** |
| 2 sing. | **λύσῃς** | **εἴπῃς** | **λύσῃ** | **λυθῇς** |
| 3 sing. | **λύσῃ** | **εἴπῃ** | **λύσηται** | **λυθῇ** |
| 1 plur. | **λύσωμεν** | **εἴπωμεν** | **λυσώμεθα** | **λυθῶμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **λύσητε** | **εἴπητε** | **λύσησθε** | **λυθῆτε** |
| 3 plur. | **λύσωσι(ν)** | **εἴπωσι(ν)** | **λύσωνται** | **λυθῶσι(ν)** |

So **ἔλθω, ἴδω, μάθω, λάβω, γένωμαι** are the aorist subjunctives of
**ἔρχομαι, ὁράω, μανθάνω, λαμβάνω, γίγνομαι** — the stems you learned as
pairs in Lesson 14 and Lesson 29, with the augment gone and the vowel long.
The aorist passive takes a circumflex throughout, because **-θέ-ω** melted
into **-θῶ** by last week's rules.

The difference between **λύω** and **λύσω** in the subjunctive is not time.
It is the difference of Lesson 14: **λύω** is the activity, **λύσω** the
single deed. **ἵνα μανθάνω** is *so that I may be learning*; **ἵνα μάθω** is
*so that I may learn* — once, and have it.

::: note
**Two small tables that complete the set.** **εἰμί**: **ὦ, ᾖς, ᾖ, ὦμεν,
ἦτε, ὦσι(ν)** — the endings alone, with nothing in front of them. And the
contract verbs melt as they did last week: **ποιῶ, ποιῇς, ποιῇ, ποιῶμεν,
ποιῆτε, ποιῶσι(ν)**; **δηλῶ, δηλοῖς, δηλοῖ, δηλῶμεν, δηλῶτε, δηλῶσι(ν)**.
For **-άω** verbs, **α** swallows the long vowel exactly as it swallowed the
short one, so **ἀγαπῶ, ἀγαπᾷς, ἀγαπᾷ, ἀγαπῶμεν, ἀγαπᾶτε, ἀγαπῶσι(ν)** is
*both* the indicative and the subjunctive. Only the sentence tells you
which, and now you know what to look for in it.
:::

## What the mood is for

Six jobs, and you have met examples of five of them already without the
name.

**One. Purpose — ἵνα, ὅπως.** *In order that.* **ἀκούω ἵνα μάθω** — *I
listen so that I may learn.* Negative **ἵνα μή**. This is the **ἵνα** of
Lesson 23, and the form after it is the one you were told to wait for.

**Two. Condition — ἐάν.** **ἐάν** is **εἰ** + **ἄν**, *if* with a particle
that opens the door to the subjunctive. **ἐὰν λέγῃς, ἀκούω** — *if ever you
speak, I listen*; **ἐὰν εἴπῃς, ἀκούσομαι** — *if you say it, I shall
listen.* The same particle makes **ὅταν** (*whenever*, **ὅτε** + **ἄν**)
and **ὃς ἄν** (*whoever*), and all of them take the subjunctive.

**Three. Exhortation — the first person plural.** **μάθωμεν** — *let us
learn.* **μὴ ψευδώμεθα** — *let us not lie.* Greek has no imperative in the
first person; the subjunctive does its work.

**Four. Deliberation — the question with no fact in it.** **τί ποιῶμεν;** —
*what are we to do?* **τί εἴπω;** — *what am I to say?* Quintus's question.
It asks not what *is* but what *should be*, and the indicative cannot ask
it.

**Five. Prohibition — μή with the aorist.** **μὴ εἴπῃς** — *don't say it*
(this once). Lesson 26 promised you this form: **μή** + present imperative
forbids a habit, **μή** + aorist subjunctive forbids a single act. **μὴ
θαυμάσῃς** — *do not be surprised* — in this week's Reading.

**Six. Emphatic denial — οὐ μή with the aorist.** Two negatives, and they
do not cancel: **οὐ μὴ εἴπω** is *I will certainly not say it*; **οὐ μὴ
ἀπόληται** is *he will by no means perish*. It is the strongest *no* Greek
has about the future.

The negative of the subjunctive is **μή**, always, in every job but the
last — and there **μή** is present too.

## Conditions, in one table

| | the *if*-clause | the main clause | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Simple** | **εἰ** + indicative | indicative | **εἰ λέγεις, ἀκούω.** *If you are speaking, I am listening.* |
| **Future, vivid** | **ἐάν** + subjunctive | future | **ἐὰν εἴπῃς, ἀκούσομαι.** *If you say it, I shall listen.* |
| **General** | **ἐάν** + subjunctive | present | **ἐὰν λέγῃς, ἀκούω.** *If ever you speak, I listen.* |
| **Contrary to fact, now** | **εἰ** + imperfect | imperfect + **ἄν** | **εἰ ἔλεγες, ἤκουον ἄν.** *If you were speaking, I would be listening.* |
| **Contrary to fact, then** | **εἰ** + aorist | aorist + **ἄν** | **εἰ εἶπες, ἤκουσα ἄν.** *If you had said it, I would have listened.* |
| **Remote** | **εἰ** + optative | optative + **ἄν** | *(a mood this book leaves for another year: **εἰ λέγοις, ἀκούοιμι ἄν**, "if you were to speak, I would listen")* |

Read the table downward and it is a scale of confidence. The top row takes
no view; the second expects; the third generalises; the fourth and fifth
deny; the sixth supposes faintly. The *if*-clause negative is **μή**, the
main clause's is **οὐ**. And **ἄν** has two jobs: with the subjunctive it
opens the door (**ἐάν**, **ὅταν**); with a past indicative it marks the
*would* of a thing that did not happen.

::: latinbridge
**Latin has a subjunctive, and here it both helps and misleads.** It
helps with the jobs. *Ut discam* is **ἵνα μάθω**; *discāmus* is **μάθωμεν**;
*quid faciāmus?* is **τί ποιῶμεν;** — Quintus could have asked his question
in Latin and been understood. *Nē dīxerīs*, the Latin *don't say it*, uses a
perfect subjunctive where Greek uses the aorist, and for the same reason:
a single act, not a habit.

It misleads in two ways. First, *ut* means *as* and *when* as well as *in
order that*, and you have spent years deciding which; **ἵνα** is purpose
and nothing else in Attic, and in Koine only stretches to *that*. Second —
and this is the one place in the course where Latin is the more complicated
language — the Latin subjunctive has four tenses and a sequence of tenses
to govern them. The Greek has none. **ἵνα μάθω** follows a present verb and
a past one alike; Greek asks the mood only about aspect, never about time,
which is why it has no augment and why Marcus's column of things Latin
cannot do does not need an entry today. It gets one anyway: Latin has no
**οὐ μή**.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **ἐάν** — if *(with the subjunctive; **εἰ** + **ἄν**)*
- **ἄν** — *(a particle: with the subjunctive, opens a general or future
  clause; with a past indicative, marks "would have")*
- **ὅταν** — whenever, when *(with the subjunctive; **ὅτε** + **ἄν**)*
- **ὥστε** — so that, with the result that
- **οὐδείς, οὐδεμία, οὐδέν** — no one, nothing *(**μηδείς** with the **μή**
  family)*
- **ἀμήν** — truly *(Hebrew; the Gospels' way of beginning a solemn
  statement)*
- **γεννάω** — I beget, I bear *(passive **γεννάομαι**, I am born; aorist
  passive **ἐγεννήθην**)*
- **ἄνωθεν** — from above; again *(both at once, in John)*
- **τὸ σημεῖον** — sign
- **ἡ νύξ** — night *(genitive **νυκτός**; **νυκτός** alone = by night)*
- **ἡ σάρξ** — flesh *(genitive **σαρκός**)*
- **τὸ ὕδωρ** — water *(genitive **ὕδατος**)*
- **ἡ φωνή** — voice, sound
- **λαμβάνω** — I take, I receive *(aorist **ἔλαβον**; Lesson 29's table)*
- **αἰώνιος** — eternal, everlasting *(the adjective of **ὁ αἰών**, Lesson
  20)*

::: note
**ἄνωθεν** is the word the whole Reading turns on. It means *from above*
and it means *again*, and Nicodemus hears the second where the speaker
means the first — or both. No Latin or English word does the same, which is
why *born again* and *born from above* are two translations of one Greek
word and both lose half of it. Lesson 27's dilemma, once more.

**οὐδείς** is **οὐδὲ εἷς**, *not even one*; it declines like **εἷς**, *one*
(**οὐδενός**, **οὐδενί**, **οὐδένα**). You met its feminine in Lesson 30:
**σκοτία οὐκ ἔστιν οὐδεμία**.
:::

1. Subjunctive — the **ῃ** where the indicative has **ει**. (Also 2 sing.

2. Indicative — short **ει**.
3. Subjunctive — aorist stem **γραψ-** with **no augment** and long **ω**.
4. Indicative — the augment **ἐ-** and short **α**. *We wrote.*

6. Indicative — augment (**α** lengthened to **η**). *You heard.*

8. Indicative, imperfect of **εἰμί** (Lesson 13).
9. Subjunctive — **ποιέ-ωμεν**; the indicative is **ποιοῦμεν**. Quintus's

10. Either: aorist subjunctive 1 sing. (*that I may loose*), or future

::: answers
1. *We listen in order to learn.* — purpose; aorist: so as to have learned.
2. *If ever the teacher speaks, the children listen.* — general condition;
   present subjunctive for the repeated case.
3. *If you do not write the letter, we shall not send it.* — future vivid;
   **μή** in the *if*-clause, **οὐ** in the main.
4. *Do not tell the judge this.* — prohibition, aorist: this once.
5. *Let us write to the teacher.* — exhortation.
6. *What am I to say? I do not know.* — deliberation; **εἴπω**, no augment.
7. *You will certainly not see the cat; for it is in the house.* — emphatic
   denial; **ἴδῃς**, aorist subjunctive of **ὁράω**.
8. *Whenever the paedagogus comes, we shall go into the house.* — **ὅταν** +
   subjunctive; **ἴμεν**, the present of **εἶμι** with future sense (Lesson
   32).
9. *Unless someone is born from above, he cannot see the kingdom of God.* —
   **ἐὰν μή** = *unless*; **γεννηθῇ**, aorist passive subjunctive; **τις**
   enclitic, so **μή** takes the acute. A pupil who writes *born again* has
   chosen one half of **ἄνωθεν**; ask what the other half was.
10. *If you had listened, you would have learned.* — contrary to fact in
    the past: both verbs aorist indicative, **ἄν** in the main clause. No
    subjunctive here; the item is in the exercise to see who notices.
:::

One sentence each.

1. Let us learn.
2. Do not say that. *(once)*
3. What am I to write?
4. I am listening in order to learn.
5. If you come, I shall write.
6. If anyone speaks the truth, the judge listens.
7. Whenever the teacher speaks, listen! *(to several)*
8. He will certainly not persuade the judge.
9. He came so that the children might learn.
10. If you had written, I would have answered.

::: answers
1. **μάθωμεν.** — aorist, as a resolve usually is. **μανθάνωμεν**, *let us
   be learning*, is correct and means something slightly different.
2. **μὴ εἴπῃς τοῦτο.** — not **μὴ εἰπέ**: Greek does not use **μή** with the
   aorist imperative. **μὴ λέγε τοῦτο** is *stop saying that*, a different
   request.
3. **τί γράψω;** — and, as the class now knows, also *what shall I write?*
4. **ἀκούω ἵνα μάθω.** — or **μανθάνω**; ask which the pupil meant.
5. **ἐὰν ἔλθῃς, γράψω.** — subjunctive after **ἐάν**, future in the main
   clause. **εἰ ἔλθῃς** is not Greek: **εἰ** does not take the subjunctive.
6. **ἐάν τις λέγῃ τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ὁ κριτὴς ἀκούει.** — general; present both
   sides.
7. **ὅταν ὁ διδάσκαλος λέγῃ, ἀκούετε.** — **ἀκούετε** is indicative and
   imperative alike (Lesson 26); the sentence makes it a command.
8. **οὐ μὴ πείσῃ τὸν κριτήν.** — **πείσῃ**, aorist subjunctive of **πείθω**.
9. **ἦλθεν ἵνα οἱ παῖδες μάθωσιν.** — after a past verb strict Attic prose
   would often shift to the optative, **μάθοιεν**; the subjunctive is also
   Attic, and is the only form the Gospels use. Accept it and say why.
10. **εἰ ἔγραψας, ἀπεκρινάμην ἄν.** — two aorists and **ἄν**. **ἀπεκρινάμην**
    is the Attic aorist middle (Lesson 31); the Gospels say **ἀπεκρίθην**.

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="John 3:16"}
The verse Theodosius knows best in Latin, and the one that uses most of
this lesson in a single sentence.

**Οὕτως γὰρ ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον, ὥστε τὸν υἱὸν τὸν μονογενῆ ἔδωκεν,
ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτὸν μὴ ἀπόληται ἀλλ᾽ ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον.**

- **οὕτως γάρ** — for thus, for so *(**γάρ** second, as always)*
- **ἠγάπησεν** — loved *(aorist of **ἀγαπάω**: lengthened **η**, no
  melting — Lesson 34's rule)*
- **τὸν κόσμον** — the world *(Lesson 27: order, ornament, world)*
- **ὥστε … ἔδωκεν** — so that he gave *(**ὥστε** with the indicative: a
  result that actually happened)*
- **τὸν υἱὸν τὸν μονογενῆ** — the only son *(attributive position, article
  repeated — Lesson 10; **μονογενής**, *only-begotten*, third declension)*
- **ἔδωκεν** — gave *(aorist of **δίδωμι**: the verb of Lesson 9 and of
  Thursday)*
- **ἵνα … μὴ ἀπόληται** — in order that he may not perish *(aorist
  subjunctive, middle, of **ἀπόλλυμαι**)*
- **πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτόν** — everyone who believes in him *(**πᾶς**
  outside the article group: *every*; **πιστεύω εἰς**, Lesson 19)*
- **ἀλλ᾽ ἔχῃ** — but may have *(present subjunctive: go on having)*
- **ζωὴν αἰώνιον** — eternal life *(no article: *life of the age*, as a
  kind of thing)*

*For God so loved the world that he gave the only son, in order that
everyone who believes in him may not perish but may have eternal life.*

**Two subjunctives, two aspects.** **ἀπόληται** is aorist: perishing is a
single event, and the purpose is that it not happen. **ἔχῃ** is present:
having life is a condition that continues, and the purpose is that it go
on. The sentence chooses an aspect for each half of its purpose and the
choice is not decoration. English *may not perish but may have* flattens
both to *may*.

**And one ὥστε.** Result, with the indicative: the giving *happened*. Had
John written **ὥστε δοῦναι**, with an infinitive, he would have said the
love was *such as to* give; with **ἔδωκεν** he says it did. Lesson 29's
method — mark the verbs — finds three here: one indicative of fact
(**ἠγάπησεν**), one indicative of result (**ἔδωκεν**), two subjunctives of
purpose. The whole shape of the sentence is in its moods.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text follows the standard Greek New
Testament (see *Sources*); check it against the edition in use. The
punctuation and the elision **ἀλλ᾽** vary between printings and do not
affect the reading.

**On οὕτως.** The Latin *sīc … ut* and the English *so … that* have
taught generations to hear *so much*; the Greek **οὕτως** more often means
*in this way*, pointing back at the bronze serpent of verse 14. Mention it
if a pupil raises it; the class has met the translator's dilemma before
and this is a famous instance of it.

**On Theodosius.** This is his verse and he will want to say so. Let him
read it first, in Greek, and then ask him what **ἀπόληται** is — he will
get it, because the lesson has just taught it — and what the aspect does.
He should discover that the sentence he has known by heart since he was six
has a grammar, and that the grammar agrees with him.
:::

**First tier — adapted.** *Attic; composed in the words of the course.*

ἦν δὲ ἄνθρωπος, Νικόδημος ὄνομα αὐτῷ, ἄρχων τῶν Ἰουδαίων. οὗτος ἦλθε πρὸς
τὸν Ἰησοῦν νυκτὸς καὶ εἶπεν αὐτῷ· "ὦ διδάσκαλε, οἴδαμεν ὅτι ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ
ἦλθες· οὐδεὶς γὰρ δύναται ποιεῖν τὰ σημεῖα ἃ σὺ ποιεῖς, ἐὰν μὴ ὁ θεὸς ᾖ
σὺν αὐτῷ."

ἀπεκρίνατο δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· "ἀμὴν λέγω σοι· ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἄνωθεν, οὐ
δύναται ἰδεῖν τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ."

ὁ δὲ Νικόδημος ἠρώτα· "πῶς δύναται ἄνθρωπος γεννηθῆναι πάλιν; ἆρα δύναται
πάλιν παιδίον γενέσθαι;"

ἀπεκρίνατο ὁ Ἰησοῦς· "ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἐξ ὕδατος καὶ πνεύματος, οὐ
δύναται εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ ἐλθεῖν. μὴ θαυμάσῃς ὅτι εἶπόν σοι· δεῖ
ὑμᾶς γεννηθῆναι ἄνωθεν. τὸ πνεῦμα πνεῖ ὅπου ἐθέλει, καὶ τὴν φωνὴν αὐτοῦ
ἀκούεις, ἀλλ᾽ οὐκ οἶσθα πόθεν ἔρχεται καὶ ποῖ ἔρχεται."

**Second tier — the original.** *John 3:1–8, Koine, from the standard
text.*

Ἦν δὲ ἄνθρωπος ἐκ τῶν Φαρισαίων, Νικόδημος ὄνομα αὐτῷ, ἄρχων τῶν Ἰουδαίων·
οὗτος ἦλθεν πρὸς αὐτὸν νυκτὸς καὶ εἶπεν αὐτῷ· ῥαββί, οἴδαμεν ὅτι ἀπὸ θεοῦ
ἐλήλυθας διδάσκαλος· οὐδεὶς γὰρ δύναται ταῦτα τὰ σημεῖα ποιεῖν ἃ σὺ ποιεῖς,
ἐὰν μὴ ᾖ ὁ θεὸς μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ. ἀπεκρίθη Ἰησοῦς καὶ εἶπεν αὐτῷ· ἀμὴν ἀμὴν λέγω
σοι, ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἄνωθεν, οὐ δύναται ἰδεῖν τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ.
Λέγει πρὸς αὐτὸν [ὁ] Νικόδημος· πῶς δύναται ἄνθρωπος γεννηθῆναι γέρων ὤν;
μὴ δύναται εἰς τὴν κοιλίαν τῆς μητρὸς αὐτοῦ δεύτερον εἰσελθεῖν καὶ
γεννηθῆναι; ἀπεκρίθη Ἰησοῦς· ἀμὴν ἀμὴν λέγω σοι, ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἐξ
ὕδατος καὶ πνεύματος, οὐ δύναται εἰσελθεῖν εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ. τὸ
γεγεννημένον ἐκ τῆς σαρκὸς σάρξ ἐστιν, καὶ τὸ γεγεννημένον ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος
πνεῦμά ἐστιν. μὴ θαυμάσῃς ὅτι εἶπόν σοι· δεῖ ὑμᾶς γεννηθῆναι ἄνωθεν. τὸ
πνεῦμα ὅπου θέλει πνεῖ καὶ τὴν φωνὴν αὐτοῦ ἀκούεις, ἀλλ᾽ οὐκ οἶδας πόθεν
ἔρχεται καὶ ποῦ ὑπάγει· οὕτως ἐστὶν πᾶς ὁ γεγεννημένος ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος.

::: gloss
- **Νικόδημος ὄνομα αὐτῷ** — Nicodemus by name *(literally "a name to
  him", the dative of possession, Lesson 9)*
- **ὁ ἄρχων** — ruler, leader *(genitive **ἄρχοντος**; from **ἀρχή**)*
- **οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι** — the Jews
- **οὗτος** — this man; he *(Lesson 17)*
- **νυκτός** — by night *(genitive of time)*
- **οἴδαμεν** — we know *(1 plur. of **οἶδα**)*
- **ἃ σὺ ποιεῖς** — which you do *(neuter plural relative)*
- **ᾖ** — may be *(subjunctive of **εἰμί**)*
- **ἀπεκρίνατο** — answered *(Attic aorist middle; the Koine has
  **ἀπεκρίθη**)*
- **γεννηθῇ, γεννηθῆναι** — be born *(aorist passive subjunctive and
  infinitive of **γεννάω**)*
- **ἰδεῖν** — to see *(aorist infinitive of **ὁράω**)*
- **γενέσθαι** — to become *(aorist infinitive of **γίγνομαι**)*
- **μὴ θαυμάσῃς** — do not be amazed *(**θαυμάζω**; prohibition with the
  aorist subjunctive)*
- **ὑμᾶς** — you *(plural, accusative)*
- **πνεῖ** — blows, breathes *(**πνέω** — the verb of **πνεῦμα**)*
- **ὅπου** — where
- **πόθεν** — from where
- **ποῖ** — to where *(Attic; the Koine asks **ποῦ ὑπάγει**, where it goes)*
- **οἱ Φαρισαῖοι** — the Pharisees
- **ῥαββί** — Rabbi, teacher *(Hebrew)*
- **ἐλήλυθας** — you have come *(perfect of **ἔρχομαι**, Lesson 33)*
- **ταῦτα** — these *(Lesson 17)*
- **μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ** — with him
- **γέρων ὤν** — being an old man *(**ὤν**, the participle of **εἰμί**)*
- **μὴ δύναται …;** — surely he cannot …? *(**μή** opening a question
  expects the answer no)*
- **ἡ κοιλία** — womb; belly
- **δεύτερον** — a second time
- **εἰσελθεῖν** — to go in *(**εἰς** + **ἐλθεῖν**)*
- **τὸ γεγεννημένον** — what has been born *(perfect passive participle,
  neuter)*
- **ὁ γεγεννημένος** — the one who has been born
- **οὕτως** — so, in this way
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. Who comes to whom, and when? Which word gives the time, and what case is
   it in?
2. Nicodemus's first sentence is in the indicative: **οἴδαμεν ὅτι … ἦλθες**.
   What kind of sentence is that — a statement, a question, or a wish?
3. His sentence ends with a subjunctive: **ἐὰν μὴ ὁ θεὸς ᾖ σὺν αὐτῷ**.
   Which job from the lesson is it doing, and how do you translate **ἐὰν
   μή**?
4. The answer begins **ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἄνωθεν**. Parse **γεννηθῇ**: tense,
   voice, mood, and what on the page tells you each.
5. Nicodemus's question is **πῶς δύναται**: indicative. The answer is
   subjunctive. Why can a *how* question not be answered in the mood it
   was asked in, here?
6. **ἄνωθεν** means two things. Which one does Nicodemus hear? Quote the
   words that show you.
7. **μὴ θαυμάσῃς** — what kind of **μή** is this (Lesson 26), and why is
   the verb aorist?
8. In the original, Nicodemus says **ἀπὸ θεοῦ ἐλήλυθας**; the adaptation
   says **ἦλθες**. Name both forms and say what the original claims that
   the adaptation does not.
9. Find three things in the original that mark it as Koine rather than
   Attic (Lesson 23). The adaptation has changed each; say what it changed
   it to.
10. **τὸ πνεῦμα ὅπου θέλει πνεῖ** — **πνεῦμα** was *breath*, *wind* and
    *spirit* in Lesson 21. Which is it here? Can the sentence be read with
    all three, and does that help or hinder a translator?
:::

::: answers
1. Nicodemus to Jesus, by night: **νυκτός**, genitive — the genitive of
   the time *within which*.
2. A statement: *we know that you have come from God.* He arrives with a
   fact.
3. Condition — general: *unless God is with him.* **ἐὰν μή** = *if not* =
   *unless*; **ᾖ** is the subjunctive of **εἰμί**.
4. Aorist (stem **γεννη-** with **-θη-**, Lesson 31), passive (**-θη-**),
   subjunctive (long **ῃ**, no augment). *Be born* — once.
5. Because the answer is not a fact about how something is done but a
   condition about what must be the case: *unless someone be born …* The
   indicative reports what is; the answer is about what would have to be.
   Nicodemus asked a mechanism and was given a requirement.
6. *Again*: **δεύτερον εἰσελθεῖν καὶ γεννηθῆναι**, *enter a second time and
   be born*. The adaptation has **πάλιν**. The speaker's **ἐξ ὕδατος καὶ
   πνεύματος** points at the other sense, *from above*.
7. A prohibition — **μή**, never **οὐ**, with a command. Aorist because it
   forbids a single reaction, now, to this sentence; **μὴ θαύμαζε** would
   mean *stop being a person who is amazed*.
8. **ἐλήλυθας**, perfect: *you have come and are here*, a present state
   resulting from a past act (Lesson 33). **ἦλθες**, aorist: *you came*.
   The original claims the arrival still stands; Nicodemus is talking to
   someone whose coming is a present fact.
9. Any three of: **ἀπεκρίθη** (Attic **ἀπεκρίνατο**); **οἶδας** (Attic
   **οἶσθα**); **ποῦ ὑπάγει** (Attic **ποῖ ἔρχεται**); **θέλει** (Attic
   **ἐθέλει**); **μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ** (**σὺν αὐτῷ**); **ῥαββί** (**ὦ
   διδάσκαλε**); **ἀμὴν ἀμήν** doubled; **ἀπὸ θεοῦ** without the article.
   Credit a pupil who notes that the Koine is not *wrong*, only different.
10. *Wind* on its surface — it blows, it has a sound, you cannot see where
    it comes from; *spirit* in its point, since the sentence ends **πᾶς ὁ
    γεγεννημένος ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος**; and *breath* underneath both, in the
    verb **πνεῖ**. The Greek holds all three in one word and the sentence
    depends on it. A translator must choose one and lose the sentence's
    turn, or keep the Greek word and lose the reader. Neither helps; both
    are honest.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. Nicodemus asks *how* and is told *unless*. Is that an answer? When is a
   condition a better reply than a mechanism?
2. The indicative reports; the subjunctive opens. A language that has a
   whole mood for what might be — is it a more hopeful language, or merely
   a more careful one?
3. **τί ποιῶμεν;** — *what are we to do?* Lesson 25 ended with a question
   that had no answer. Does this one? Who is supposed to supply it?
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
Quintus asked again, at the door, what they were to do on Thursday.

"**τί ποιῶμεν;**" he said, and got the vowel right, and knew it.

"You have asked correctly," said Theodoros, "and now I am obliged to answer,
and I shall. On Thursday we read the oldest verbs in the language — the
ones that were already old when **λύω** was new. One of them has been on
your vocabulary list since Lesson 9 and you have never been shown how it
works. And I shall give something back."

"Give what back?"

"**δίδωμι**," said Theodoros, "is the verb. Learn to ask about the rest in
it, and I shall tell you."
:::
