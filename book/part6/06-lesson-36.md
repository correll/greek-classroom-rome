# The Old Verbs

::: {.story time="5 min"}
Theodoros came in with the box.

Everyone knew the box. It had sat on the shelf beside the Psalms since the
first week, and it was the one object in the room nobody had ever been
allowed to label, and the one object Felix had never tried to.

He put it on the table and opened it, and took things out one at a time,
and gave them back.

"**δίδωμι**," he said, and gave Sabina a tablet on which eight adjectives
had been written and seven crossed out. "**δίδωμι**," and Aelia had the
first sentence she had ever said in Greek, written down afterwards by
someone else, with the error marked. Quintus got his argument about the
soul, and Marcus got a tablet he had not seen since Lesson 18, with *God
was the Word* on it in his own hand, and Theodosius the Beatitudes with the
Latin down the margin, and Livia a sheet of sentences with no subjects.
Felix got his letter to the cat. The cat got nothing, and watched.

"**δίδωμι. δίδως. δίδωσι.** I give, you give, he gives. There is no omega,
and there is no vowel between the stem and the ending, because this verb is
older than the vowel. It was already old when **λύω** was new. It is the
verb on your list since Lesson 9 that I have never once shown you how to
build, and it is the last thing you learn from me, and I have kept it back
on purpose."

He reached into the box once more and took out three small wooden tags
tied together with a thread, and put them on the bench in front of Julia,
and said nothing about them at all, and went to the board.

Julia looked at the tags. The top one read **ὁ διδάσκαλος**. She looked at
Theodoros's back for some time. He did not turn round.

"**ἀπέδωκα**," he said to the board. "I have given back. Aorist. Once,
completely. Now: the oldest verbs in the language. There are six of them
you need, and you have been using two since Part I without knowing they
were strange."
:::

::: {.newgreek time="10 min"}
## The verbs that did not change

Every verb you have learned ends its first person in **-ω**, and puts a
vowel — **ο** or **ε** — between the stem and the ending: **λύ-ο-μεν,
λύ-ε-τε**. That vowel is the *thematic vowel*, and the **-ω** verbs are the
younger family.

The older family has no thematic vowel and ends its first person in
**-μι**. The ending sits directly on the stem; the stem vowel is **long in
the singular and short in the plural**. There are not many of these verbs,
but they are the commonest words in the language — *give*, *put*, *stand*,
*be*, *go*, *say* — because the words people use most are the ones that
change least. Latin kept the same ones: *dō*, *sum*, *eō*. English *am* is
the same ending as **εἰμί**.

## δίδωμι, τίθημι, ἵστημι: the present

| | **δίδωμι** *give* | **τίθημι** *put* | **ἵστημι** *make stand* |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **δίδωμι** | **τίθημι** | **ἵστημι** |
| 2 sing. | **δίδως** | **τίθης** | **ἵστης** |
| 3 sing. | **δίδωσι(ν)** | **τίθησι(ν)** | **ἵστησι(ν)** |
| 1 plur. | **δίδομεν** | **τίθεμεν** | **ἵσταμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **δίδοτε** | **τίθετε** | **ἵστατε** |
| 3 plur. | **διδόασι(ν)** | **τιθέασι(ν)** | **ἱστᾶσι(ν)** |
| infinitive | **διδόναι** | **τιθέναι** | **ἱστάναι** |
| imperative | **δίδου, δίδοτε** | **τίθει, τίθετε** | **ἵστη, ἵστατε** |
| participle | **διδούς** | **τιθείς** | **ἱστάς** |

Each present stem is *reduplicated*: the first consonant repeated with
**ι** — **δι-δω-**, **τι-θη-** (the **θ** softened to **τ**), **ἱ-στη-**
(the **σ** became a rough breathing). That is the reduplication of Lesson
33 doing a different job: there it marked the perfect; here it marks the
present of an old verb. The stems themselves are **δω/δο**, **θη/θε**,
**στη/στα**, long in the singular and short in the plural, exactly as the
table shows.

The imperfect takes an augment and the short-vowel endings:
**ἐδίδουν, ἐδίδους, ἐδίδου, ἐδίδομεν, ἐδίδοτε, ἐδίδοσαν**; **ἐτίθην,
ἐτίθεις, ἐτίθει, ἐτίθεμεν, ἐτίθετε, ἐτίθεσαν**; **ἵστην, ἵστης, ἵστη,
ἵσταμεν, ἵστατε, ἵστασαν**. The singulars of the first two have borrowed
the look of a contract verb; the plurals are plain.

## The aorist

| | **δίδωμι** | **τίθημι** | **ἵστημι** |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **ἔδωκα** | **ἔθηκα** | **ἔστην** |
| 2 sing. | **ἔδωκας** | **ἔθηκας** | **ἔστης** |
| 3 sing. | **ἔδωκε(ν)** | **ἔθηκε(ν)** | **ἔστη** |
| 1 plur. | **ἔδομεν** | **ἔθεμεν** | **ἔστημεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἔδοτε** | **ἔθετε** | **ἔστητε** |
| 3 plur. | **ἔδοσαν** | **ἔθεσαν** | **ἔστησαν** |
| infinitive | **δοῦναι** | **θεῖναι** | **στῆναι** |
| imperative | **δός, δότε** | **θές, θέτε** | **στῆθι, στῆτε** |
| participle | **δούς** | **θείς** | **στάς** |
| subjunctive | **δῶ, δῷς, δῷ, δῶμεν, δῶτε, δῶσι(ν)** | **θῶ, θῇς, θῇ, θῶμεν, θῆτε, θῶσι(ν)** | **στῶ, στῇς, στῇ, στῶμεν, στῆτε, στῶσι(ν)** |

Three things to see.

**One.** The aorist drops the reduplication: the stem is bare. **ἔδωκα** is
**ἐ-δω-κ-α**: augment, stem, and a **κ** where the first aorist has **σ**.
The plural has no **κ** at all and the short stem: **ἔδομεν**. Learn the
singular and the plural as two halves.

**Two.** **ἵστημι** has two aorists with two meanings. **ἔστησα** (a
regular first aorist) is *I made to stand, I set up*; **ἔστην** (the one
in the table) is *I stood*. And its perfect **ἕστηκα** means *I am
standing* — the perfect as a present state, Lesson 33, in its purest case.

**Three.** The imperatives are the shortest words in the language. **δός**
— *give!* **θές** — *put!* **στῆθι** — *stand!*, with the **-θι** of
**γνῶθι**, Lesson 26. And the aorist subjunctives are the lesson of Lesson
35 reduced to a single long vowel and an ending: **δῶ**, *that I may give*.

## The middle

The middle endings of Lesson 31 go straight onto the short stem. Present:
**δίδομαι, δίδοσαι, δίδοται, διδόμεθα, δίδοσθε, δίδονται**; so **τίθεται**,
*is put*, **ἵσταται**, *stands* (the middle of *make stand* is *stand*).
Aorist middle: **ἐδόμην, ἔδου, ἔδοτο, ἐδόμεθα, ἔδοσθε, ἔδοντο**; **ἐθέμην,
ἔθου, ἔθετο …** The rest of the verb is regular and you can already read
it: future **δώσω, θήσω, στήσω**; perfect **δέδωκα, τέθηκα, ἕστηκα**; aorist
passive **ἐδόθην, ἐτέθην, ἐστάθην**; future passive **δοθήσομαι** — *it
will be given*, the word this lesson's Ancient Voice turns on.

## εἰμί, in full

You have had it in pieces since Lesson 5. Here is the whole.

| | present | imperfect | future | subjunctive |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **εἰμί** | **ἦν** *(or **ἦ**)* | **ἔσομαι** | **ὦ** |
| 2 sing. | **εἶ** | **ἦσθα** | **ἔσῃ** | **ᾖς** |
| 3 sing. | **ἐστί(ν)** | **ἦν** | **ἔσται** | **ᾖ** |
| 1 plur. | **ἐσμέν** | **ἦμεν** | **ἐσόμεθα** | **ὦμεν** |
| 2 plur. | **ἐστέ** | **ἦτε** | **ἔσεσθε** | **ἦτε** |
| 3 plur. | **εἰσί(ν)** | **ἦσαν** | **ἔσονται** | **ὦσι(ν)** |

Imperative **ἴσθι, ἔστω, ἔστε, ἔστων**; infinitive **εἶναι**; participle
**ὤν, οὖσα, ὄν** (genitive **ὄντος**). The stem is **ἐσ-**: **ἐσ-μί**
became **εἰμί**, **ἐσ-σί** became **εἶ**, and **ἐσ-τί** stayed exactly as
it was — which is why it is also Latin *est* and, with the vowel worn off
the front, English *is*. The third singular of the future, **ἔσται**, has
no thematic vowel either; it is the oldest form in the table.

## εἶμι

**εἶμι**, *I go*, is a different verb from **εἰμί** by a breathing and an
accent, and in Attic it has **future meaning**: Lesson 32 gave it to you as
the future of **ἔρχομαι**, and that is how to use it.

| | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **εἶμι** | 1 plur. | **ἴμεν** |
| 2 sing. | **εἶ** | 2 plur. | **ἴτε** |
| 3 sing. | **εἶσι(ν)** | 3 plur. | **ἴασι(ν)** |

Imperfect **ᾖα, ᾔεισθα, ᾔει, ᾖμεν, ᾖτε, ᾖσαν**; imperative **ἴθι, ἴτε**;
infinitive **ἰέναι**; participle **ἰών, ἰοῦσα, ἰόν**. Two of its forms
collide with **εἰμί**. **εἶ** is *you are* and also *you will go*; **εἶσι**
is *he will go*, and **εἰσί** — accent on the end, no circumflex — is
*they are*. What in the word tells you? The accent, and nothing else. It
is the last time this book will ask the question, and the answer is the
same as the first time.

## φημί

**φημί**, *I say, I assert*, is the Attic verb for stating a view, and the
one Plato uses when Socrates reports what somebody *said*.

| | | | |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 sing. | **φημί** | 1 plur. | **φαμέν** |
| 2 sing. | **φῄς** | 2 plur. | **φατέ** |
| 3 sing. | **φησί(ν)** | 3 plur. | **φασί(ν)** |

Imperfect **ἔφην, ἔφησθα, ἔφη, ἔφαμεν, ἔφατε, ἔφασαν**; infinitive
**φάναι**. It takes the accusative and infinitive (Lesson 16): **φησὶ τὸν
ἄνθρωπον σοφὸν εἶναι**. **οὔ φημι** is not *I do not say* but *I say no, I
deny*. And **ἔφη**, *he said*, is dropped into the middle of a quotation
in the way English does with *said he*: **ὦ ἄνδρες, ἔφη, ἀκούετε** — *men,
he said, listen.* Every dialogue of Plato has it on every page.

::: note
**Enclitics, for the last time.** The present of **εἰμί** (except **εἶ**)
and of **φημί** (except **φῄς**) is enclitic — it leans on the word before
and gives up its accent, by the rules of Lesson 25. So **σοφός ἐστιν**,
**ὁ θεὸς φῶς ἐστιν**, **οἱ πολλοί φασιν**. **ἐστί** gets its own accent on
the first syllable, **ἔστι**, when it begins a sentence, when it follows
**οὐκ**, **καί**, **ἀλλά**, **εἰ** or **τοῦτο**, and when it means *exists*
or *is possible*: **οὐκ ἔστιν οὐδεμία** in Lesson 30. **εἶμι** and its
forms are never enclitic — a second way, besides the accent's position, to
tell *they are* from *he will go*.
:::

::: latinbridge
**Latin kept the same old verbs, and this is the lesson where the two
languages turn out to be one, further back than either.**

| Greek | Latin | |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **δίδωμι, δίδως, δίδωσι** | *dō, dās, dat* | the same root **δω-/dō-** |
| **ἵστημι** | *sistō* | both reduplicated: **ἱ-στη-**, *si-st-* |
| **ἔστην** *I stood* | *stō, stetī* | |
| **εἰμί, εἶ, ἐστί, ἐσμέν, ἐστέ, εἰσί** | *sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt* | *est* is **ἐστί** untouched |
| **εἶμι, εἶ, εἶσι, ἴμεν, ἴτε, ἴασι** | *eō, īs, it, īmus, ītis, eunt* | |
| **φημί, φάναι, φήμη** | *for, fārī, fāma* | |

**τίθημι** looks as if it has no Latin cousin, and it has several in
hiding: the root **θη-** is the *-dō* of *con-dō* (put together, found),
*ab-dō* (put away) and *crē-dō* (put one's trust: believe). It is also
English *do* and *deed*.

Where Latin misleads: *dō* has lost its reduplication, so a pupil will look
for *didō* and not find it; Latin put the doubling into the perfect
instead, *dedī*, where Greek has **δέδωκα**. And Latin *sum* has no
subjunctive **ὦ** and no participle **ὤν** of its own, which is why *ens*
had to be invented by philosophers and **ὄν** did not. Marcus's column
gets one line today — *Latin cannot say **ὤν*** — and then he may close
it, because the column was never really about Latin.
:::
:::

::: vocab
- **τίθημι** — I put, I place *(aorist **ἔθηκα**; middle **τίθεμαι**, I set
  for myself, I make)*
- **ἵστημι** — I make stand, I set up *(aorist **ἔστησα**); **ἔστην**, I
  stood; perfect **ἕστηκα**, I am standing*
- **φημί** — I say, I assert *(imperfect **ἔφην**; **ἔφη**, he said; **οὔ
  φημι**, I deny)*
- **εἶμι** — I will go *(the future of **ἔρχομαι**; infinitive **ἰέναι**;
  imperative **ἴθι**)*
- **ἀποδίδωμι** — I give back, I pay back *(aorist **ἀπέδωκα**)*
- **εὑρίσκω** — I find *(aorist **εὗρον**; future **εὑρήσω**)*
- **ἀνοίγω** — I open *(future passive **ἀνοιγήσομαι**, I will be opened)*
- **ὁ ἄρτος** — bread, a loaf
- **ὁ λίθος** — stone
- **τὸ τέκνον** — child *(as someone's son or daughter; **παῖς** is the
  child as a young person)*
- **πονηρός** — wicked, bad; worthless
- **μᾶλλον** — more, rather *(**πόσῳ μᾶλλον**, how much more)*
- **παύω** — I stop *(someone); middle **παύομαι**, I cease, I stop
  (myself)*
- **ἡ πόλις** — city *(genitive **πόλεως**; third declension)*
- **ἡ τιμή** — honour; price

::: note
**δίδωμι** has been in your vocabulary since Lesson 9 and **εἰμί** since
Lesson 5; they are the two oldest words on the list and this lesson has
finally shown you their whole shape. They are not re-listed here. Their
principal parts, for the back of the book: **δίδωμι, δώσω, ἔδωκα, δέδωκα,
δέδομαι, ἐδόθην**.

**ἀποδίδωμι** is **ἀπό** + **δίδωμι**, and the augment goes *between* the
two: **ἀπ-έ-δωκα**, as **ἀντι-παρ-ῆλθεν** did in Lesson 22. Its middle,
**ἀποδίδομαι**, is *I sell* — to give away for oneself. **τιμή** is both
*honour* and *price*, which tells you something about how the Greeks
thought of each.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Retrieval"}
From memory. These range over the whole year.

1. **τὸ δῶρον** — nominative or accusative?
2. Why would a writer put **ἐγώ** in front of **λέγω**, when the ending
   already says *I*?
3. **εἶπον** and **ἔλεγον** — which is the aorist, and how do you know?
4. **Ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα** — what does Attic have that this line does
   not?
5. **ὁ ποιήσας τὸ ἔλεος** — what kind of word is **ποιήσας**, and what does
   the whole phrase mean?
6. **ἔρρωσο** — where in a letter, and what form is it?
7. **ἐλυσάμην** against **ἐλύθην** — voices?
8. **μενῶ** — tense? What tells you?
9. **ποιεῖ** — unmelt it, and name the person.
10. **μὴ εἴπῃς** — what mood, and why aorist?
:::

::: answers
1. Either; neuter, so the sentence must decide (Lesson 3).
2. For contrast: *I* say, as against someone else (Lesson 11).
3. **εἶπον** — a different stem from the present **λέγ-** (Lesson 14).
4. The article: Homer has none where Attic would say **τὸν ἄνδρα** (Lesson
   15).
5. An aorist participle, *having done*; *the one who did the mercy* — the
   lawyer's answer in Lesson 22.
6. The close; a perfect middle imperative, *be strong* — *farewell*
   (Lesson 28).
7. **ἐλυσάμην** middle, *I loosed for myself*; **ἐλύθην** passive, *I was
   loosed* (Lesson 31).
8. Future — a contracted future of a liquid stem; the circumflex is the
   only mark it has (Lesson 32).
9. **ποιέ-ει**, third singular present active (Lesson 34).
10. Subjunctive, a prohibition; aorist because it forbids a single act
    (Lesson 35).
:::

::: {.exercise title="Which old verb?"}
Give the verb, person, number and tense of each, and the one thing in the
form that told you.

1. **δίδωσι**
2. **ἔθηκε**
3. **ἔστη**
4. **ἔστησε**
5. **τίθεμεν**
6. **εἶσι**
7. **εἰσί**
8. **φησί**
9. **ἔδοσαν**
10. **ἴθι**
:::

::: answers
1. **δίδωμι**, 3 sing. present: reduplication **δι-**, long **ω**, **-σι**.
2. **τίθημι**, 3 sing. aorist: augment, bare stem **θη-**, the **κ**.
3. **ἵστημι**, 3 sing. aorist, *he stood*: augment, bare **στη-**, no
   ending at all.
4. **ἵστημι**, 3 sing. aorist, *he set up*: the **σ** of a first aorist.
   The difference between 3 and 4 is the difference between standing and
   making something stand, and a pupil who gives *stood* for both has
   missed the lesson's one trap.
5. **τίθημι**, 1 plur. present: reduplication **τι-**, short **ε**,
   **-μεν**.
6. **εἶμι**, 3 sing. present with future sense, *he will go*: circumflex on
   the first syllable.
7. **εἰμί**, 3 plur. present, *they are*: acute on the last syllable.
8. **φημί**, 3 sing. present, *he says*: **φη-** and **-σι**; enclitic.
9. **δίδωμι**, 3 plur. aorist, *they gave*: augment, short **δο-**, no
   **κ**, **-σαν**.
10. **εἶμι**, 2 sing. imperative, *go!*: the **-θι** of **γνῶθι** and
    **στῆθι**.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Translate"}
Composed, except where a source is given.

1. **ὁ διδάσκαλος δίδωσι τὰ δῶρα τοῖς παισίν.**
2. **ποῦ ἔθηκας τὴν ἐπιστολήν;**
3. **Σωκράτης ἔφη τὸν ἀνεξέταστον βίον οὐ βιωτὸν εἶναι.**
4. **ἔστη ὁ παιδαγωγὸς ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ καὶ οὐκ ἤθελεν ἰέναι.**
5. **δός μοι τὸ βιβλίον.**
6. **οἱ πολλοί φασιν ὅτι σοφός ἐστιν· ἐγὼ δὲ οὔ φημι.**
7. **αἰτεῖτε, καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν.** *(Matthew 7:7)*
8. **ἀπέδωκεν ὁ διδάσκαλος τοῖς παισὶ τὰ γραμματεῖα ἃ εἶχεν.**
   *(**τὸ γραμματεῖον** = tablet)*
9. **ὁ αἴλουρος οὐδενὶ οὐδὲν δίδωσιν.**
10. **"ἴμεν εἰς τὴν πόλιν," ἔφη ὁ πατήρ· "ὁ γὰρ ἱερεὺς ἔρχεται."**
:::

::: answers
1. *The teacher gives the gifts to the children.* — **παισί(ν)**, dative
   plural of **παῖς**.
2. *Where did you put the letter?* — **ἔθηκας**, aorist, the single act.
3. *Socrates said that the unexamined life is not worth living.* — **ἔφη**
   with accusative and infinitive: Lesson 17's sentence reported by Lesson
   16's rule, with the verb Plato would use. The class has now written this
   sentence three ways.
4. *The paedagogus stood in the road and did not want to go.* — **ἔστη**,
   *stood*; **ἰέναι**, the infinitive of **εἶμι**.
5. *Give me the book.* — **δός**, with **μοι** leaning on it.
6. *Most people say that he is wise; but I say he is not.* — **οἱ πολλοί**
   keeps its acute before the enclitic; **οὔ φημι** is a denial, not a
   silence.
7. *Ask, and it will be given to you.* — **αἰτεῖτε**, contract imperative
   (Lesson 34); **δοθήσεται**, future passive of **δίδωμι** (Lesson 32's
   future, Lesson 31's passive).
8. *The teacher gave back to the children the tablets which he had.* —
   **ἀπέδωκεν** with the augment inside; **εἶχεν**, imperfect of **ἔχω**:
   he had been keeping them.
9. *The cat gives nothing to anyone.* — **οὐδενί, οὐδέν**: the two
   negatives reinforce. The sentence is from Lesson 9's story and was true
   then.
10. *"We shall go into the city," said the father; "for the priest is
    coming."* — **ἴμεν**, future in sense; **ἔφη** inside the quotation.
:::

::: {.exercise title="Into Greek"}
One sentence each.

1. I give you the book.
2. The teacher gave back the tablets. *(**τὰ γραμματεῖα**)*
3. Put the letter in the house. *(one person, now)*
4. We stood in the road.
5. He says that the cat is wise. *(use **φημί**)*
6. They will go to the city.
7. Give! *(one person, now)*
8. Be good. *(one person)*
9. God gives good things to the children.
10. The teacher sees.
:::

::: answers
1. **δίδωμί σοι τὸ βιβλίον.** — **δίδωμι** takes a second accent before
   the enclitic **σοι**, by the rule of Lesson 25.
2. **ὁ διδάσκαλος ἀπέδωκε τὰ γραμματεῖα.** — augment between prefix and
   stem.
3. **θὲς τὴν ἐπιστολὴν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ.** — aorist imperative **θές**; grave
   before the next word.
4. **ἔστημεν ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ.** — the second aorist; **ἐστήσαμεν** would mean we
   set something up.
5. **φησὶ τὸν αἴλουρον σοφὸν εἶναι.** — accusative and infinitive, Lesson
   16; a pupil who writes **φησὶν ὅτι ὁ αἴλουρος σοφός ἐστιν** has written
   Koine, which is not wrong, and should say which they meant to write.
6. **ἴασιν εἰς τὴν πόλιν.** — **εἶμι**, future in sense. **ἐλεύσονται** is
   the Koine future and is also correct.
7. **δός.** — one syllable.
8. **ἴσθι ἀγαθός.** — the imperative of **εἰμί**; **ἀγαθή** for a girl.
9. **ὁ θεὸς ἀγαθὰ δίδωσι τοῖς τέκνοις.** — **ἀγαθά** without an article,
   *good things*, as in Matthew 7:11.
10. **ὁ διδάσκαλος βλέπει.** — Lesson 3's sentence, and Julia's. Nothing in
    it has changed. Everything in the pupil who writes it has.

::: {.voice time="10 min" source="Matthew 7:7"}
One verse, three clauses, and every lesson of Part VI in it.

**Αἰτεῖτε καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν, ζητεῖτε καὶ εὑρήσετε, κρούετε καὶ
ἀνοιγήσεται ὑμῖν·**

- **αἰτεῖτε** — ask! *(present imperative of **αἰτέω**, a contract verb —
  Lesson 34; keep asking)*
- **δοθήσεται** — it will be given *(future passive of **δίδωμι**: stem
  **δο-**, **-θη-** of the passive, **-σ-** of the future, middle ending —
  Lessons 31, 32 and 36 in one word)*
- **ὑμῖν** — to you *(plural dative)*
- **ζητεῖτε** — seek! *(**ζητέω**)*
- **εὑρήσετε** — you will find *(future of **εὑρίσκω**)*
- **κρούετε** — knock! *(**κρούω**, an ordinary **-ω** verb at last)*
- **ἀνοιγήσεται** — it will be opened *(future passive of **ἀνοίγω**)*

*Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it
will be opened to you.*

**Three present imperatives.** Not *ask once*: *be asking*, *be seeking*,
*be knocking* — the aspect of a habit, Lesson 26, and the sentence is
advice about a way of living, not about a single request. **Three
futures.** Two of them passive, with no agent named: *it will be given*,
*it will be opened* — by whom, the Greek does not say and the hearer is
meant to supply. **And one -μι verb,** hidden inside the longest word.
**δοθήσεται** is **δίδωμι** with the reduplication gone, the passive
marker, the future marker and a middle ending: four lessons stacked on one
stem. A year ago you could not read the letters. Now you can take that word
apart and name each piece and say what it is doing.

::: teacheronly
**Verify before teaching.** The text follows the standard Greek New
Testament (see *Sources*); check it against the edition in use, and note
that some editions print **ἀνοίγεται** for the second **ἀνοιγήσεται** in
the verse that follows.

**On the verse as a summary.** It is chosen because it is short, known to
the class in Latin (*petite et dabitur vobis*), and contains a contract
imperative, a future, a future passive and a **-μι** stem in nine words.
Have each pupil take one word and account for every piece of it aloud.
This is the Great Reading's method applied to a single verse, and it is a
fair final examination of Part VI.

**On the box.** The things Theodoros gives back are the class's own errors
from the year, kept. If your own class has a year's worth of tablets, this
is the lesson to return them; if it does not, the scene still works as a
story, but it works better as a fact.
:::
:::

::: {.reading time="20 min" title="Ask, and it will be given" source="Matthew 7:7–11; Plato, *Apology* 29d–e"}
The last two readings, side by side: a Gospel and a philosopher, each with
an old verb at its centre. Read the adapted tier first, marking every
**-μι** form and every subjunctive; then the originals.

**First tier — adapted.** *Attic; composed in the words of the course.*

αἰτεῖτε, καὶ δώσει ὑμῖν ὁ θεός· ζητεῖτε, καὶ εὑρήσετε. πᾶς γὰρ ὁ αἰτῶν
λαμβάνει, καὶ ὁ ζητῶν εὑρίσκει. τίς ἐστιν ἐξ ὑμῶν ἄνθρωπος ὃς τῷ τέκνῳ,
ἐὰν ἄρτον αἰτῇ, λίθον δίδωσιν; εἰ οὖν ὑμεῖς, πονηροὶ ὄντες, ἀγαθὰ δίδοτε
τοῖς τέκνοις ὑμῶν, πόσῳ μᾶλλον ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν ὁ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ δώσει ἀγαθὰ τοῖς
αἰτοῦσιν αὐτόν;

ἐγὼ ὑμᾶς, ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι, φιλῶ, πείσομαι δὲ μᾶλλον τῷ θεῷ ἢ ὑμῖν· καὶ
οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι ζητῶν τὴν ἀλήθειαν καὶ λέγων ὑμῖν· "ὦ ἄνθρωπε, διὰ τί τὰ
μὲν χρήματα ζητεῖς, τὴν δὲ ψυχὴν οὐ τιμᾷς;"

**Second tier — the originals.** *Matthew 7:7–11, Koine, from the standard
text; then Plato, Apology 29d–e, Attic, from Burnet's text.*

Αἰτεῖτε καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν, ζητεῖτε καὶ εὑρήσετε, κρούετε καὶ ἀνοιγήσεται
ὑμῖν· πᾶς γὰρ ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει καὶ ὁ ζητῶν εὑρίσκει καὶ τῷ κρούοντι
ἀνοιγήσεται. ἢ τίς ἐστιν ἐξ ὑμῶν ἄνθρωπος, ὃν αἰτήσει ὁ υἱὸς αὐτοῦ ἄρτον,
μὴ λίθον ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ; ἢ καὶ ἰχθὺν αἰτήσει, μὴ ὄφιν ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ; εἰ οὖν
ὑμεῖς πονηροὶ ὄντες οἴδατε δόματα ἀγαθὰ διδόναι τοῖς τέκνοις ὑμῶν, πόσῳ
μᾶλλον ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς δώσει ἀγαθὰ τοῖς αἰτοῦσιν αὐτόν.

ἐγὼ ὑμᾶς, ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι, ἀσπάζομαι μὲν καὶ φιλῶ, πείσομαι δὲ μᾶλλον
τῷ θεῷ ἢ ὑμῖν, καὶ ἕωσπερ ἂν ἐμπνέω καὶ οἷός τε ὦ, οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι
φιλοσοφῶν καὶ ὑμῖν παρακελευόμενός τε καὶ ἐνδεικνύμενος ὅτῳ ἂν ἀεὶ
ἐντυγχάνω ὑμῶν, λέγων οἷάπερ εἴωθα, ὅτι "ὦ ἄριστε ἀνδρῶν, Ἀθηναῖος ὤν,
πόλεως τῆς μεγίστης καὶ εὐδοκιμωτάτης εἰς σοφίαν καὶ ἰσχύν, οὐκ αἰσχύνῃ
ἐπιμελούμενος χρημάτων μὲν ὅπως σοι ἔσται ὡς πλεῖστα, καὶ δόξης καὶ τιμῆς,
φρονήσεως δὲ καὶ ἀληθείας καὶ τῆς ψυχῆς ὅπως ὡς βελτίστη ἔσται οὐκ
ἐπιμελῇ οὐδὲ φροντίζεις;"

::: gloss
- **ὑμῖν, ὑμῶν, ὑμᾶς, ὑμεῖς** — to you, of you, you, you *(plural)*
- **ὁ αἰτῶν, ὁ ζητῶν** — the one who asks, the one who seeks *(present
  participles of contract verbs)*
- **τοῖς αἰτοῦσιν** — to those who ask
- **ἐξ ὑμῶν** — of you, among you
- **αἰτῇ** — asks for *(present subjunctive of **αἰτέω**)*
- **ὄντες** — being *(participle of **εἰμί**, plural)*
- **πόσῳ μᾶλλον** — how much more
- **ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι** — men of Athens *(the vocative Socrates uses to the
  jury)*
- **πείσομαι** — I shall obey *(future of **πείθομαι**, Lesson 26)*
- **ἤ** — than; *(at the start of a sentence)* or
- **τὰ χρήματα** — money, possessions
- **ὦ ἄνθρωπε** — man! *(vocative)*
- **κρούω** — I knock; **τῷ κρούοντι** — to the one who knocks
- **ὃν αἰτήσει … ἄρτον** — whom his son will ask for bread *(**αἰτέω** with
  two accusatives)*
- **μὴ … ἐπιδώσει;** — he will not hand him …, will he? *(**ἐπιδίδωμι**,
  hand over; **μή** expects the answer no)*
- **ὁ ἰχθύς** — fish
- **ὁ ὄφις** — snake
- **οἴδατε … διδόναι** — you know how to give *(**διδόναι**, present
  infinitive of **δίδωμι**)*
- **τὸ δόμα** — gift *(Koine; Attic says **δῶρον**)*
- **ἀσπάζομαι** — I greet; I am fond of *(Lesson 28)*
- **ἕωσπερ ἂν ἐμπνέω** — for as long as I breathe *(subjunctive after
  **ἄν**)*
- **οἷός τε ὦ** — I am able *(**ὦ**, subjunctive of **εἰμί**)*
- **φιλοσοφῶν** — philosophising *(participle of **φιλοσοφέω**)*
- **παρακελευόμενος, ἐνδεικνύμενος** — urging, pointing out *(middle
  participles)*
- **ὅτῳ ἂν ἀεὶ ἐντυγχάνω ὑμῶν** — to whichever of you I ever meet
- **λέγων οἷάπερ εἴωθα** — saying the very things I am accustomed to say
- **ὦ ἄριστε ἀνδρῶν** — best of men
- **πόλεως τῆς μεγίστης καὶ εὐδοκιμωτάτης** — of the greatest and most
  famous city *(genitive of **πόλις**)*
- **εἰς σοφίαν καὶ ἰσχύν** — for wisdom and strength
- **οὐκ αἰσχύνῃ ἐπιμελούμενος** — are you not ashamed, caring
- **ὅπως σοι ἔσται ὡς πλεῖστα** — that you may have as much as possible
  *(literally "how there will be most to you")*
- **ἡ φρόνησις** — understanding, good sense
- **ὡς βελτίστη** — as good as possible
- **οὐκ ἐπιμελῇ οὐδὲ φροντίζεις** — you do not care, nor give thought
:::

::: {.exercise title="Understanding the reading"}
Read both tiers aloud before you answer anything.

1. In the Gospel, who asks and who gives? Which verbs carry each, and in
   what forms?
2. **αἰτεῖτε … δοθήσεται**: name the mood and aspect of the first and the
   tense and voice of the second. Why is the first present and not aorist?
3. **πᾶς ὁ αἰτῶν λαμβάνει** — parse **ὁ αἰτῶν**. What is **πᾶς** doing
   outside the article group (Lesson 34's note)?
4. The adaptation has **δίδωσιν** where the original has **ἐπιδώσει**.
   Name both forms. What does the original's tense do to the question?
5. **μὴ λίθον ἐπιδώσει αὐτῷ;** — what answer does a question beginning
   **μή** expect? Translate it so that an English reader expects the same.
6. **εἰ οὖν ὑμεῖς … οἴδατε … δώσει** — which row of Lesson 35's table is
   this condition, and what tells you?
7. Socrates says **πείσομαι τῷ θεῷ**. Which verb is this the future of, and
   which lesson taught you that to obey is to have been persuaded?
8. **οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι φιλοσοφῶν** — which job from Lesson 35 is this, and
   what is the strength of the claim?
9. Find every form of **εἰμί** in the Plato: there are three, in three
   different moods or tenses. Name each.
10. Both passages end on **ὁ πατήρ … δώσει ἀγαθά** and **τῆς ψυχῆς … οὐκ
    ἐπιμελῇ**. One is a promise and one is a reproach. What do the two men
    agree about, and what sentence in each shows it?
:::

::: answers
1. The hearers ask (**αἰτεῖτε**, **ὁ αἰτῶν**, **αἰτήσει**); the father
   gives (**δοθήσεται**, **ἐπιδώσει**, **διδόναι**, **δώσει**). Four forms
   of **δίδωμι** in five verses, one in each of present, future, future
   passive and infinitive.
2. **αἰτεῖτε** — imperative, present: *keep asking*, a practice. **δοθήσεται**
   — future passive, 3 sing. Present because the instruction is a way of
   living, not a single request; **αἰτήσατε** would be *ask, once*.
3. Present participle, masculine nominative singular, of **αἰτέω**, with
   the article: *the one who asks*. **πᾶς** outside the group is *every*:
   *every asker receives*.
4. **δίδωσιν**, present of **δίδωμι**; **ἐπιδώσει**, future of
   **ἐπιδίδωμι**. The future puts the son's request and the father's
   response into a particular imagined occasion — *will he hand him a
   stone?* — rather than a general habit.
5. *No*: **μή** in a question is the questioner saying *surely not*. *He
   won't hand him a stone, will he?*
6. The first row — simple: **εἰ** with the indicative (**οἴδατε**) and an
   indicative (**δώσει**) in the main clause. It takes the *if* as granted:
   *since you know how*.
7. **πείθομαι**, the middle of **πείθω** — Lesson 26. Socrates will *be
   persuaded by* the god rather than by the jury, which is the Greek for
   obeying one rather than the other.
8. Emphatic denial: **οὐ μή** with the aorist subjunctive. *I will by no
   means stop.* The strongest future *no* the language has, said to a court
   that could have him killed, and did.
9. **ὦ** (subjunctive, in **οἷός τε ὦ**), **ὤν** (participle, in **Ἀθηναῖος
   ὤν**), **ἔσται** (future, twice). Three of the five columns of this
   lesson's table in one sentence.
10. That what matters most is not what is asked for first. The Gospel says
    the father gives **ἀγαθά** — not stones and snakes, but not bread and
    fish either: *good things*, left undefined. Socrates says the
    Athenians care for **χρήματα, δόξα, τιμή** and not **φρόνησις,
    ἀλήθεια, ψυχή**. Both set the soul's good against the obvious goods,
    and both expect the hearer to have asked for the wrong one. Accept any
    answer that quotes the Greek on both sides.
:::
:::

::: {.question time="5 min"}
1. *Ask, and it will be given.* Socrates asked the Athenians a question for
   thirty years and was given hemlock. Is one of these two sentences wrong,
   or are they about different kinds of asking?
2. The oldest verbs in the language are *give*, *put*, *stand*, *be*,
   *go*, *say*. If you had to guess the six oldest verbs of any language
   from nothing, would you have guessed these? What does the list leave out,
   and why?
3. *What in the sentence tells you that?* You have been asked it of every
   sentence for a year. Name one thing outside Greek it can be asked of,
   and ask it.
:::

::: {.story time="—"}
The tags stayed on Julia's bench until the lesson ended, and she did not
touch them until everyone else had gone, and then she untied the thread and
read all three, and put them in her sleeve, and said nothing either.

That was the end of the year.

Eleven years later, in a house in another city, where she taught Greek to
children who complained about it exactly as she had, a letter came for
her from Theodoros. It was short, as his letters were. It began **χαίρειν**
and ended **ἔρρωσο**, and between the two formulas there was a single
sentence, in a hand that had got smaller.

**ἔβλεπον.**

Imperfect. *I was watching.* No beginning reported, no end: the tense of
Lesson 13, with the whole of Lesson 14 behind it, chosen by a man who had
spent his life choosing tenses, to say the one thing he had not said when
he put her tags in his sleeve — that it had been true all along, and that
he had known.

She did not need the grammar explained. That was the point of the grammar.
:::
